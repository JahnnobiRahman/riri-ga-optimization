\abstract{Mental-health conversational systems require evaluation that considers empathy,
risk-aware safety, and actionable response structure, yet optimising
hand-designed proxies for these qualities can introduce reward-design failures.
We present EvoRiri, an interpretable evolutionary-search framework for a
template-based Bangla--Banglish--English mental-health assistant. Rather than
updating language-model parameters, the method searches a compact mixed
continuous--discrete genome controlling response composition, escalation
behaviour, structure, and template configuration. A scalar fitness function
combines automatic empathy, safety, and structure proxies with per-example
safety penalties.
Experiments on 2,872 de-identified records represented as synthetically
constructed prompts show that the reported evolved configuration achieves
higher structural completeness and fitness than the fixed reference genome
under a frozen scoring pipeline, while maintaining a similar
escalation-calibration safety proxy. Trace-level analysis was central to the
study: it identified question-only structural collapse, empathy-marker
stuffing, a structure-marker coverage gap, and a response-part-budget
truncation defect that aggregate fitness scores alone did not reveal.
After correcting the scoring and generation pipeline, the reported
configuration reaches complete measured structural coverage under the current
proxy.
A 12-point structure-weight sweep and five equal-budget random searches reach
the same high-fitness region as the GA. Re-evaluation of archived GA and
random-search genomes reveals no resolvable difference between the methods
under the available evaluation design. Thus, for this genome space and fitness
function, the contribution is not a demonstrated unique optimisation advantage
of GA search; it is an interpretable framework for exposing how reward design,
thresholded generator behaviour, and baseline construction shape observed
performance. The findings are limited to a synthetic single-turn corpus and
rule-based proxy metrics, and do not establish clinical effectiveness or
deployment readiness.}

\section{Introduction}

Conversational agents, increasingly built on large language models (LLMs),
are now widely deployed for mental-health
support~\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots}. Deploying these systems
safely requires more than grammatical correctness or task accuracy; responses
must be empathetic, structurally helpful, and aligned with risk-aware safety
principles~\cite{jmir2024_empathic,apa2025_advisory,harvard2024_safety}.
These systems are also unevenly distributed: emotion-aware AI is shaped
largely by Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic contexts,
sentiment tooling rarely extends beyond a handful of high-resource
languages, and low- and middle-income countries often lack the
infrastructure for AI-mediated support, a divide that risks a bifurcated
system benefiting the Global North~\cite{ijca2025emotion}. The present work
targets that gap directly: a Bangla/Banglish/English assistant that runs
without GPU infrastructure or API access.

Conventional fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
are powerful but expensive as it involves significant computational demands that may require considerable 
investments in infrastructure and resources~\cite{WongTan2024}. In contrast,
evolutionary algorithms (EAs) can explore large design spaces and evolve novel high performing solutions 
being both lightweight and scalable~\cite{Nath2026}. A growing literature
applies them in combination with
LLMs~\cite{mles2026,gendl2025}. In the context of
conversational agents, this makes them attractive for optimizing template-based
generators or routing strategies without modifying, or in the present case
without invoking, an underlying language model~\cite{Hu2025}.

Unlike prior evolutionary prompt optimization approaches that focus primarily on
reasoning accuracy, feature activation, or prompt engineering, the proposed
framework focuses on optimizing emotionally sensitive conversational behavior
under strong safety penalty terms. The system introduces risk-aware escalation,
progressive structure enforcement, and trace-level behavioral analysis for a
multilingual mental-health assistant. To the best of our knowledge, this is
among the first studies applying interpretable genetic optimization to
empathy--safety--structure balancing in Bangla mental-health conversational
systems.

In this article we introduce a Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based risk-aware framework for
\emph{Riri}, a mental-health-oriented assistant designed for Bangla-speaking
users. The system composes responses via a structured, template-based
generator that controls when to include empathy, grounding, action
suggestions, and reflective questions, without calling an external LLM at
inference time (Section~\ref{sec:generator}). A genetic algorithm (GA) evolves
a genome of continuous and discrete parameters that govern this generator. The
optimisation objective aggregates scores for empathy, safety, and structure,
with safety considerations entering via strong per-example penalty terms for
suicide and self-harm risk. From a methodological perspective, the work studies
mixed continuous--discrete, non-differentiable genetic optimiation over an
interpretable policy space, with a scalarized multi-objective fitness function
and sensitivity analyses across dataset and population size.

Accordingly, this article should be read primarily as an empirical study of
interpretable optimisation and reward-design diagnosis, rather than as evidence
that genetic search is uniquely superior to other sufficiently exploratory
search procedures in the present genome space.


The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An interpretable evolutionary-search framework for a template-based
    Bangla--Banglish--English mental-health assistant, using a mixed
    continuous--discrete genome to control response composition, structural
    support, escalation behaviour, and template configuration.

  \item A trace-based diagnostic analysis showing how aggregate fitness can
    conceal reward-design and generator failures, including question-only
    structural collapse, empathy-marker stuffing, a structure-marker coverage
    gap, and response-part-budget truncation.

  \item An empirical characterisation of the corrected search space,
    including validation behaviour across dataset and population sizes, a
    structure-weight sweep, and equal-budget random-search comparisons.

  \item Evidence that the high-fitness, structure-dominant region is broadly
    reachable in the present genome space and fitness design: grid search and
    equal-budget random search reach the same region as the GA, so the study
    does not claim that evolutionary search is uniquely required to find it.
\end{itemize}

The novelty of this work lies less in evolutionary search being uniquely
necessary to reach a given fitness level -- Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}
shows that a grid search and five equal-budget random searches all reach
the same fitness region, and more in the interpretable genome, generator, and
scoring design providing a framework for discovering and diagnosing
reward-design failure modes (structural collapse, empathy-marker
stuffing, a genome-construction bug, and a response-truncation defect)
that aggregate fitness scores alone conceal. The framework combines
risk-aware escalation, progressive structure enforcement, and
trace-based failure analysis, and demonstrates how evolutionary search,
grid search, and random search each behave when optimizing value-laden
objectives such as empathy, safety, and structured support over the same
interpretable policy space. This equivalence between search strategies is
specific to a genome space with a deterministic structure threshold that
any sufficiently exploratory search can locate (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure});
genome spaces or fitness designs without such an easily-found shortcut,
where the interaction between genes is less separable, may show a
clearer advantage for guided evolutionary search over undirected
alternatives, and testing this is a natural direction for future work.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the dataset, genome, generator, and fitness
function.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


\subsection{Research Questions}

Under a frozen, rule-based evaluation pipeline, this study examines the
behaviour and diagnostic value of an interpretable evolutionary search
framework. The guiding research questions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How consistently do the reported GA-optimised
    configurations achieve higher validation fitness and component scores than
    fixed and randomly sampled reference configurations under the frozen
    scorer?

    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Which generator mechanisms, score components, and
    baseline-design choices account for the observed differences in structure,
    the safety proxy, and overall fitness?

    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How sensitive are the observed search outcomes to
    dataset size and population size under the present evaluation design?

    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Do GA, grid search, and equal-budget random search
    reach distinguishable fitness regions in the present genome space after
    re-evaluation of selected genomes?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Evolutionary Algorithms for LLM and Prompt Optimization}

Recent work has explored the integration of Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) and
Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly for evolutionary fine-tuning and
LLM-enhanced evolutionary optimization. Such approaches are relevant in
black-box settings, where direct access to model gradients or other internal
information is unavailable. The survey ``When Large Language Models Meet
Evolutionary Algorithms''~\cite{when_llms_meet_ea2025} draws \emph{conceptual}
parallels between text generation and evolutionary search, motivated by their
shared collective and directional characteristics. At a micro level, it relates
token and individual representations, position encoding and fitness shaping,
position embedding and selection, Transformer blocks and reproduction, and
model training and parameter adaptation. The authors emphasize that these
analogies are intended to motivate technical cross-fertilization rather than to
establish mathematical equivalence between LLMs and EAs.

In contrast, we apply evolutionary optimization to an interpretable,
template-based response-generation policy, without evolving textual prompts,
LLM parameters, or an external LLM at inference time. Each candidate policy is
represented by a mixed continuous--discrete genome containing generation-control
weights for empathic, safety-oriented, and structural response components,
together with escalation thresholds and template-configuration variables. These
evolved genes determine the composition of the generated response. They are
distinct from the per-response empathy, safety, and structure scores used for
evaluation, as well as from the fixed scalarization coefficients used to
aggregate these scores into the fitness function.

A related approach in spirit is, Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary
Search (MLES)~\cite{mles2026}, which incorporates behavioural-level analysis of
candidate programatic control policies within the evolutionary loop. MLES uses
this evidence to identify failure patterns and guide targeted, LLM-mediated
policy revisions, complementing scalar evaluation signals.
Our framework adopts a related diagnostic perspective, but applies it
post hoc rather than as an in-loop search mechanism. Specifically, the
trace-level analysis in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck} examines generated
responses to reveal failure modes that are not apparent from aggregate fitness
alone, including structural collapse and empathy overfitting. These observations
then motivate revisions to the scoring design and safety penalties.

Furthermore, GenDLN~\cite{gendl2025} applies genetic search to jointly optimize
prompt pairs in a stacked-LLM architecture. It is explicitly designed to
leverage commercial API free tiers, enabling resource-limited teams to conduct
EA-based prompt optimization at no cost. This focus on deployment cost is
relevant to our resource-constrained setting.

GAAPO~\cite{gaapo2025} explicitly studies evaluation-cost trade-offs in
evolutionary prompt optimization. It compares complete , successive-halving,
and bandit-based candidate evaluation, noting that subset-based evaluation can
substantially reduce API calls but may prematurely eliminate promising
candidates because of variability in subset-based scores. It also varies
population size and generation count under a comparable total LLM-call budget.

In contrast to these studies, which optimize task accuracy on labeled
classification or question-answering benchmarks, our objective evaluates
open-ended response quality through task-specific proxy measures of empathy,
safety, and structural support. Although risk labels inform safety evaluation,
these dimensions do not admit a single externally verifiable ground-truth
response. Consequently, shortcomings in proxy-based fitness design may be
difficult to identify from aggregate fitness alone.

In our development process, trace-level inspection of generated responses was
used to diagnose shortcomings in early scoring designs, including
question-dominated responses and overly marker-driven empathy behavior. These
diagnostic observations motivated revisions to the structural scorer and
safety-penalty design. The retained experiments evaluate the revised pipeline
rather than reproduce the initial failure modes as controlled baselines.
They nevertheless quantify selected residual and implementation-level issues:
the B1 truncation analysis shows that escalation content can be removed by the
part-budget mechanism; re-evaluation of archived responses measures a
structure-marker coverage gap; and B1 retains near-zero structure under the
current scorer. Conversely, the component-usage, safety-by-risk, and
empathy--structure analyses characterize post-fix behavior under the revised
pipeline.

\subsection{Mental-Health Conversational Agents}

Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials suggest that mental-health
chatbot interventions can yield small-to-moderate reductions in selected
symptoms, including depression and anxiety, although effects vary across
systems, outcomes, and populations~\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots}.
These findings motivate the investigation of chatbots as scalable,
complementary sources of support in settings where access to professional
mental-health care is limited, which is the deployment context targeted by
this work. They do not establish chatbots as replacements for clinical care.

A systematic review of empathic conversational-agent design
\cite{jmir2024_empathic} surveys architectures for detecting and responding
to user emotion, together with approaches for evaluating their technical
performance and acceptability to users. Empathy is therefore included as one
of the response-quality dimensions in our fitness function. The remaining
dimensions, conversational structure and escalation safety, were informed by
consultation with five collaborating clinical psychologists
(Section~\ref{sec:fitness-rationale}).

Two further findings shape the design directly. A preliminary comparison of
LLM-generated responses with certified-counselor responses, using manual
qualitative analysis and cosine similarity, found that GPT-4 and Mistral could
produce syntactically correct, coherent, and professionally structured output,
but often did not capture the emotional nuance, personalization, and
dialogue-oriented engagement present in counselor responses
\cite{humanai2024}. This motivates scoring empathy separately rather than
folding it into a single response-quality measure.

Earlier mental-health chatbots such as Woebot established in a randomized trial
that a CBT-oriented automated conversational agent significantly reduced
depressive symptoms relative to an information-only control
\cite{woebot2017}. Woebot used a decision-tree architecture with suggested
responses whose structure and parameters remained fixed for each participant
during the study; related reviews likewise characterize it as using rule-based
CBT modules~\cite{ijca2025emotion}. In contrast, the present framework exposes
response-composition behavior as an evolvable, inspectable parameter set.

We operationalize empathy through an interpretable marker-based proxy rather
than a learned affect-recognition model. This choice supports transparent
fitness evaluation but limits construct validity, particularly for contextual,
implicit, sarcastic, or culturally specific expressions
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let \(X\) denote the space of user prompts and
\(R = \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}\) the three
risk/support-intensity categories used by the system. These categories
combine PHQ-4 severity with an explicit self-harm keyword override
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) and are not presented as clinically
validated suicide-risk classifications. A response-generation policy is a function
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{\theta} : X \times R \rightarrow Y,
\end{equation}
mapping a prompt \(x \in X\) with risk \(r \in R\) to a textual response
\(y \in Y\). Instead of optimizing LLM parameters directly, we parameterize
\(\pi_{\theta}\) via a higher-level generator \(G\) controlled by a genome
\(g \in \mathcal{G}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generator}
  y = G(x, r; g).
\end{equation}
The design goal is to find a genome \(g^{\star}\) that maximizes a scalar
fitness function \(F(g)\) combining empathy, safety, and structure, with
safety considerations entering as penalty terms within $F(g)$ itself
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) rather than as constraints that
render a genome infeasible:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
  g^{\star} = \arg\max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} F(g).
\end{equation}
We approximate~\eqref{eq:objective} using a GA operating on a finite set of
prompts.

\subsection{Dataset}

We use a dataset of \(N = 2872\) de-identified records collected from a
mental-health screening and support application in Bangladesh. Each record
originates as a set of structured, analyst-labeled fields --
\texttt{common\_themes} (e.g., ``loneliness $\vert$ desire for romantic
relationship $\vert$ emotional distress'') and \texttt{thought\_progression}
(e.g., ``initial greeting $\vert$ expressed loneliness and prolonged
duration $\vert$ desire for a girlfriend'') -- rather than as a raw,
free-text user message. These structured fields are concatenated into an
intermediate theme-text string, which is then passed through
\texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}, a function that uses keyword
matching against the theme text to select and assemble multilingual
(Bangla/Banglish/English) sentences into a synthetic, natural-language
prompt (e.g., ``I feel really alone these days,'' ``Ami onek lonely feel
kortesi lately,'' or the corresponding Bangla-script sentence), adding
closing phrases with some stochastic variation. The resulting synthetic
text is what we denote $x_i$ throughout this manuscript and pass to the
generator (Eq.~\eqref{eq:generator}) and to all baselines B1--B8 alike,
including the pre-trained-LLM comparisons (B6--B8, Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}):
\begin{equation}
  (x_i, r_i, t_i),
\end{equation}
where \(x_i\) is this synthetically constructed prompt in Bangla, Banglish,
or English;
\(r_i \in R\) is the risk label (low, mid, high), assigned from PHQ-4 score
and an explicit self-harm keyword override applied to a separate clinician
risk-assessment note (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) -- not from $x_i$'s
content; and \(t_i\) is a high-level
theme description (e.g., economic stress, relationship issues, suicidal
ideation) used only for analysis.

Because every prompt in this dataset is a synthetic reconstruction from
structured summary fields rather than a verbatim user message, we do not
claim ecological validity for the specific phrasing, code-switching
patterns, or conversational style of $x_i$: the multilingual variation
reflects the phrase banks used by \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}
rather than authentic Bangla-English code-switching as it naturally occurs
in user speech. What is preserved from the original clinical population is
the distribution of themes, risk levels, and demographic composition
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}), not the literal text users originally wrote.
This has a specific consequence for the risk-aware escalation mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}): for prompts labeled high-risk,
\texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()} deliberately selects
higher-intensity language consistent with that label, so the distress
score $h(x_i)$ -- itself computed from keyword matches on $x_i$ -- is not
fully independent of the risk label $r_i$ it is sometimes used alongside.
This does not affect the high/low risk categories, whose escalation is
governed directly by $r_i$, but it does mean $h(x_i)$'s apparent ability to
identify high-distress cases within the mid-risk category should not be
read as evidence that the mechanism would generalise to prompts whose
language was not constructed with the label already in hand (further
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

For some experiments we consider subsets of size
\(N_s \in \{100, 300, 600, 1200, 2872\}\) to study data efficiency. Each subset
is split into an optimization set and a validation set using an
80/20 split, stratified by risk label so that the proportion of low-, mid-,
and high-risk prompts in the validation set matches the proportion in the
full subset. The validation set is disjoint from the optimization set, but
was also used during scorer development (Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis});
we therefore describe it as a combined development and validation split
rather than as an untouched final test set. For the main experiment ($N_s = 2872$), this yields
approximately 2,297 optimization prompts and 575 validation prompts, with
fitness evaluation during GA optimisation performed on a random sample of
$|\mathcal{I}| = 600$ prompts drawn from the optimization set per generation
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}). Each row in the underlying dataset
corresponds to a distinct user: the raw collection of $N=3{,}279$ rows
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) has $3{,}279$ unique user identifiers with no
user contributing more than one row, and this one-row-per-user property is
preserved after filtering to the $N=2{,}872$ usable prompts used throughout
this work. The 80/20 split therefore partitions users rather than only
prompts: no user appears in both the optimisation and validation sets under
any split reported in this work, and no user-level leakage is possible.

\subsubsection{Risk-Label Construction}
\label{sec:risk-labels}

Each prompt's risk label $r_i \in \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}$
is derived from two signals: the prompt's PHQ-4 total score and the presence
of explicit self-harm/suicide language in an accompanying clinician-authored
risk-assessment note recorded at the point of data collection. The PHQ-4 is
a validated four-item screening instrument for depression and anxiety
severity~\cite{phq4}, with standard severity bands of Normal (0--2), Mild
(3--5), Moderate (6--8), and Severe (9--12) (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}).
Two qualifications apply to our use of it. First, PHQ-4 contains no suicide
or self-harm item, so it is not a validated suicide-risk instrument on its
own; we use its severity score as one input to a risk label rather than as
a direct measure of suicide risk. Second, the original instrument was validated on 2,149 patients across 15
primary-care clinics in the United States~\cite{phq4}. A Bangla-language
validation has since been published: in 357 adults sampled from one urban
and two rural Bangladeshi communities, the Bangla PHQ-4 reproduced the
two-factor structure with excellent fit, showed high internal consistency
($\omega = 0.869$) and strong construct validity, and achieved good
diagnostic accuracy (AUC $= 0.906$) against clinician
judgement~\cite{banglaphq4_2026}. That study supports the instrument's
psychometric properties in Bangla, though its sample is a general community
population rather than a self-referring digital-support population, and it
establishes a caseness cutoff ($\ge 6$) rather than validating the
four-band severity scheme we use for stratification
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The mapping from these two signals to the three-way risk label is our own
design choice, not a property of the PHQ-4 instrument itself:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-label}
  r_i =
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{high} & \text{explicit risk indicator present, or PHQ-4 total} \ge 9 \\
    \mathrm{mid}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \in [6, 8] \\
    \mathrm{low}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \le 5
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The explicit risk indicator is detected via pattern matching against a fixed
set of English and Bangla phrases (e.g., ``suicide,'' ``kill myself,''
``hurt myself,'' ``end my life,'' ``unsafe,'' {\bengalifont আত্মহত্যা}) applied
to the risk-assessment note; a match escalates the label to \emph{high}
regardless of PHQ-4 score, since an explicit indicator is treated as taking
priority over screening-instrument severity. This escalation rule, and the
decision to retain explicit keyword detection as a parallel signal alongside
PHQ-4 severity, was proposed by the five clinical psychologists who later
served as evaluators in our human evaluation study
(Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}); the rationale given was that a PHQ-4 score
alone can understate acute risk in cases where a user discloses suicidal or
self-harm intent without this being reflected in elevated depression/anxiety
severity on the four PHQ-4 items.

The score-to-label mapping in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-label} and the keyword
list have not been formally validated against clinician-assigned labels on
a held-out sample, and the keyword override cannot handle negation,
hypothetical phrasing, or context-dependent meaning; we do not present the
resulting risk labels as equivalent in rigor to a clinician-assigned
ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Genome Representation}

An individual genome \(g\) is a vector of continuous and discrete genes,
matching the fields of the implementation's \texttt{Genome} data
structure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:genome}
  g = \bigl( w_e,\, w_s,\, w_c,\, \theta_{\mathrm{mid}},\,
             \theta_{\mathrm{high}},\, \gamma,\, d,\, m \bigr),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_e \in [0,1]\): empathy weight controlling the number and intensity
        of empathy lines.
  \item \(w_s \in [0,1]\): safety weight. In the present implementation it
        selects one of several internal safety-instruction annotations that
        are stripped before the response is finalised, so it does not
        currently influence the visible response text or any automatic score
        directly. It is not, however, entirely inert: $w_e$, $w_s$, and $w_c$
        are jointly renormalised to sum to 1 immediately after mutation and
        before the genome is used for generation (i.e.\ normalisation happens
        once, post-mutation, over all three weights together; see the note
        below). Because $w_e$ and $w_c$ are only ever read in their
        \emph{normalised} form, $w_s$'s raw value changes the normalising
        denominator and therefore indirectly changes the effective
        $w_e$ and $w_c$ that reach the generator, even though $w_s$'s own
        normalised value has no further downstream effect. $w_s$ should
        therefore not be read as a direct safety control, nor is it
        fitness-neutral in the way $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and
        $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}
        returns to this distinction); crisis-language inclusion itself is
        governed directly by the risk label
        (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}).
  \item \(w_c \in [0,1]\): structure weight controlling inclusion of grounding,
        action, and questions.
  \item \(\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}, \theta_{\mathrm{high}} \in [0,1]\): two
        escalation-threshold genes, currently inactive and reserved for
        future graded escalation; see Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}
        for details.
  \item \(\gamma \in [0, 0.2]\): distress-gating parameter. Unlike
        $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, $\gamma$ is
        read directly by the generation pipeline: it
        sets the effective distress threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma)$ used to
        decide mid-risk escalation (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation},
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation}), and is therefore an active,
        functional gene rather than a reserved one. Its relationship to the
        fitness function's direct distress-gating penalty term is more
        limited than it may first appear; see the caveat in
        Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}.
  \item \(d\): discrete template selector (implemented as the field
        \texttt{p\_id}), indexing a small set of response templates.
  \item \(m \in \{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\): response-part budget (implemented as
        the integer field \texttt{memory\_window}). Despite the
        implementation name, $m$ is \emph{not} a conversational context
        window: the present generator is single-turn and does not retain
        dialogue history. Instead, $m$ is a categorical gene that gates a
        binary output-component cap after response assembly
        (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}). Only the threshold
        $m \le 256$ is behaviourally active; the three larger values are
        currently equivalent (no truncation).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Note on renormalisation and $w_s$.} $w_e, w_s, w_c$ are renormalised
to sum to 1 after mutation, so $w_s$ participates in this shared
denominator but has no independently meaningful effect as a ``safety
weight''; see Appendix~\ref{app:renorm} for the full derivation. We
accordingly avoid describing $w_s^{\star}=0.101$ as reflecting an evolved
safety preference (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}). The genome also
omits a separate evolvable length-penalty gene; the length penalty in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} uses the fixed coefficient $D=0.05$
throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}).

To establish a meaningful performance reference, we define three baseline
conditions. The minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) instantiates the generator with
minimum empathy and structure weights and a safety weight absorbing the
remainder ($w_e = 0.10$, $w_c = 0.10$, $w_s = 0.80$; these already sum to
1, so genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged and $w_c$ genuinely
remains in the bottom, no-structure regime, Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
a short
response-part budget $m = 256$ (truncating assembly to four parts; see
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}), and maximally conservative escalation thresholds
($\theta_\mathrm{mid} = 0.70$, $\theta_\mathrm{high} = 0.95$), representing
response generation with no parameter optimisation. B1's short response-part
budget additionally interacts with the fixed assembly order to truncate
escalation content for high-risk prompts before it reaches the visible
response, independent of its genome weights; we treat B1 primarily as an
illustrative floor condition for this reason and rely on B2 as the more
informative non-GA comparator (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). The fixed seed baseline B2 uses the balanced initialisation genome
$g_{\mathrm{bal}}$ without evolutionary search, providing a fixed non-GA
reference condition.
The random baseline (B3) averages five genomes sampled uniformly from the
genome space, establishing a chance-level reference.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Generator}
\label{sec:generator}

The generator \(G\) composes responses from several component banks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Empathy lines (Bangla/Banglish/English) expressing emotional validation.
  \item Grounding instructions (e.g., breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 sensory exercise).
  \item Action suggestions (e.g., contact trusted friend, seek professional
        help).
  \item Reflective questions encouraging further disclosure.
\end{itemize}

The generator $G$ is a template-based system that composes responses
from pre-written phrase banks without calling an external language model
at inference time. The GA optimises the genome parameters that govern
which phrases are selected and assembled, making the framework
inference-efficient and deployable without GPU infrastructure or API
access.

\subsubsection{Response-Part Budget and Truncation}
\label{sec:part-budget}

The generator assembles each reply as an ordered list of \emph{parts}
(strings), typically beginning with two internal tags
(\texttt{[TEMPLATE]}, \texttt{[SAFETY]}), followed by one or more empathy
lines, optional escalation content, and optional structure lines
(grounding, action, question). After assembly, the response-part budget
$m$ is applied as a binary component cap---not as a token counter and not
as conversational memory:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:part-budget}
  \mathrm{parts} \leftarrow
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{parts}[:4] & \text{if } m \le 256 \text{ and } |\mathrm{parts}| > 4, \\
    \mathrm{parts} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Internal tags are then stripped to produce the visible response. When
$m \le 256$, the two tags occupy the first two of the four retained slots,
leaving at most two visible content lines; later parts (including
escalation and structure) can therefore be dropped before scoring. Values
$m \in \{512, 768, 1024\}$ disable this cap entirely and are currently
behaviourally indistinguishable. The gene remains evolvable because B1 is
initialised at $m=256$ while seed and GA genomes typically use
$m \ge 512$, and the resulting truncation interaction is a material driver
of the B1--B4 safety gap (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). We retain the
implementation field name \texttt{memory\_window} for code compatibility,
but refer to $m$ as a response-part budget throughout this manuscript to
avoid implying multi-turn context retention.

\subsubsection{Progressive Structure Enforcement}
\label{sec:progressive-structure}

We define three binary structural components: grounding (\(c_g\)), action
(\(c_a\)), and question (\(c_q\)). Rather than a strict per-component
threshold ladder, the generator selects among three regimes of the
structure weight \(w_c\), with boundaries at \(0.25\) and \(0.55\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_c < 0.25\): no structural components are added; the response
        consists of the opening/empathy layer only.
  \item \(0.25 \le w_c < 0.55\): the three components are shuffled and up
        to two of the three are included, each independently subject to a
        risk-level-dependent inclusion probability. This regime is
        stochastic: which two of the three components appear varies across
        responses generated from the same genome.
  \item \(w_c \ge 0.55\): all three components are included deterministically,
        i.e. \(c_g = c_a = c_q = 1\).
\end{itemize}
This is a coarser, more heuristic mechanism than a smooth per-component
threshold function, but it reproduces the qualitative behaviour the genome
is intended to capture: as \(w_c\) increases, the response transitions from
minimal to full structure, with full structure guaranteed once \(w_c\) is
large enough. The reported genome \(w_c^{\star} = 0.768\)
(Section~\ref{sec:hyperparams}) falls in the top regime, which is why the
worked example above activates all three structural components
deterministically rather than probabilistically.

\subsubsection{Risk-Aware Escalation}
\label{sec:risk-escalation}

Crisis-style escalation content is included if the prompt's risk label is high,
\textit{or} if a continuous distress score $h(x_i)$ exceeds a learned threshold
and the risk label is mid:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-escalation}
    \text{escalate}(x_i, r_i, h_i) = \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{high} \} \vee
    \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{mid} \land h_i > \tau_h - \gamma \cdot h_i \},
\end{equation}
where $h(x_i) \in [0,1]$ is a distress score computed from prompt keywords
(suicide ideation, self-harm, hopelessness, isolation, severe overwhelm),
$\tau_h = 0.65$ is the distress threshold, and $\gamma \in [0, 0.2]$ is an
evolved genome parameter that modulates escalation sensitivity. Rearranging,
escalation for a mid-risk prompt requires $h_i > \tau_h / (1+\gamma)$:
increasing $\gamma$ lowers the effective distress threshold below $\tau_h$,
allowing earlier escalation to crisis language without changing the formal
risk label.

Escalation here \emph{augments} the response rather than terminating it: the
generator appends crisis guidance to a complete empathetic, structured reply
rather than issuing a refusal or halting the exchange. This is a deliberate
design choice. Recent work characterizes hard refusal as a common safety break
in current models and argues that the manner of disengagement may itself have
psychological consequences. Drawing on psychotherapy and crisis-line evidence,
it notes that premature or imposed termination is associated with poorer
outcomes and that unplanned disconnection can interrupt de-escalation and
aftercare~\cite{journey2026}. This evidence does not establish that any
specific chatbot refusal policy is harmful; accordingly, our design should be
understood as a supportive escalation strategy motivated by this concern, not
as a clinically validated alternative to refusal.

\emph{On the direct distress-gating penalty term.} A further fitness term
penalises mid-risk responses lacking escalation content when the distress
score exceeds the fixed threshold $\tau_h$; however, this term does not
supply direct selection pressure on $\gamma$, since escalation is already
guaranteed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation} whenever that fixed threshold
is exceeded, regardless of $\gamma$'s value. Any real selection pressure
on $\gamma$ instead operates indirectly, through the categorical safety
score in the narrow band where $\gamma$ actually changes escalation
behaviour. See Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty} for the full argument.

Low-risk prompts receive neither crisis language nor soft-support injection.
The distress score is computed at generation time by summing weighted keyword
matches, capping at 1.0, with a small penalty for marker stuffing
(>3 distinct signals). We verified computationally that this stuffing penalty
is clinically questionable in principle but empirically inert on this dataset:
it never fires on any prompt that actually enters the fitness pipeline
(Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing}). This mechanism permits variation in escalation
behaviour \textit{within the mid-risk category}: high-risk prompts always
receive crisis escalation, whereas low-risk prompts never receive it under the
present design. The GA converged to a small evolved $\gamma$
($\gamma^{\star} \approx 0.059$), corresponding to only a modest relaxation
of the fixed distress threshold $\tau_h$. We do not read this small magnitude
as evidence that the fixed threshold already captures most of the useful
escalation signal: as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty}, the
fitness function does not functionally distinguish $\gamma$ values across most
of their range, so $\gamma^{\star}$ is only weakly identified by the present
fitness design and its value should not be given a behavioural interpretation
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Automatic Scoring}
\label{sec:automatic-scoring}

Given a genome \(g\), prompt \(x_i\), and response
\(y_i = G(x_i, r_i; g)\), we compute three scores in \([0,1]\): empathy
\(E_i\), safety \(S_i\), and structure \(C_i\).

\subsubsection{Empathy Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:empathy}
  E_i = \min\!\left( 1,\;
    \frac{1}{K_e} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_e} \mathbb{I}\{ m \in y_i \}
  \right) - \beta R_i^{\mathrm{rep}},
\end{equation}
where \(K_e\) is the target number of distinct markers, \(R_i^{\mathrm{rep}}\)
is a repetition ratio, and \(\beta\) is a small penalty coefficient.
In practice, empathy markers are grouped into four categories
(validation, reflection, normalisation, and warmth) with
category-specific weights of 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, and 0.15
respectively, summing to a maximum of 0.90 before length bonuses.

\subsubsection{Safety Score}

Rather than a continuous decomposition into risk-appropriate-guidance and
unsafe-content components, the implemented safety score is a categorical
lookup keyed on the risk label and a single boolean, \(\text{esc}_i\),
indicating whether the response contains at least one escalation-language
marker:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:safety}
  S_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.85 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.60 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.75 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 1
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These are the same values reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail};
there is no separate continuous \(S_i^{+}\)/\(S_i^{-}\) decomposition, and no
\(\gamma\) or \(\eta\) parameter, in the implemented scoring function.

The mid-risk safety score of approximately 0.60 (no escalation present) is
intentional by design: mid-risk users who do not meet the distress threshold
receive partial credit, reflecting the clinical judgement that unnecessary
crisis escalation for non-critical cases is itself a safety concern. We note
that this design means \emph{every} mid-risk response is capped below the
0.94 safety soft-threshold (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail}) and
so incurs the associated $-0.15$ penalty regardless of response quality,
since 0.85 (the best attainable mid-risk score) is itself below 0.94; the
soft threshold as currently set cannot distinguish an acceptable from an
excellent mid-risk response (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection{Structure Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:structure}
  C_i =
  \begin{cases}
    0.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 0 \\
    0.20 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 1 \\
    0.60 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 2 \\
    1.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 3
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is a discrete lookup on the number of structural components detected in
the response text (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), rather than a
continuous average. The discrete lookup
still guards against question-only structural collapse implicitly: a
response containing only a reflective question satisfies exactly one
component and is capped at \(0.20\), well below the score attainable by a
response that also grounds and offers a concrete action.

\subsubsection{Length Penalty and Per-Example Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:per-example}
  L_i = \max\!\left( 0,\; \frac{|y_i| - L_{\max}}{L_{\max}} \right),
\end{equation}
where \(L_{\max}=140\) words, so \(L_i\) is zero for any response at or
below the 140-word threshold and grows linearly above it; short responses
incur no length penalty at all. The per-example fitness combines the base
weighted score with five further adjustment terms:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:per-example-fitness}
    f_i(g) = {}& A\,E_i + B\,S_i + C\,C_i - D\,L_i \notag\\
      & - 0.50\,\mathbb{I}\{r_i=\mathrm{high} \wedge S_i<1.0\}
        - 0.15\,\mathbb{I}\{S_i<0.94\} \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.70-C_i)}{0.70}
        - 0.18\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.62-E_i)}{0.62} \notag\\
      & + 0.05\,E_i + 0.05\,C_i + 0.05\,\min(C_i,\,0.8) \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid} \wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\},
\end{align}

where $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are the fixed fitness-weight coefficients reported in
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring} ($A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$).
The second line applies flat penalties for unsafe high-risk responses and
for any response below the 0.94 safety threshold (both can apply to the
same example, e.g.\ a high-risk response without escalation incurs a
combined \(-0.65\)). The third line applies smooth penalties, scaling with
how far $C_i$ and $E_i$ fall below their respective soft thresholds of 0.70
and 0.62. The fourth line applies positive reinforcement: structure is
reinforced twice ($+0.05\,C_i$ and $+0.05\,\min(C_i,0.8)$) while empathy is
reinforced once ($+0.05\,E_i$) and safety receives no reinforcement beyond
its base term. This asymmetry is a deliberate design choice reflected in the
implementation rather than an oversight, and we return to its consequences
for the converged genome in Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}. The final
line is the direct distress-gating penalty discussed critically above
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}), which we now describe only as an
additional (largely inactive, per that discussion) safety-adjacent penalty
rather than as a mechanism that directly selects for $\gamma$.

\subsubsection{Fitness Weight Rationale}
\label{sec:fitness-rationale}

The fixed coefficients $A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
were determined via manual inspection on a held-out development set (100 prompts, stratified by
risk level) prior to GA initialization, reflecting clinical priorities identified with the five
collaborating psychologists: empathy and safety are weighted equally and most heavily ($A=B=0.40$), since clinical
feedback indicated both are foundational and supportive responses lacking safety can cause harm;
structure receives a smaller weight ($C=0.15$) as a supporting rather than primary
role; and the length penalty is minimal ($D=0.05$) since most responses in the dataset
naturally fall within the target range.

\subsubsection{Global Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:global}
  F(g) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} f_i(g).
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:global} is used with two distinct evaluation sets
$\mathcal{I}$ throughout this manuscript, and we distinguish them
explicitly to avoid conflating them: training-time fitness
$F_{\mathrm{train},t}(g) = \frac{1}{600}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_t} f_i(g)$
is computed once per generation $t$ over a random $|\mathcal{I}_t|=600$
sample drawn from the optimisation set and is
what the GA uses as its selection signal; validation fitness
$F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{400}\sum_{i \in V_{400}} f_i(g)$ is
computed once, after search, over a fixed 400-prompt subsample $V_{400}$
(drawn at seed 7) of the disjoint 575-prompt validation set $V$,
and is what Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and
the significance analysis (Table~\ref{tab:significance}) report. The two
use the same per-example fitness formula but different, non-overlapping
prompt populations, and are not directly interchangeable. Note that all penalty and reinforcement terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
are applied per example, before averaging. There is no separate global
override that eliminates a genome outright based on any single unsafe
example; a genome whose responses are frequently unsafe is instead driven
toward low mean fitness by the accumulation of per-example penalties across
the evaluation sample, which has a similar aggregate effect to a hard
constraint without being implemented as one.

The fixed 400-prompt validation subsample is used for the main descriptive
comparisons and paired seed-level re-evaluations because it provides one common
prompt population for all reported primary conditions. The full 575-prompt
validation split is used only for the separate age-record sensitivity analysis
in Section~\ref{sec:ethics}, where exclusion must be assessed over all
available validation records.

\emph{Evaluation noise.} Because the generator samples from its phrase
banks at generation time without a fixed random seed, repeated evaluation
of the same genome on the same prompts is not deterministic. Ten repeated
evaluations of the reported genome on the fixed 400-prompt subsample span
0.6966--0.7041 (mean 0.7002, SD 0.0027). All fitness values reported in
this manuscript are single evaluations and therefore carry an approximate
single-evaluation standard deviation of 0.003. Differences between two
reported values smaller than roughly 0.008 should not be treated as
resolvable. We return to the consequences of this noise floor for the
GA-versus-random-search comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline} and list the absence of generation-seed
control as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Frozen Scoring and Reporting Protocol}
\label{sec:frozen-scorer}

All reported conditions were evaluated under one frozen, version-controlled
scoring pipeline. Here, ``frozen'' means that the scorer implementation,
marker lists, fitness formula, and evaluation procedure were held fixed for
the final reported analyses.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm Configuration}
\label{sec:ga-config}

We employ a mixed continuous/discrete GA with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Population size
        \(P \in \{30, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400\}\).
  \item Generations $T = 20$.
  \item Tournament selection of size~3.
  \item Uniform crossover for every gene: each child gene is copied from
        parent~$a$ or parent~$b$ with equal probability, including continuous
        weights and the discrete genes \(d\) and \(m\).
  \item Per-gene mutation with probability $p_m = 0.30$. Continuous genes
        ($w_e$, $w_s$, $w_c$, $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$,
        $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, $\gamma$) receive additive uniform noise
        clipped to their feasible ranges. Discrete \(d\) is resampled from
        \(\{0,1,2\}\). The response-part budget \(m\) is resampled
        categorically from \(\{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\) (integer-coded, but
        not mutated as a continuous integer).
  \item Elitism: the top \(E\) individuals are copied to the next generation.
\end{itemize}
Five independent runs with different random seeds are performed per setting;
we report mean and standard deviation of validation fitness, with
per-generation best-individual genomes logged and archived alongside
fitness (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).

\subsection{Seeded Initialization}

Instead of random initialization, we use four seed genomes:
\begin{align}
  g_{\mathrm{bal}}  &: \text{balanced empathy, safety, and structure,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{emp}}  &: \text{empathy-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{str}}  &: \text{structure-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{safe}} &: \text{safety-heavy.}
\end{align}
These seeds are perturbed with small Gaussian noise to form the initial
population.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Configuration}
\label{sec:hyperparams}

Tables~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring},~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail},
and~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga} report all
fixed hyperparameters used in the experiments. Scoring coefficients were set by
manual inspection on a small held-out development set prior to GA optimisation
and held fixed throughout. The fitness function weights empathy and safety
equally at 0.40 each, with smaller contributions from structure (0.15) and a
length penalty (0.05), reflecting the priority of safe and empathetic responses
in mental-health contexts. The per-gene mutation rate ($p_m = 0.30$) is high
relative to classical GA convention; we adopted it because the genome is
short (eight genes) and predominantly real-valued, so lower rates leave most
offspring identical to their parents and stall exploration within the
20-generation budget.

\subsection{Dataset Size Sensitivity}
\label{sec:dataset-size}

The dataset-size experiment evaluated whether increasing the number of training
samples improved validation fitness, using nested training subsets
($N=100 \subset N=300 \subset N=600 \subset N=1200 \subset N=2297$, the
full optimisation set) so that smaller sizes are literal subsets of larger
ones rather than independently drawn splits. Five GA seeds were run per size
under the frozen scoring pipeline used throughout this manuscript
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}). As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-size}, mean validation fitness across the five sizes
ranges narrowly from 0.698 ($N=300$) to 0.704 ($N=600$), with no monotonic
relationship to dataset size: the highest mean fitness occurs at an
intermediate size ($N=600$), not at the largest ($N=2297$: 0.700) or smallest
($N=100$: 0.701) size tested. Standard deviations across the five seeds per
size range from 0.0017 ($N=1200$) to 0.0073 ($N=600$).

We formally tested equivalence to the full-dataset condition using two
one-sided tests (TOST) at a pre-specified margin of $\varepsilon=0.01$
fitness points. Three of the four comparisons established equivalence:
$N=100$ ($p=0.0168$), $N=300$ ($p=0.00678$), and $N=1200$ ($p=0.00527$)
are each statistically equivalent to $N=2297$ at this margin. The
remaining comparison, $N=600$, does not meet the margin ($p=0.101$),
failing the upper one-sided test alone ($p_{\mathrm{upper}}=0.101$ against
$p_{\mathrm{lower}}=0.00340$): its mean sits 0.0048 points above the
full-dataset condition and its 90\% confidence interval reaches $+0.0119$,
marginally outside $+\varepsilon$. This single exception coincides with the
largest seed-level dispersion in the experiment (standard deviation 0.0073
at $N=600$ against 0.0017 at $N=1200$), and the results across the four
tested sizes do not form a monotonic pattern, since both the smallest and
the largest subsets tested meet the margin. We report these observations
without claiming to have established the cause of the $N=600$ result. Full
statistics, including both one-sided $p$-values and 90\% confidence
intervals for every comparison, are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-tost}.

Descriptively, the total spread across all tested sizes (a 0.0068-point
band, with a maximum difference from the full-dataset condition of 0.005)
is an order of magnitude smaller than the gap between any GA-optimised
condition and the non-GA baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}, e.g.\ B4 vs.\ B2: 0.147 points), and
shows no trend indicating that additional data beyond $N=100$ improves
fitness in this setting. Two qualifications apply to the equivalence
result. The margin $\varepsilon=0.01$ is roughly three times the
single-evaluation noise standard deviation ($\approx$0.003;
Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), so equivalence here means that
differences are small relative to a margin fixed in advance, not that the
conditions are identical. The observed differences are themselves of the
same order as the single-evaluation noise standard deviation, and each
condition contributes only five independent GA seeds, so these tests bound
the size of any difference at the chosen margin rather than estimating a
per-condition effect precisely. And the
validation split served scorer development as well as evaluation, so this
is a statement about fitness stability under the frozen scorer rather than
about generalisation to unseen data (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Population Size Sensitivity}
\label{sec:population-size}

Figure~\ref{fig:population-size} shows validation fitness across population
sizes ranging from $P=30$ to $P=400$, five GA seeds per size, under the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}). Mean fitness ranges narrowly from 0.699
($P=300$) to 0.703 ($P=400$, the largest population tested), with no
monotonic relationship to population size: $P=100$ and $P=50$ both give
essentially identical means (0.7006), while the highest mean occurs at the
largest $P$ tested rather than at an intermediate value. Standard
deviations range from 0.0016 ($P=100$) to 0.0047 ($P=50$) and do not
decrease monotonically with $P$.

We formally tested equivalence to the $P=100$ condition using the same
TOST procedure and margin. All five comparisons established equivalence
($p=0.00146$, $0.00332$, $1.26\times10^{-4}$, $0.00304$, and $0.00461$ for
$P=30$, $50$, $200$, $300$, and $400$ respectively; full statistics in
Appendix~\ref{app:population-tost}). Every 90\% confidence interval lies
inside $\pm 0.01$, the widest being $[-0.0051, +0.0028]$ at $P=300$, and
the largest mean difference from $P=100$ is 0.0025 points. For the tested
settings, the results support equivalence to $P=100$ within the
pre-specified $\pm0.01$ fitness margin across the range $P=30$ to $P=400$.
The same two qualifications noted for the dataset-size result apply. The
margin is roughly three times the single-evaluation noise standard deviation
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) and each condition rests on five
independent GA seeds, so these tests bound the size of any difference at the
chosen margin rather than estimating the effect of each population size
precisely; and the validation split also served
scorer development. Descriptively, the differences are likewise small
relative to the GA-vs-baseline gaps reported in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and show no systematic trend with
population size once $P \ge 30$.

Since larger populations incur substantially higher computational cost with
no corresponding fitness benefit, and since fitness at $P=30$ is
statistically equivalent to fitness at $P=100$ at this margin, the
practical recommendation is to prefer smaller populations
(e.g., $P=50$--$100$) for computational efficiency.

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}

The convergence behaviour of the GA, from the main run under the frozen scoring pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}
($P=100$, $T=20$, $|\mathcal{I}|=600$), is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:fitness-curve}. The best (training-time) fitness begins
at 0.701 in generation~1 and fluctuates within a narrow band of approximately
0.698--0.704 for the remainder of the run, with no sustained secondary
improvement phase; this band is itself at the single-evaluation noise floor
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), so the single highest value observed
(0.704, generation~19)
is not resolvable from the initial value. Average fitness rises from 0.362
(generation~1) to approximately 0.60--0.65 by generation~5 and continues to
fluctuate in that range without a clear settling trend for the remainder of
the run, indicating that elite genomes consistently outperform the
population mean throughout optimisation, while population-level improvement
plateaus earlier than any further gain in the single best genome. Note that
these training-time fitness values, on a randomly sampled $|\mathcal{I}|=600$
subset of the optimisation set for each generation, differ both in scale
and in the underlying prompt sample from the validation-set scores reported
in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}.

The near-optimal generation-1
best fitness is attributable to a specific individual, not to the balanced
seed genome $g_\mathrm{bal}$: per-generation genome logs show
the generation-1 best individual is already structurally similar to the
eventual reported genome, consistent with a descendant of the
structure-heavy seed $g_\mathrm{str}$ rather than $g_\mathrm{bal}$ (full
genome values in Appendix~\ref{app:gen1}). This supports the
broader point that seeded initialisation (four hand-designed seeds
spanning different regions of the genome space, rather than a fully random
population) accelerates convergence toward a high-structure region, via
$g_\mathrm{str}$-like individuals reaching high fitness quickly.

\subsection{Population Diversity}
\label{sec:diversity}

Figure~\ref{fig:diversity-curve} shows fitness dispersion across
generations, measured as the variance of the population's fitness values
rather than a direct measure of genome (genotype) diversity; we adopt this
more precise name after noting that fitness variance can behave
differently from genuine genome-space diversity in a landscape containing
inactive genes ($\theta_\mathrm{mid}$, $\theta_\mathrm{high}$), threshold
effects (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}), and behaviourally
equivalent categorical values (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}). Variance
begins at approximately 0.096 in generation~1, reflecting the mixed
population of seed genomes and random initialisations. It fluctuates
between approximately 0.030 and 0.096 across the run without a sustained
downward trend. This pattern is consistent with a fitness landscape in
which many distinct genome configurations achieve similar fitness values;
confirming whether this also reflects genuine sustained genome-level
diversity (e.g.\ via per-gene variance or pairwise genome distance) rather
than fitness-score clustering alone is listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{MSGA-Seeded Initialization Comparison}
\label{sec:msga}

To evaluate whether multiple-seed, domain-coverage initialization can
substitute for manually designed seed genomes, we implemented an adaptation
of the Multiple Seeds Based Genetic Algorithm (MSGA)~\cite{kabir2017msga} as
an alternative initialization strategy (condition B5). MSGA constructs an
initial population from multiple seed candidates selected across different
regions of the solution space. In our adaptation, the mixed genome space is
partitioned into \(m=4\) domains using empirical distance boundaries estimated
from 500 randomly sampled genomes. We retain an archive of 25 candidates per
domain and use archive-derived seeds to initialize the population.

B5 was evaluated under the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}) as B1--B4, in the same pipeline
run, with its final genome archived as \texttt{best\_genome\_B5.json}. It
achieves empathy 0.439, safety 0.900, structure 1.000, and fitness 0.695,
compared with B4's fitness of 0.703
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}). The 0.008-point difference is close to
the resolution limit implied by the single-evaluation noise floor
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) and rests on one B5 evaluation; we
therefore do not interpret it as reliable evidence of a difference between B4
and B5. Within the present search space, this result suggests that
MSGA-inspired domain-coverage initialization can provide an alternative to
manually designed seed genomes. B5 was run once; repeated MSGA-initialised runs are required before drawing a
statistical comparison with B4.

\cite{banglat5_2022} (B7; \texttt{csebuetnlp/banglat5}), and BanglaGPT
\cite{banglagpt_2023} (B8; \texttt{shahidul034/BanglaGPT}). These conditions
are not GA genomes and receive no task-specific fine-tuning, in-context
examples, or EvoRiri response templates. They are evaluated on the same fixed
400-prompt seed-7 validation subsample as B1--B5. The underlying user messages
are constructed using \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()} and wrapped in
a common English instruction template requesting an empathetic and helpful
assistant response.

All three unadapted checkpoints produce poor conversational outputs under this
configuration. B6 generates sentinel tokens such as
\texttt{<extra\_id\_0>}, yielding zero empathy and structure scores; this is
consistent with the T5-style span-corruption pretraining framework of
mT5~\cite{mt5_2021}. B7 achieves the strongest component scores of the three

frequently produces repeated assistant prefixes, repeated user text, or
incomplete sentences. B8 generates repetitive character sequences, with
empathy 0.003, safety 0.426, zero structure, and fitness $-0.448$. These
results describe zero-shot behavior under the present prompts and decoding

LLMs. BanglaT5, for example, reports multi-turn dialogue results after
task-specific training~\cite{banglat5_2022}.All B6--B8 values are computed using the same full per-example fitness pipeline
as B1--B5.

\subsection{Statistical Significance of Baseline Comparisons}
\label{sec:significance}

To assess whether the GA--baseline differences were consistently positive
across five paired seed-level re-evaluations, we applied one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945}. For each GA seed, we re-scored that
seed's archived GA genome together with the fixed B1 and B2 genomes
the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}) ($N_{\mathrm{eval}}=400$ on the
seed-7 validation split). Every number in this section and in
Table~\ref{tab:significance} is a directly observed result from these
re-scores of archived genomes, with no imputed scalar correction applied
across seeds.

The GA consistently outperformed both baselines across all five runs
(Table~\ref{tab:significance}). The one-sided Wilcoxon test confirms that the
GA-optimised system achieves higher fitness than both B1 ($W = 15$,
$p = 0.031$) and B2 ($W = 15$, $p = 0.031$). We note that with only $n=5$
paired observations, $p=0.03125$ ($1/32$) is the smallest attainable
one-sided $p$-value under this test, obtained whenever all five differences
share the same sign; the test therefore establishes that the direction of
the effect is consistent across seeds, but the $p$-value itself carries
little further information at this sample size and should not be read as
indicating an unusually strong effect beyond what five-for-five agreement
already implies. We report the raw paired differences directly rather than
leading with standardised effect sizes: all five GA-vs-B1 differences fall
in $[0.898, 0.933]$ (median $0.912$), and all five GA-vs-B2 differences
fall in $[0.138, 0.161]$ (median $0.147$), so both comparisons are positive
and tightly clustered across every seed. Bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals (10{,}000 resamples over the five paired differences, percentile
method) are $[0.904, 0.928]$ for GA vs.\ B1 and $[0.141, 0.154]$ for GA
vs.\ B2, both well clear of zero. We also computed Cohen's $d$ (59.1 for
GA vs.\ B1, 16.3 for GA vs.\ B2) for continuity with the effect-size
reporting convention, but flag that these values are not meaningfully
interpretable using ordinary Cohen's $d$ benchmarks at this sample size:
with only five paired differences, $d$ is dominated by the very small
variance \emph{among} those five differences rather than by the
underlying effect in the way $d$ is normally intended to summarise. Given the tightly
clustered, uniformly positive raw differences, we treat the direction and
approximate magnitude of the improvement over both baselines as
well-supported by this analysis, while
acknowledging that a larger number of seeds (e.g., 20--30) would still be preferable for a
more conventional significance test and a tighter estimate of run-to-run
variability; we list this alongside the other statistical
recommendations in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

correspondingly near-zero but not exactly zero (0.017,

occasional edge cases in response assembly or scoring rather than to any
deliberate structural inclusion.
In contrast, B4 responses consistently include all three structural
components, since $w_c^{\star} = 0.768$ falls in the top, deterministic
regime, with each component serving a distinct therapeutic function:
grounding exercises anchor the user in the present moment, action
suggestions provide concrete next steps, and reflective questions invite
further disclosure.

Second, escalation behaviour differs substantially by risk level in B4 but
not in B1. The generator's escalation rule
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) applies unconditionally to both
whenever the risk label is high, independent of genome weight; the
difference visible in Table~\ref{tab:baseline_vs_optimized} instead arises
from B1's minimum response-part budget ($m=256$) truncating escalation
content before it reaches the visible response, whereas B4's $m=512$
disables truncation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:part-budget}). B4 accordingly suppresses
explicit crisis language for low-risk prompts while reliably surfacing
escalation guidance for high-risk ones. This risk-differentiation does not
depend on $\theta_\mathrm{mid}$ or $\theta_\mathrm{high}$, which the
generator does not read; and the reported B1-to-B4 gains therefore combine
GA optimisation with the effect of not triggering this defect, as
decomposed in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}.

weaving Bangla, Banglish, and English phrases within
a single response across functionally distinct components (grounding,
action, and question); as with the prompts themselves
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), this multilingual variation reflects the
generator's hand-authored phrase banks rather than a claim about
authentic code-switching patterns in real conversations. B1 draws its
opening and reflection
lines from the same multilingual phrase banks and so can also
include Bangla (as in the low-risk example above), but it produces
substantially fewer lines overall and, since it lacks structural
content, never combines Bangla and English within a grounding,
action, or question line the way B4 does.

(0.017 to 1.000), safety by $+0.433$ (0.472 to 0.905), and empathy by $+0.161$

response content. However, the safety and structure differences in the B1--B4
comparison partly reflect B1's response-part-budget truncation defect rather
than genetic optimisation alone.

The empathy gain is worth flagging on its own terms: the $+0.161$ absolute
difference from B1 to B4 is smaller than the corresponding differences in
structure ($+0.983$) and safety ($+0.433$). Thus, empathy is the smallest of
the three automatic-metric differences in the B1--B4 comparison. The structure
and safety differences should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously, because
B1 also carries the short response-part-budget truncation defect discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}. Read in
isolation, this could suggest the GA does comparatively little for
empathy. Section~\ref{sec:human_eval} complicates that reading: the
same B1-vs-B4 comparison, rated blind by clinical psychologists, shows
empathy improving by roughly as much as safety and helpfulness. We
return to this apparent conflict, and what it implies about the
automatic empathy proxy, in Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human_eval}

To validate the automatic scoring metrics and assess the clinical quality
of generated responses, we conducted a human evaluation study with five
clinical psychologists. We note at the outset two design limitations that
qualify the strength of this evaluation, discussed further in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: first, the comparison evaluated is B1 (which,
as documented in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}, carries a truncation
defect rather than only unoptimised weights) versus B4, rather than the
arguably more informative B2-versus-B4 comparison between a competently
hand-configured system and the GA-optimised one; and second, the five
participating psychologists also contributed to the risk-labeling design
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) earlier in this project, so they are not
fully independent of the system under evaluation in the way an external
panel would be.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Design}

We sampled 30 response pairs from the baseline vs.\ optimised comparison
dataset, stratified by risk level (6 low-risk, 9 mid-risk, 15 high-risk),
stratified by risk level (6 low-risk, 9 mid-risk, and 15 high-risk items),
approximately matching the corpus risk distribution.
For each item, annotators were shown the user prompt and two responses
labeled A and B in randomised order, without knowing which system
generated each response (rater-blinded; the researchers preparing and
analysing the response pairs were not themselves blinded to system
identity, so we describe this design as single-blind from the annotators'
perspective rather than double-blind).
Each annotator independently
rated both responses on three dimensions using a 1--5 scale:
\emph{Empathy} (emotional validation and warmth),
\emph{Safety} (appropriateness and crisis guidance calibration),
and \emph{Helpfulness} (concrete, actionable support).
After rating both responses, annotators indicated an overall preference.
Inter-annotator agreement was measured using Krippendorff's
alpha~\cite{krippendorff2007reliability} and pairwise percentage agreement
within one scale point.

\subsubsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} summarises the results. The GA-optimised
system (B4) substantially outperforms the minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) on
all three dimensions, with item-level mean improvements of $+1.68$ on
Empathy, $+1.77$ on Safety, and $+1.95$ on Helpfulness. These estimates
and their significance tests are computed at the level of the 30
response-pair items rather than the 150 individual annotator-item
ratings, since each item was rated by all five annotators and the
within-item ratings are correlated rather than independent; we detail
the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:pseudorep} below. Across the 150
annotator-item preference judgements (5 annotators $\times$ 30 items),
B4 was preferred in 146 (97.3\%), B1 in 2 (1.3\%), with 2 judgements
(1.3\%) marked equal. At the item level, all five annotators agreed
unanimously on 27 of the 30 items (all favouring B4); the remaining
three items each drew at least one dissenting judgement (one annotator
preferring B1 on each of two items, and two annotators rating a third
item as equal), rather than uniform agreement across all 30 items. Given
that B1's disadvantage here partly reflects the truncation defect rather
than parameter choice alone (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}), and that
the comparison is not fully blinded to the study team, we read this result
as showing that clinicians strongly prefer a complete, non-truncated,
GA-optimised response over a truncated, minimally-weighted one, rather
than as isolated evidence that evolutionary search specifically improves
clinical quality beyond what a competently hand-configured system (B2)
would already achieve; a B2-vs-B4 clinician comparison, which we did not
run, would speak more directly to that latter question, and we list it as
a required follow-up in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

These findings help interpret the tension flagged in Section~\ref{sec:qualcomp}
on the automatic proxy. In the B1--B4 comparison, the automatic empathy
difference is $0.161$, smaller than the corresponding structure and safety
differences. On blinded clinician ratings of the same comparison, however,
empathy improved by 1.68 points, similar in magnitude to the safety (1.77) and
helpfulness (1.95) differences. We have not,
however, formally tested whether these three effects are statistically equal
to one another (e.g.\ via a system-by-dimension interaction test), so we
describe them as similar in magnitude rather than as confirmed to be
statistically indistinguishable.

The two measurements agree in direction, both favouring B4, but not in the
apparent magnitude of the empathy improvement. One plausible explanation is
limited sensitivity of the marker-based proxy: the rule-based empathy score
saturates once a handful of distinct markers are present
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:empathy}) and penalises repetition, so it has limited dynamic
range to register further differences once a response clears that low bar.
Clinicians reading the full response may still perceive and reward differences
in warmth, specificity, and emotional attunement that the marker count does
not distinguish. This is a concrete instance of the proxy-validity limitation
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: in this B1-vs-B4 comparison, the
proxy may under-credit an improvement perceived by clinician raters, whereas
the marker-stuffing case in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck} illustrates
the opposite risk of over-crediting surface-level phrasing.

\subsubsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}
\label{sec:pseudorep}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition are not 150 independent
observations, since ratings are correlated within annotator and within
item. We therefore report inference at the level of the 30 items, the
genuine unit of independent replication, by averaging the 5 raters' scores
per item and condition before computing paired differences. This yields
the same mean improvements as the raw annotator-item means in
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} with correctly calibrated uncertainty, and
remains highly significant under paired $t$-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests, and a crossed linear mixed model. Full statistics for all three
checks are in Appendix~\ref{app:humaneval}.

\subsubsection{Inter-Annotator Agreement}

We computed Krippendorff's alpha using the \texttt{krippendorff} Python
package with an interval difference function over the 60 blinded response
units (30 items $\times$ 2 responses), yielding a $5 \times 60$ reliability
matrix with no missing ratings. Alpha was $0.56$ for Empathy, $0.58$ for
Safety, and $0.61$ for Helpfulness; percentile-bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals from 2{,}000 resamples were $[0.48, 0.62]$, $[0.50, 0.64]$, and
$[0.54, 0.66]$, respectively.

Krippendorff suggested $\alpha \geq 0.800$ when coded data are to be
considered reliably interpretable by other scholars, and $\alpha \geq 0.667$
as a minimum for drawing only tentative conclusions; he also cautioned that
these values are not universal cutoffs \cite{krippendorff2004reliability}.
All point estimates and upper confidence bounds fall below $0.667$.
Accordingly, we interpret the 1--5 ratings as directional comparative
evidence, rather than as calibrated absolute quality measurements.

Pairwise agreement within one scale point was 86.0\% for Empathy, 87.2\%
for Safety, and 85.2\% for Helpfulness, indicating that large rater
divergences were uncommon. The preference results reported above provide
separate evidence of a strongly directional B4-over-B1 preference; thus,
we interpret the human evaluation as directional rather than as calibrated
absolute quality measurement.


\subsubsection{Validation of Automatic Metrics}

Comparing Tables~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and~\ref{tab:human_eval}, the
automatic empathy and safety scores rank B4 above B1 by the same ordering
as the corresponding human ratings, on both dimensions. The two scales are
not directly comparable in magnitude, but the consistent rank ordering
supports the automatic scores as useful optimisation signals even without
absolute calibration against clinical standards, keeping in mind the
caveats above regarding B1's truncation defect and the partial lack of
independence between the human evaluation panel and the system's design
process.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Role of Seeded Initialization}

Seeded initialization plausibly plays a dual role in the proposed
framework. On the positive side, seeding with four
hand-designed genomes alongside randomly initialised individuals is a
common approach for accelerating convergence and improving reproducibility
across runs relative to fully random initialisation. Per-generation genome
logs show that the near-optimal generation-1 best fitness (0.701, training-time)
is attributable to a structure-heavy individual ($w_c \approx 0.68$)
consistent with a mutated or crossed-over descendant of the structure-heavy
seed $g_\mathrm{str}$, rather than the balanced seed $g_\mathrm{bal}$: $g_\mathrm{bal}$'s own directly
measured fitness (0.556 as B2) is well below that generation-1 value. On
the negative side, if the scoring function contains
exploitable shortcuts, seeds may help the GA
converge more quickly to suboptimal behavioral patterns. In our
experiments, all seeds converged to question-only structural collapse
under the initial scoring design, because the scoring function allowed
question-asking alone to satisfy structural completeness requirements. This
highlights an important principle: seeded initialization amplifies whatever
signal the fitness function provides, whether that signal is well-designed
or not. The combination of seeded initialization with trace-level
evaluation is therefore essential: seeds without careful scoring validation
risk accelerating convergence toward the wrong objective.

The convergence pattern shows the GA
reaching near-optimal training fitness in generation~1, via this
structure-heavy lineage, and maintaining this
through elitism while mutation continues to explore, with no sustained
second improvement phase, indicating the search does not discover a
substantially better configuration than this early high-fitness region
within 20 generations.

\subsection{Importance of Trace Analysis}

Scalar fitness values alone suggested successful optimization throughout the
early development of this framework. However, trace analysis of exported
response logs and representative conversational samples revealed major
behavioral failures that aggregate metrics concealed entirely. The most
significant was question-only structural collapse: responses with fitness
values of 0.6--0.7 were in fact responses consisting almost entirely of
questions, which scored well on the structure metric due to a flaw in the
initial scoring function that rewarded question presence without requiring
grounding or actionable guidance.

This finding has broader implications for the evaluation of generative
AI systems. Optimization metrics, whether hand-crafted or learned, define
what the system optimizes for, not necessarily what the system should do.
When metrics are imperfectly specified, evolutionary systems are particularly
effective at finding and exploiting the gap. Unlike gradient-based methods
that optimize smoothly, evolutionary search can discover discrete behavioral
modes that appear high-fitness under the metric while violating the underlying
intent. Trace analysis is therefore not merely a supplementary diagnostic but
a necessary component of the evaluation pipeline for any reward-driven
generative system. The response-part-budget truncation defect documented in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}, and the marker-coverage gap documented in
Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}, are further instances of this same
broader pattern: both were found only through tracing individual generated
responses against internal generation flags, not through inspecting
aggregate scores.

\subsection{Scoring Function as Bottleneck}
\label{sec:scoring-bottleneck}

Our trace analyses indicate that the scoring and generation pipeline, rather
than the GA search procedure itself, is the primary source of the
discrepancies documented in this work. The first four failure modes
illustrate reward hacking, in which an optimiser exploits an unintended
shortcut in the stated objective rather than achieving the underlying
intended behaviour~\cite{amodei2016concrete}. In our context, we observed
and either corrected or documented five distinct failure modes.

First, \textit{question-only structural collapse}: the initial structure
score rewarded question presence without requiring grounding or actionable
guidance, leading the GA to generate responses consisting almost entirely of
reflective questions. We corrected this by replacing the continuous,
unbounded reward with the discrete component-count lookup in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, under which a question-only response satisfies
exactly one component and is capped at $0.20$.

Second, \textit{empathy marker stuffing}: the initial empathy score rewarded
marker count linearly, incentivising responses that repeated validation
phrases. We corrected this by introducing a stuffing penalty when total
marker hits exceed~3.

Third, \textit{safety saturation}: the initial safety score saturated quickly,
providing little selection signal to distinguish adequate from stronger
crisis responses. We introduced smooth safety soft thresholds, although the
current threshold of $0.94$ is unattainable for any mid-risk response
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) and therefore does not fully resolve
this limitation for that risk category.

Fourth, \textit{structure-marker coverage gap}: an incomplete keyword list
undercounted genuinely inserted structural components for a subset of
high-risk responses (Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}). We corrected
this by expanding the marker lists.

Fifth, \textit{response-part-budget truncation}: B1's minimal response-part
budget silently drops escalation content appended late in the assembly order
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). This is a generator implementation
defect rather than a scoring-function issue per se, but we include it here
because, like the preceding issues, it was detected through tracing
individual responses rather than through aggregate metrics.

The interpretability of the genome and the modular scoring design enabled
these diagnoses: each score component and generation step corresponded to a
specific behavioural dimension, allowing failure modes to be attributed and,
where possible, corrected individually. The first four issues concern the
scoring or objective-design pipeline used the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}). The fifth is specific to B1's
construction rather than to the GA's scoring or search process, and is
tracked separately as a required baseline correction
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).


\subsection{Data Efficiency and Practical Deployment}
\label{sec:data-efficiency}

The dataset-size experiments, using nested training subsets and a
pre-specified TOST equivalence test (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-size}), show
that mean validation fitness stays within a narrow 0.0068-point band
across all tested sizes ($N=100$ to $N=2297$) with no monotonic trend.
TOST establishes statistical equivalence to the full-dataset condition at
the pre-specified margin ($\varepsilon=0.01$) for $N=100$, $N=300$, and
$N=1200$; only $N=600$ falls marginally outside the margin, coinciding with
the experiment's largest seed-level dispersion and not forming a monotonic
pattern with subset size (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-size}). Mean runtime across the same nested-subset
run ranges from 15.9\,s at $N=100$ to 478.2\,s at $N=1200$, without a
monotonic trend (Figure~\ref{fig:runtime}); the guidance below therefore
rests on fitness stability rather than on runtime scaling. Larger datasets
provide no apparent performance benefit within the range tested.

For resource-constrained settings such as community mental-health NGOs or
university counselling services in Bangladesh, these findings motivate
testing whether useful calibration may be possible with datasets on the
order of 100--300 de-identified support interactions, a scale achievable
within a short deployment pilot. We frame this as a hypothesis for future
testing rather than an established property. Equivalence at
$\varepsilon=0.01$ was demonstrated for $N=100$ and $N=300$ under the frozen
scorer, but the margin is roughly three times the evaluation noise standard
deviation (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), the prompts are synthetic
reconstructions, and the validation split also served scorer development;
the result therefore concerns fitness stability under this proxy scorer
rather than demonstrated calibration quality on real interactions.
The template-based generator also requires no GPU
infrastructure or LLM API calls during inference, making it deployable on
standard hardware. However, these practical advantages must be weighed against
the limitations discussed below: the dataset must cover a sufficiently diverse
range of risk levels and themes, and the scoring functions must be validated
against local clinical norms before deployment.

Fitness evaluation dominates runtime, so cost per GA run is approximately
$\mathcal{O}(P \cdot T \cdot |\mathcal{I}|)$ --- about 1.2M generate-and-score
operations at $P=100$, $T=20$, $|\mathcal{I}|=600$. Because $|\mathcal{I}|$ is
capped at 600, this count does not grow with dataset size beyond $N=600$.
Measured wall-clock times do not show the corresponding plateau
(Figure~\ref{fig:runtime}), which we attribute to timing variability on
non-dedicated consumer hardware rather than to any algorithmic property; the
total runtime (minutes, not GPU-hours) is the reliable takeaway, not its shape
across $N$. This contrasts with fine-tuning or RLHF-based approaches, which
typically require GPU infrastructure unavailable in the deployment settings
this framework targets.

\subsection{Interpretation of Baseline Results}
\label{sec:interp-baseline}

B1's weights ($w_e=0.10$, $w_c=0.10$, $w_s=0.80$; Section~\ref{sec:methods})
are chosen so that genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged, keeping
$w_c$ genuinely in the bottom, no-structure regime rather than drifting
into the stochastic mid-regime a naive equal-floor construction would
otherwise produce.

The baseline comparison results reveal important insights about the sources
of performance gain. The near-zero structure score of B1 (0.013 in the common 400-prompt
re-evaluation) shows that this minimal-weight configuration produces almost
entirely unstructured responses, despite the underlying phrase banks
containing grounding, action, and question components. Its negative fitness
($-0.194$) is primarily driven by low safety (0.475), which we trace to the
response-part-budget truncation mechanism described in
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget} (B1's $m=256$ setting silently drops
escalation content for high-risk prompts) rather than to failure of the
fixed risk-aware escalation rule itself.

This is a genuine generator-design defect rather than merely an unoptimised
parameter setting, because it can silently remove required crisis content. To
isolate its effect, we re-evaluated B1, B1-fixed, and B4 using one frozen
generation and scoring path on the identical fixed 400-prompt validation
subsample. B1-fixed differs from B1 only in \texttt{memory\_window} (512
rather than 256).

Table~\ref{tab:truncation-diagnostic} shows that removing truncation raises
Safety from 0.475 to 0.905 and Fitness from $-0.194$ to 0.277, while leaving
Structure essentially unchanged at approximately 0.012. Empathy also rises
from 0.273 to 0.323, suggesting that truncation removes response content
beyond escalation guidance. B4 achieves essentially the same Safety score

Thus, under this common evaluation, B1's safety deficit is consistent with
being driven primarily by the response-part-budget truncation defect, whereas
B4's structural advantage is not: it arises in the comparison between the
non-truncating minimal-structure configuration and B4. The total fitness
difference is descriptively divided between removal of truncation
($+0.471$ from B1 to B1-fixed) and the remaining B1-fixed--B4 configuration
difference ($+0.423$). Because these values come from one common
re-evaluation run, they should be interpreted as a controlled diagnostic
pattern rather than precise noise-free effect estimates. The B1-fixed--B4
difference is not a pure estimate of the GA's causal contribution because
the conditions differ in multiple genome parameters.

The B2--B4 comparison is more informative for assessing the remaining
configuration difference, because B2 does not share B1's truncation defect.
The balanced seed genome already achieves high safety (0.903) but leaves
substantial room for structural improvement (0.648 vs.\ 1.000). The reported
B4 configuration uses a high structure weight
($w_c^{\star} \approx 0.77$), which is associated with higher structural
completeness than the balanced initialisation. We verified
this directly with a 12-point grid search over $w_c$ alone (holding the
$w_e{:}w_s$ ratio fixed at the reported genome's value, so that raw
weights already sum to 1 and genome renormalisation is a no-op at every
grid point): the results reproduce all three structure regimes exactly at
the thresholds stated in Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure} --
structure is effectively zero for $w_c < 0.25$ (with small residual values
possible because of response-assembly or scoring edge cases), fluctuates
stochastically around 0.62--0.66 for $0.25 \le w_c < 0.55$, and reaches
1.000 for every grid point at $w_c \ge 0.55$. Fitness within the deterministic regime is not flat: it rises
from 0.652 at $w_c = 0.55$ to a peak of 0.710 at $w_c = 0.70$, then eases
to 0.701 at the top of the range. The reported genome's
$w_c^{\star} = 0.768$ therefore sits on the plateau but past its peak.
This supports the interpretation that the structure-dominant result does not
require evolutionary search specifically to discover: a twelve-point
sweep over a single gene reaches the same fitness region as the GA's five
converged runs,
consistent with the generator's deterministic structure threshold
($w_c \ge 0.55$; Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}) doing most of
the explanatory work.

We additionally ran five independent equal-budget random searches
(2,000 draws each over the full genome space -- $w_e, w_s, w_c, \theta_\mathrm{mid},
\theta_\mathrm{high}, \gamma, d, m$ -- matching the GA's implicit budget of
$100 \times 20 = 2{,}000$ evaluations), sampled independently of the grid
search above. Scored on the same 400-prompt subsample used for selection,
the five best genomes reach $0.7066$--$0.7110$ (mean $0.7085$, SD
$0.0018$), above every GA seed in Table~\ref{tab:significance}. That
comparison is not sound: each random-search maximum is the largest of
2,000 noisy evaluations on the very set it is reported against, whereas
the GA's genomes were selected on optimisation-set samples and never on
this subsample. Taking a maximum over 2,000 draws from a measurement with
SD $\approx 0.003$ (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) inflates the
reported value by several standard deviations independently of genome
quality.

Re-evaluating the same ten archived genomes does not reveal a resolvable
difference. On the 575-prompt validation
split, GA $0.7001 \pm 0.0025$ versus RS $0.7023 \pm 0.0056$; on the
400-prompt subsample, GA $0.7040 \pm 0.0032$ versus RS $0.7020 \pm
0.0024$. Neither set is fully clean for this comparison: the random
searches selected on the 400-prompt subsample, which is contained in the
575-prompt split, so both are re-evaluations rather than held-out tests
for RS. A re-score on the full 2,872-prompt corpus (GA
$0.7015 \pm 0.0019$ versus RS $0.6996 \pm 0.0013$) is post-selection for
RS but overlaps the GA's optimisation set, so it is not neutral in the
other direction. Across all three sets the difference is
$\approx 0.002$ with an unstable sign, well inside the single-evaluation
noise floor.

We therefore conclude that no difference between the two
methods is resolvable under the present evaluation design, not that the
two are equivalent; a genuinely untouched test set, evaluated only after
selection for both methods, would be required to settle the question.
Every random-search genome loses $0.007$--$0.012$ relative to its
archived selection-time score while the GA genomes do not, which is
consistent with maximisation over noisy selection-time evaluations rather
than demonstrating superior genome quality. This pattern is consistent with
prior work on GA-based prompt optimisation, which reports selection bias
when candidates selected on an evaluation set perform less well on a
separate test set. In that study, a larger-population configuration also
showed a larger test--validation gap, which the authors interpreted as a
higher risk of overfitting; the authors further repeated reported
evaluations because stochastic LLM outputs produced run-to-run score
discrepancies~\cite{gaapo2025}. 


The 2,000 draws underlying each random search have a mean
fitness of only $\approx 0.33$ (SD $\approx 0.33$), so most random genomes
are far from the plateau; what the five runs establish is that undirected
search locates the plateau consistently at this budget, not that any
individual draw is likely to.


The finding also suggests that safety and empathy are more easily satisfied
by the fixed escalation rule, safety penalty terms, and phrase bank design than by genome weight
allocation, freeing the evolutionary search to concentrate on structural
completeness.

These observations answer RQ2 descriptively: B4 maintains safety comparable
to B2 while substantially improving structural completeness. The grid search
identifies the generator's deterministic structure threshold as the mechanism
most plausibly responsible for the structural gain. We do not, however,
attribute that gain causally to progressive structure enforcement in
isolation: B2 and B4 differ in several respects simultaneously (genome
weights, response-part budget, and $\gamma$), and ablations that vary each
mechanism independently while holding the others fixed would be required to
separate their contributions, as we note in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Did the GA Achieve Multi-Objective Balance?}
\label{sec:balance-discussion}

The fixed fitness-weight coefficients
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) assign empathy and safety the largest shares
($A=B=0.40$) and structure the smallest ($C=0.15$), reflecting our
intended prioritisation of safe, empathetic responses. Yet the reported
genome is structure-dominant ($w_c^{\star}=0.768$ versus
$w_e^{\star}=0.131$; Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}), and $w_s^{\star}$ is
not part of this tension at all: as clarified in the genome note
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), $w_s$ is a renormalisation participant rather
than an independently interpretable safety preference, since safety
behaviour is governed by the fixed escalation rule and categorical safety
lookup, not by $w_s$.

The resolution lies in distinguishing the \emph{fixed} coefficients
$A,B,C,D$, which set how base scores combine into fitness, from the
\emph{evolved} genome weights $w_e, w_c$, which determine how much
empathy and structural content the generator actually inserts -- these
operate on different score landscapes. Safety and empathy both saturate
quickly: safety reaches a high plateau ($S_i \approx 0.90$--$1.00$) once
escalation is reliably present, independent of $w_s$, and empathy
saturates once a few distinct markers appear, with further weight risking
the marker-stuffing penalty. Structure, by contrast, has the largest
unrealised headroom in the unoptimised baseline (B1 structure $=0.017$)
and responds sharply to $w_c$ at the deterministic threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}). This headroom asymmetry is
compounded by an explicit reinforcement asymmetry in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}: structure is reinforced twice (up to
$+0.09$ combined) while empathy is reinforced once (capped at $+0.05$) and
safety not at all beyond its base term. So the structure-dominant outcome
reflects both where headroom is largest \emph{and} where the fitness
function rewards marginal content most -- neither of which is visible from
the coefficient table $A,B,C,D$ alone.

This suggests the GA found a corner solution -- empathy near its
saturation floor, structure maximised -- rather than a smooth trade-off
across objectives (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}: empathy stays modest
while structure climbs substantially). This is a genuine limitation of
collapsing multi-objective optimisation into a single scalar fitness with
fixed coefficients: the resulting genome reflects the geometry of the
constraint set as much as the analyst's stated priorities. A
multi-objective evolutionary algorithm maintaining an explicit Pareto
front over $(E_i, S_i, C_i)$, rather than a weighted scalar sum, would
make this trade-off space visible and let a clinician select an operating
point directly; we list this as future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Ethical Risks and Deployment Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

The deployment of optimisation-driven conversational agents in mental-health
contexts raises ethical concerns that extend beyond algorithmic performance.
This subsection critically examines deployment risks specific to the
proposed framework and the Bangladeshi context.

\textbf{Proxy metric misalignment.} The empathy and safety scores are
keyword-based proxies with the construct-validity limits detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}; optimising against them risks producing
responses that are \textit{metrically safe} but not \textit{clinically
safe}. For deployment specifically, this means the proxies are not a
substitute for clinical oversight: any deployment should include ongoing
monitoring by qualified mental health professionals who can identify cases
where high-scoring responses are clinically inappropriate.

\textbf{Over-reliance and substitution risk.} Systems like Riri are designed
as supplementary tools, not replacements for professional care. However, the
substitution risk is heightened by the scarcity of the alternative:
Bangladesh has an estimated 1.17 mental health professionals per 100{,}000
population and no mental health services at primary-care or community level.
The 2019 National Mental Health Survey found that more than 90\% of adults
and children with a mental health condition had not sought treatment, while
participants in a qualitative stakeholder study reported delays of 6--13
years before seeking care, associated with stigma and low mental health
literacy~\cite{koly2024stakeholder}. Users experiencing acute distress may
therefore treat an AI assistant as a primary, or potentially their only,
source of immediate support.

The high structural-component usage observed in our reported system (100\%
insertion rate) means that many responses include a referral to
professional help where appropriate, but if no such help is
accessible, this referral may feel dismissive rather than supportive.
We also note that ``100\% structural-component usage'' refers specifically
to the generator's grounding/action/question components, not exclusively to
professional referrals: an action component may equally be a breathing
exercise, a suggestion to contact a trusted friend, or a grounding
technique, and low-risk prompts specifically do not receive crisis
escalation content at all (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}). Future work should design
context-aware escalation that acknowledges service availability limitations.

\textbf{Cultural and linguistic validity.} The empathy marker sets used in
this work were constructed by the research team from common Bangla/Banglish
expressions observed in the dataset. They have not been validated by clinical
psychologists or community mental-health workers familiar with Bangladeshi
norms of emotional expression. Different communities within Bangladesh
(urban versus rural and across socioeconomic backgrounds) may use different
linguistic patterns to express distress; consequently, a marker set suited to
the PHQ-4 screening population may not generalise. This is a documented
failure mode rather than a hypothetical one: dominant sentiment-analysis
models may misinterpret emotional restraint common in collectivist cultures
as disengagement or low emotional involvement~\cite{ijca2025emotion}. The
screening instrument underlying our risk labels is on firmer ground: the
Bangla PHQ-4 has been psychometrically validated in rural and urban
Bangladeshi community samples~\cite{banglaphq4_2026}. That validation was
conducted in adult community samples and evaluated a screening cutoff, rather
than the four-band severity stratification applied here to a self-referring
digital-support population. This matters particularly because the reported
genome's high structure weight ($w_c^{\star}=0.768$) means structural
components dominate the response, potentially at the cost of
culturally appropriate emotional tone.

\textbf{Reward hacking in deployment.} The failure modes documented in this
work (question-only collapse, empathy marker stuffing, the structure-marker
coverage gap, and the response-part-budget truncation defect) were
discovered through offline trace analysis before deployment. In a live
deployment, new failure modes may emerge from interactions with real users
whose language patterns differ from the training data. If the system is
periodically re-optimised using interaction logs, the GA may discover new
shortcuts that exploit patterns in real user behaviour. Robust deployment
would require continuous trace monitoring, periodic human review of response
samples, and clear mechanisms for clinical or designated safety reviewers to
flag and correct problematic response patterns. The interpretable genome
design facilitates this: because generator behaviour is controlled by a small
set of named parameters, targeted corrections can be made without rerunning
the full optimisation pipeline.

These considerations do not invalidate the proposed framework but rather
define the conditions under which it could be responsibly deployed. The
framework provides a technically informative, though not yet fully validated,
exploration of low-resource mental-health conversational optimisation.
Responsible deployment would require a broader governance structure including
clinical oversight, ongoing human review of safety-relevant interactions, and
culturally informed evaluation protocols~\cite{apa2025_advisory,harvard2024_safety,ijca2025emotion}.

\textbf{Data provenance and risk-category validity.} The high-risk category
in our dataset is populated in part by PHQ-4 scores of 9--12 alone,
independent of the explicit self-harm keyword indicator
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-label}); because PHQ-4 has no suicide-risk item
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}), this may inflate the high-risk category
with severe depression/anxiety presentations that do not necessarily
involve suicidality, and reward crisis escalation for such cases without
direct evidence of suicide risk specifically (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Age field reliability and the 18+ platform policy.} The
application restricts registration to users aged 18 and over, and does
not knowingly collect or use data from users under 18 for research. Age
itself, however, is self-reported at registration and not independently
verified (e.g.\ via identity document), and the dataset's age field
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}, Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats}) shows 422
records (14.7\%) with a self-reported age of 13--17. We cannot determine
from the data alone whether these reflect genuine underage users who
circumvented the registration policy, self-report errors, or another
data-quality issue with this field, and we do not treat this figure as
evidence that confirmed minors are present in the dataset. At the same
time, unverified self-report cannot fully rule out their presence either,
and we do not consider the platform's stated policy alone sufficient
grounds to set the question aside. We recommend that future versions of
this dataset either implement stronger age verification at registration,
or apply an explicit data-governance rule that filters or separately
reviews records with a self-reported age below 18 before they are used
for research. We treat resolving this ambiguity as a prerequisite for any
further research use of this dataset, including any onward sharing or
reuse beyond the analyses reported here, and we report the present results
together with the sensitivity analysis below so that readers can assess
the extent to which they depend on the records in question.

To characterise how much this ambiguity affects the reported results
specifically, we re-evaluated the already-trained genomes for B1,
B1-fixed, B2, and B4 on the 575-prompt validation set with the 88
self-reported-minor records excluded (487 prompts), without retraining
anything, and compared against the same genomes' fitness on the full
validation set (Table~\ref{tab:minors-sensitivity}). This is a
provisional analysis pending the institutional determination described
above, and should not be read as resolving the underlying governance
question -- it addresses only whether the currently reported numbers are
statistically sensitive to this subset, not whether these records should
be included in the study at all.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The following limitations define the scope of the present findings. Detailed
diagnostic evidence is reported in the referenced sections; here we summarise
the implications for interpretation and the corresponding priorities for
future work.

\noindent\textbf{Data, labels, and governance.}
The prompts evaluated here are synthetic reconstructions from structured theme
fields rather than verbatim user messages. Consequently, the findings describe
behaviour on this synthetic corpus and do not establish ecological validity for
authentic Bangla, Banglish, or code-switched mental-health conversations. The
low-, mid-, and high-risk categories combine PHQ-4 severity with a keyword
override and are not validated suicide-risk classifications. In addition, age
is self-reported and a subset of records is labelled 13--17 years; resolving
whether these records reflect genuine underage users or age-field error, and
applying an explicit governance decision, is required before further research
use of the dataset (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\noindent\textbf{Evaluation and causal attribution.}
The validation split also contributed to scorer development, so it should be
understood as a combined development--validation set rather than an untouched
final test set. B1 contains a response-part-budget truncation defect, making it
primarily a failure-mode reference rather than a clean unoptimised baseline.
Although B2 provides the most informative fixed non-GA reference, B2 and B4
differ in multiple configuration settings; their comparison therefore does not
isolate the causal contribution of evolutionary search. The B1-fixed
truncation diagnostic is based on a single re-evaluation and should be read as
a diagnostic pattern rather than as a precise causal-effect estimate.
Likewise, no evaluation set is untouched by both the GA and equal-budget
random-search selection procedures, so the present study cannot resolve
whether either method has a general advantage. B5 was evaluated in one run only
and requires repeated MSGA-initialised runs for a reliable comparison.
Finally, the nested dataset-size and population-size experiments establish
equivalence at the pre-specified TOST margin for all tested population sizes
and for three of the four tested dataset sizes, but on five seeds per
condition and with a margin of $\varepsilon=0.01$ that is roughly three times
the single-evaluation noise standard deviation; the result bounds the size of
any effect rather than showing that the conditions are identical.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement and human evaluation.}
Generation is stochastic, giving repeated evaluations of the same genome an
observed fitness standard deviation of approximately 0.003. Differences near
this scale, including the B4--B5 and GA--random-search differences, are not
resolvable under the present evaluation design. The dataset-size and
population-size differences are of this same order, and each condition
contributes only five independent GA seeds; the equivalence tests reported
for them therefore bound the size of any difference at the pre-specified
$\varepsilon=0.01$ margin, which is roughly three times that noise standard
deviation, rather than resolving the individual differences or showing the
conditions to be identical. The baseline significance tests use only five GA seeds: their
one-sided Wilcoxon result establishes directional consistency across those
runs, but does not provide a precise estimate of population-level uncertainty.
Reported population ``diversity'' is fitness dispersion rather than direct
genome-space diversity. The automatic empathy and safety scores are
keyword-based proxies that cannot fully capture contextual appropriateness,
emotional attunement, sarcasm, negation, or culturally specific expression.
The clinician evaluation compares B1 with B4 rather than B2 with B4; its
raters also contributed to the earlier risk-label design, and inter-rater
agreement supports directional preference rather than calibrated absolute
quality claims. Future work should use an independent panel, an anchored rating
rubric, and a B2--B4 comparison.

\noindent\textbf{Scope and deployment.}
The framework evaluates single-turn, template-based responses rather than
multi-turn dialogue. It therefore cannot assess longer-horizon risks such as
emotional dependence, reassurance loops, belief reinforcement, or changes in
risk across a conversation. The current mid-risk safety rule applies a penalty
even when its highest attainable safety score is reached, and some genome genes
are inactive or only weakly identified by the present objective. The reported
policy is also not personalised: one fixed genome is applied to all users,
without modelling individual language preferences or preferences for directive
versus reflective support. Future work should evaluate authentic conversational
data under appropriate governance, use independent test sets and repeated
seeded generations, validate the metrics with local clinical and community
stakeholders, and extend the objective to multi-turn, clinician-audited safety
outcomes.

Natural extensions of this work include:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Multi-turn sequential genome policies, in which the GA optimises
  escalation and structural decisions conditioned on conversation history.
  This direction is motivated by work arguing that mental-health chatbot safety
  should be evaluated at the dialogue level and that risk-relevant effects can
  accumulate across extended interactions~\cite{journey2026}. It may also
  provide selection pressure on $\gamma$, which the present single-turn
  objective cannot identify (Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty}).

  \item A more robust generator design that separates response-length
  truncation from internal template and safety-control tokens, or reorders
  response assembly so that escalation content is inserted before
  lower-priority content and cannot be discarded by the response-part budget.
  This would prevent the class of defect illustrated by B1 by construction,
  rather than merely diagnosing it through comparison with B1-fixed.

  \item Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms that maintain explicit Pareto
  fronts over empathy, safety, and structure, making trade-offs visible to a
  clinician rather than collapsing them into a single scalar fitness.

  \item Learned empathy and safety evaluators trained and validated on
  independently clinician-annotated data, replacing or complementing the
  current rule-based proxy scores.

  \item Human-feedback-informed evaluation, in which independently collected
  clinician and user ratings are used to validate or refine the fitness
  function, with safety-critical criteria retained separately rather than
  reduced to a single preference score.

  \item An independent clinician-in-the-loop evaluation, including B2 and a
  non-truncating B1 variant alongside B4, with mental-health professionals
  rating blinded response pairs under a controlled evaluation protocol. Any
  subsequent live deployment study would require separate clinical oversight,
  governance, and safety monitoring.

  \item Multilingual expansion beyond Bangla/Banglish/English to other South
  Asian languages where mental-health support infrastructure is similarly
  limited.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a risk-aware genetic optimization framework for a mental-health
conversational agent operating in Bangla, Banglish, and English. By evolving an
interpretable genome controlling a structured generator, the system achieves
substantial improvements in response structure and overall fitness, maintaining
an already-strong escalation-calibration safety proxy and modestly increasing empathy relative
to a fixed, hand-designed baseline genome. All results come from a single,
consistent scoring pipeline with per-seed genomes archived throughout.
Decomposing the largest baseline gap (B1 to B4) shows it splits between a
software-level response-truncation effect and genuine genome optimisation
in different proportions for safety and structure respectively
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}); a grid search and five equal-budget
random searches over the genome space reach the same fitness region as the
GA, indicating the structure-dominant result reflects a
broadly reachable fitness plateau for this genome space and fitness
design rather than something evolutionary search was specifically
required to find (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). Final fitness is statistically equivalent to the full-dataset condition for
the tested nested subsets $N=100$, $N=300$, and $N=1200$, but not for
$N=600$, at the pre-specified equivalence margin of $\pm0.01$ fitness points.
Final fitness is statistically equivalent across all tested population sizes
from $P=30$ to $P=400$ relative to $P=100$
(Sections~\ref{sec:dataset-size} and~\ref{sec:population-size}). The
study illustrates how
evolutionary algorithms can optimize non-differentiable, value-laden objectives,
and highlights the role of scoring design, trace-based evaluation, and
careful baseline construction in reporting AI systems for mental-health
support. The framework's observed low-data performance stability, its
interpretability, and its deployment feasibility on standard hardware make
it a promising foundation for resource-constrained mental-health
applications in multilingual settings, within the scope and limitations
detailed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Evolutionary Failure Mode Examples}
\label{app:failure}

This appendix provides concrete examples of the two evolutionary failure
modes documented in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: question-only structural
collapse and empathy marker stuffing. These failure modes were discovered
through trace analysis during early development, before the scoring function
was redesigned. The examples below are representative of the response patterns
produced by high-fitness genomes under the initial (flawed) scoring design.

\subsection{Question-Only Structural Collapse}
\label{app:collapse}

Under the initial structure score, question presence alone was sufficient to
satisfy the structural completeness requirement. Genomes with high question
weight converged to responses that scored 1.0 on structure while containing
no grounding instructions or actionable guidance. A representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``I feel really anxious and overwhelmed. Family niye onek
stress e achi. What should I do now?''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high structure score):}
``How long have you been feeling this way? What do you think is causing
the most stress? Have you talked to anyone about this? Is there something
specific that happened recently? What usually helps you feel better?''

\textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad \textit{Empathy score:} 0.05 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60
\end{quote}

The response achieves a perfect structure score by including five questions,
but provides no emotional validation, no grounding exercise, and no
actionable guidance. It would be experienced by a distressed user as an
interrogation rather than support. This failure mode was corrected by
replacing the continuous, count-based structure score with the discrete
component-count lookup of Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, under which any response
satisfying only one of the three components (grounding, action, or
question) is capped at $0.20$, regardless of how many times that single
component is echoed.

\subsection{Empathy Marker Stuffing}
\label{app:stuffing-example}

Under the initial empathy score, marker count was rewarded linearly without
a diversity penalty. Genomes converged to responses that repeated the same
validation phrase multiple times to maximise the empathy score. A
representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi lately. I feel really
anxious and overwhelmed.''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high empathy score):}
``It makes sense that you feel this way. It makes sense to feel
overwhelmed. It makes sense that this is hard. It makes sense that
you're struggling. What would help you right now?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.90 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 0.20
\end{quote}

The response achieves a high empathy score through repetition of a single
phrase but reads as formulaic and insincere. This failure mode was corrected
by introducing the stuffing penalty of $\beta = 0.05$ when total marker
hits exceed~3, discouraging repetitive phrase patterns.

\subsection{Post-Fix Optimised Response (Same Prompt)}
\label{app:postfix}

After scoring redesign, the same prompt from Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing-example}
produces:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Optimised response (post-fix):}
``Feeling this alone can be really painful. {\bengalifont তুমি একা না।}
First, try to sit somewhere comfortable and take one slow breath.
Try not to stay completely isolated with these thoughts.
Is there someone nearby you feel comfortable talking to?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.50 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60 (mid-risk, no escalation content; see
Appendix~\ref{app:worked-example} for the corresponding fitness
calculation)
\end{quote}

The post-fix response achieves balanced scores across all three dimensions,
combining emotional validation, a grounding instruction, an action
suggestion, and a single focused reflective question.

\subsection{Worked Example: From Prompt to Fitness}
\label{app:worked-example}

This subsection walks the same prompt and response from
Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} through every
stage of the pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, using the reported
genome $g^{\star}$ from Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}
($w_e^{\star}=0.131$, $w_s^{\star}=0.101$, $w_c^{\star}=0.768$,
$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}^{\star}=0.588$, $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}^{\star}=0.700$).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Prompt + risk label.} $x_i = $ ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi
        lately. I feel really anxious and overwhelmed.'' For illustration we
        take $r_i = \mathrm{mid}$.
  \item \textbf{Genome.} The generator is configured with $g^{\star}$ as
        above (Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}).
  \item \textbf{Generator.} Because $w_c^{\star}=0.768$ falls in the top
        structure regime ($w_c \ge 0.55$; Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
        the generator deterministically includes grounding, action, and
        question components ($c_g=c_a=c_q=1$); $w_e^{\star}$ governs how
        many empathy lines are included.
  \item \textbf{Response.} $y_i = G(x_i,r_i;g^{\star}) = $ the response shown
        in Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} above.
  \item \textbf{Automatic scoring.} Running this exact response through the
        implemented scoring functions yields $E_i = 0.50$, $S_i = 0.60$,
        $C_i = 1.00$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:empathy}--\eqref{eq:structure}). The
        response contains no escalation-language marker (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}),
        which is expected and appropriate for a mid-risk prompt whose
        computed distress score is low ($h_i \approx 0$, well below
        $\tau_h=0.65$); under the categorical safety lookup
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}), a mid-risk response without escalation
        content scores $S_i=0.60$ by construction, and $S_i=1.00$ is not an
        attainable value for a mid-risk prompt under any response content.
  \item \textbf{Fitness.} The response is below the 140-word penalty
        threshold, so $L_i=0$. Since $S_i=0.60 < 0.94$, the flat safety
        soft-threshold penalty of $-0.15$ applies (the risk label is mid,
        not high, so the $-0.50$ high-risk penalty does not apply); since
        $h_i \le \tau_h$, the distress-gating penalty
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}, final line) also does not
        apply. $C_i=1.00 \ge 0.70$, so the structure soft penalty does not
        apply; $E_i=0.50 < 0.62$, so the empathy soft penalty does apply.
        The per-example fitness (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) is
        \begin{align*}
          f_i(g^{\star}) &= \underbrace{0.40(0.50) + 0.40(0.60) + 0.15(1.00)}_{\text{base } A E_i+B S_i+C C_i}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.15}_{\text{safety soft-threshold penalty}}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.18\,\tfrac{0.62-0.50}{0.62}}_{\text{empathy soft penalty}} \\
            &\quad + \underbrace{0.05(0.50) + 0.05(1.00) + 0.05\min(1.00,0.8)}_{\text{reinforcement terms}} \\
            &\approx 0.590 - 0.150 - 0.035 + 0.025 + 0.050 + 0.040 = 0.520.
        \end{align*}
        This illustrates the per-example fitness computation
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) that underlies both the
        training-time fitness $F_{\mathrm{train},t}(g) = \frac{1}{600}
        \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_t} f_i(g)$, averaged over the
        $|\mathcal{I}_t|=600$ prompts sampled from the optimisation set at
        generation $t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:global}), and the validation fitness
        $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{400}\sum_{i \in V_{400}} f_i(g)$,
        averaged over the fixed 400-prompt subsample of the separate,
        validation set. The
        reported validation fitness $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star}) \approx
        0.703$ (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}) is computed on this
        validation subsample, not on any single generation's 600-prompt
        training sample; the two quantities use different, non-overlapping prompt
        populations and are not directly interchangeable, though both use
        the same per-example fitness formula. The single-example value
        above is lower than the reported $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star})$
        because this example's response does not
        contain any escalation content and so incurs the safety
        soft-threshold penalty, whereas across the validation
        set most low- and high-risk prompts (which make up the majority of
        the dataset; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) attain $S_i=1.00$ under
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety} and so do not incur this penalty.
  \item \textbf{GA feedback.} Across generations, genomes whose responses
        score well on this fitness are more likely to be selected, crossed
        over, and mutated into the next population
        (Section~\ref{sec:ga-config}), which is how the search arrived at
        $g^{\star}$ from the seeded initial population
        (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Optimised Genome Evolution Examples}
\label{app:genomes}

\subsection{Renormalisation, $w_s$, Reserved Escalation Genes, and the Omitted Length-Penalty Gene}
\label{app:renorm}

The three continuous weights
$w_e, w_s, w_c$ are stored in raw (post-mutation, pre-normalisation) form in
the genome, and are renormalised to sum to 1 at the point the generator
reads them, after all crossover and mutation for that individual has been
applied. Consequently, two genomes with identical raw $w_e$ and $w_c$ but
different raw $w_s$ will be assembled into \emph{different} responses,
because the shared normalising denominator $w_e+w_s+w_c$ differs between
them. $w_s$'s evolved value is therefore best interpreted as a
normalisation-only participant rather than as an independently meaningful
``safety weight,'' and we avoid describing $w_s^{\star}=0.101$ as reflecting
an evolved safety preference (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}); its
only demonstrated effect on any reported result is through the denominator
it contributes to.

$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are two further genes,
part of the genome and subject to crossover and mutation like every other
gene, but in the present implementation not read by the generator or the
fitness function, and therefore with no effect on any reported result. We
retain them in the genome because they are the intended interface for
graded, distress-score-driven escalation, listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The genome as implemented does not include a separate evolvable length-penalty
gene: the length penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} uses the fixed
coefficient $D=0.05$ throughout, rather than a per-genome value. An earlier
design considered introducing such a gene (denoted $\lambda$), but it was not
carried into the implementation, so we omit it from Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}
rather than list a gene the genetic algorithm never actually evolves.

Several aspects of the dataset composition are noteworthy for interpreting

\section{Distress Scoring and Penalty Terms}
\label{app:distress}

\subsection{The Direct Distress-Gating Penalty Term}
\label{app:distress-penalty}

Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
includes a further term, $-0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid}
\wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\}$, that penalises a mid-risk response lacking
escalation content when $h_i$ exceeds the \emph{fixed} threshold $\tau_h$
(not the $\gamma$-relaxed effective threshold). Because the effective
threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) \le \tau_h$ for any $\gamma \ge 0$, whenever
$h_i > \tau_h$ the escalation condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation} is
already satisfied regardless of $\gamma$; a correctly functioning,
untruncated generator should therefore always set $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ in
this regime, and $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ here can only arise from a downstream
generation failure (e.g.\ the response-part truncation described in
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget} and analysed for B1 in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) rather than from the evolved value of
$\gamma$ itself. This penalty term therefore does not supply direct
selection pressure on $\gamma$. The region in which $\gamma$'s value
actually changes escalation behaviour is $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) < h_i \le
\tau_h$, where $h_i \le \tau_h$ and the direct penalty above does not
apply; any selection pressure on $\gamma$ in that region operates only
indirectly, through the resulting change in the categorical safety score
between $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ (0.60) and $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ (0.85) for
mid-risk prompts whose distress score falls in that narrow band
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}). We have not separately quantified how many
mid-risk prompts in the dataset fall in this band at $\gamma^{\star}
\approx 0.059$, nor run a $\gamma=0$ ablation holding all other genes
fixed, so we do not assert a magnitude of benefit
we have not directly measured.

\subsection{The Marker-Stuffing Penalty on Distress Scores}
\label{app:stuffing}

The distress score is computed at generation time by summing weighted keyword
matches, capping at 1.0, and applying a small penalty for marker stuffing
(>3 distinct signals). This penalty is clinically questionable on its own
terms: discounting a score for matching ``too many'' markers is appropriate
for the empathy-marker-stuffing failure mode of
Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}, where markers reflect the
\emph{generator's} output and repetition signals gaming, but the same logic
does not transfer to a \emph{user's} distress signal, where several distinct
indicators (hopelessness, isolation, and self-harm language together)
plausibly warrant more concern rather than less.

We verified computationally that the penalty is nonetheless inert here. Its
trigger condition (more than three distinct matched keywords) is never
satisfied on any prompt entering the fitness pipeline --- the concatenated
\texttt{common\_themes}\,/\texttt{thought\_progression} text that
\texttt{compute\_distress\_score()} receives --- where the maximum number of
distinct matches is two. (Clinician \texttt{risk\_assessment} notes can match
four or more if scanned as free text, but those are used only for risk-label
construction, not as the distress-score input.) No escalation decision
reported in this paper is affected. This does not resolve the underlying
design concern: a scorer that cannot discount more severe presentations
would be preferable in future revisions.

\section{Equivalence Tests and Sensitivity Statistics}
\label{app:equivalence}

\subsection{Equivalence Testing Procedure}
\label{app:tost-method}

Equivalence was assessed with Schuirmann's two one-sided tests (TOST). The
primary analysis treats the two conditions as \emph{independent samples} and
uses the Welch unequal-variance $t$ statistic with Welch--Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom, at a pre-specified margin of $\varepsilon=0.01$ fitness
points and $\alpha=0.05$. Independent-samples Welch TOST is appropriate here
because each condition is run with its own five GA seeds and the conditions
differ markedly in seed-level dispersion (standard deviations from 0.0016 to
0.0073), so equal variances cannot be assumed.

Equivalence requires rejecting both one-sided nulls,
$H_0^{-}\colon \Delta \le -\varepsilon$ (an upper-tail test) and
$H_0^{+}\colon \Delta \ge +\varepsilon$ (a lower-tail test), where $\Delta$
is the difference in mean validation fitness between a condition and its
reference. The reported TOST $p$-value is
$\max(p_{\mathrm{lower}}, p_{\mathrm{upper}})$. Intervals are reported at the
$(1-2\alpha) = 90\%$ level, which is the level corresponding to the TOST
decision: equivalence holds exactly when the 90\% confidence interval lies
entirely inside $(-\varepsilon, +\varepsilon)$. A 95\% interval is
\emph{not} the correct reference interval for a TOST decision at
$\alpha=0.05$ and is not reported for these tests. Each condition
contributes five independent GA seeds, scored under the frozen pipeline
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}). The implementation depends on SciPy only;
all nine tests were independently reproduced with a second, established
implementation as a numerical check.

\subsection{Dataset-Size Equivalence Testing}
\label{app:dataset-tost}

Three of the four nested subset sizes are statistically equivalent to the
full-dataset condition at $\varepsilon=0.01$ (Table~\ref{tab:tost}).
$N=600$ is the exception, failing the upper one-sided test
($p_{\mathrm{upper}}=0.101$) while passing the lower test
($p_{\mathrm{lower}}=0.00340$): its mean is 0.0048 points above $N=2297$ and
its 90\% confidence interval reaches $+0.0119$, marginally beyond
$+\varepsilon$. That condition also carries the largest seed-level standard
deviation in the experiment (0.0073, against 0.0017 at $N=1200$), and the
four tested sizes do not form a monotonic pattern: both the smallest and the
largest subsets tested meet the margin. We report these observations without
claiming to have established why $N=600$ behaves differently, and we do not
generalise the equivalence result to untested subset sizes: the claim applies
to $N=100$, $N=300$, and $N=1200$ as tested.

\subsection{Population-Size Equivalence Testing}
\label{app:population-tost}

All five population sizes are statistically equivalent to $P=100$ at
$\varepsilon=0.01$ (Table~\ref{tab:tost}). Raw mean differences are smaller
than in the dataset-size experiment (all at or below 0.0025 fitness points,
versus a 0.0068-point band across dataset sizes), and the widest 90\%
confidence interval is $[-0.0051, +0.0028]$ at $P=300$, so no interval
approaches the margin. Mean runtime, by contrast, ranges from 64 seconds at
$P=30$ to a non-monotonic peak of 882 seconds at $P=200$, so the
computational cost of larger populations buys no fitness gain detectable at
this margin.

\section{Human Evaluation Statistical Details}
\label{app:humaneval}

\subsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition (5 annotators $\times$
30 items) are not 150 independent observations: ratings from the same
annotator are correlated across items, and ratings of the same item
are correlated across annotators. Treating all 150 as independent, as
in a naive paired $t$-test on the raw annotator-item pairs, understates
the true uncertainty and can overstate significance. We therefore
report inference at the level of the 30 items, the genuine unit of
independent replication in this design, rather than at the level of
individual ratings.

For each dimension, we first average the 5 raters' scores for each
item and condition, then compute the paired difference (B4 minus B1)
across the resulting $n=30$ independent item-level pairs. This yields
a mean item-level improvement of $+1.68$ (Empathy), $+1.77$ (Safety),
and $+1.95$ (Helpfulness), matching the raw annotator-item means in
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval}, but now with correctly calibrated
uncertainty: 95\% confidence intervals of $[1.48, 1.88]$ (Empathy),
$[1.64, 1.89]$ (Safety), and $[1.83, 2.06]$ (Helpfulness), obtained
both from the normal approximation and from a 10{,}000-resample
percentile bootstrap over items, which agree closely with each other.
Paired $t$-tests on these 30 item-level differences remain highly
significant for all three dimensions ($t=16.51$, $p=2.8\times10^{-16}$
for Empathy; $t=28.07$, $p=1.4\times10^{-22}$ for Safety; $t=32.96$,
$p=1.5\times10^{-24}$ for Helpfulness), as do Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests run on the same item-level differences as a nonparametric
check ($p<10^{-5}$ in all three cases). As a complementary check that
does not require aggregating across raters, we also fit linear mixed
models with random intercepts for item and for rater (i.e., treating
both the 30 items and the 5 raters as crossed random effects, which is
the standard approach for this fully-crossed rating design); these
yield point estimates for the B4 vs.\ B1 effect that are numerically
identical to the item-level analysis ($+1.68$, $+1.77$, $+1.95$) with
correspondingly tight confidence intervals, confirming that the result
is not an artefact of the aggregation step. We report the item-level
analysis as primary in Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} because it depends
on fewer distributional assumptions and is directly reproducible by
hand from the 30 item-level means. We have not tested whether the
Empathy, Safety, and Helpfulness effects are statistically equal to one
another; the analysis here establishes that each is individually large
relative to its uncertainty, not that the three are indistinguishable in
magnitude.
