\abstract{Mental-health conversational systems must balance empathy, risk-aware
safety, and actionable structure, yet optimising hand-designed proxies for these
qualities can expose reward-design failures. We present EvoRiri, a template-based
Bangla--Banglish--English mental-health assistant that uses no language model.
Its response policy is an eight-gene mixed continuous--discrete genome (empathy,
safety and structure weights, a distress-gating gene, two reserved escalation
thresholds, a template selector, and a response-part budget). A genetic algorithm
(population 100, 20 generations, tournament selection, uniform crossover; 2,000
evaluations per run) maximises a scalar fitness that weights rule-based empathy,
safety and structure scores (0.40/0.40/0.15) with per-example safety penalties.
On 2,872 de-identified records rendered as synthetic prompts, the evolved genome
raises structural completeness from 0.65 to 1.00 and fitness from 0.556 to 0.703
over a fixed reference genome, with comparable safety. A one-factor ablation
attributes this gain to a single structure-weight threshold, and over 30 paired
seeds genetic and equal-budget random search are equivalent on validation
prompts. Trace analysis exposed failures that aggregate fitness hid, including
question-only collapse, empathy-marker stuffing, and response truncation. The
contribution is a diagnostic case study of reward design on a synthetic
single-turn corpus, not an optimisation advantage or clinical validation.}

\section{Introduction}

Conversational agents, increasingly built on large language models (LLMs),
are now widely deployed for mental-health
support~\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots}. Contextual awareness allows these systems to respond to a user’s current emotional situation, suggest appropriate interventions, and facilitate empathic communication~\cite{jmir2024_empathic}. 
Moreover, safe deployment of these conversational systems requires risk-aware safeguards and evaluation.
Because generative chatbots may fail to recognise distress or respond appropriately to
mental-health crises~\cite{harvard2024_safety}, systems intended for
mental-health support should be developed and evaluated with risk-aware safety
protocols, expert-informed guardrails, and appropriate independent evaluation
\cite{apa2025_advisory}.
These systems are also unevenly distributed: emotion-aware AI is shaped
largely by western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic contexts,
sentiment tooling rarely extends beyond a handful of high-resource
languages, and low- and middle-income countries often lack the
infrastructure for AI-mediated support, a divide that risks a bifurcated
system benefiting the Global North~\cite{ijca2025emotion}. The present work
targets that gap directly: a Bangla/Banglish/English assistant that runs
without GPU infrastructure or API access. EvoRiri itself uses no language model: responses are assembled from templates, the evolved genome controls only their composition, and LLMs appear solely as zero-shot baselines (Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}).

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can be computationally demanding: it may require substantial GPU resources, large numbers of generated training samples and skilled 
human annotators~\cite{WongTan2024}. In contrast, genetic algorithms are population-based, derivative-free search
methods that can explore multiple regions of a solution space while mutation
helps maintain population diversity~\cite{Nath2026}. A growing literature
applies them in combination with
LLMs~\cite{mles2026,gendl2025}. Evolutionary methods can support black-box optimisation of language-model
inputs or configurations without requiring access to internal gradients
\cite{when_llms_meet_ea2025}. In the present work, we instead apply
evolutionary optimisation to a template-based response generator that does
not invoke an external language model at inference time.
Unlike prior evolutionary prompt optimisation approaches that focus primarily on
reasoning accuracy, feature activation, or prompt engineering, the proposed
framework focuses on optimising emotionally sensitive conversational behaviour
under strong safety penalty terms. The system introduces risk-aware escalation,
progressive structure enforcement, and trace-level behavioural analysis for a
multilingual mental-health assistant. To the best of our knowledge, this is
among the first studies applying interpretable genetic optimisation to
empathy--safety--structure balancing in Bangla mental-health conversational
systems.

In this article we study a template-based, risk-aware response generator for
Bangla-speaking users, whose parameters are searched with a genetic algorithm
(GA) and which we evaluate on synthetic prompts reconstructed from
de-identified records (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). The system composes responses via a structured, template-based
generator that controls when to include empathy, grounding, action
suggestions, and reflective questions, without calling an external LLM at
inference time (Section~\ref{sec:generator}). A genetic algorithm (GA) evolves
a genome of continuous and discrete parameters that govern this generator. The
optimisation objective aggregates scores for empathy, safety, and structure,
with safety considerations entering via strong per-example penalty terms for
suicide and self-harm risk. From a methodological perspective, the work studies a mixed
continuous--discrete, non-differentiable search space over an interpretable
policy, optimised against a single scalar (weighted-sum) fitness function.
Section~\ref{sec:landscape} states several properties of this search space
formally, and Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion} shows that the resulting
optimum is dominated by one component, structure, rather than balanced across
empathy, safety, and structure. The genetic algorithm itself is standard; the
study does not propose a new optimisation method.

Accordingly, this article should be read primarily as a diagnostic case study of
interpretable optimisation and reward design, rather than as evidence that
genetic search is uniquely superior to other sufficiently exploratory search
procedures in the present genome space.


The technical contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1. An interpretable response policy.} A mixed
    continuous--discrete genome (Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}) parametrises a
    template-based Bangla--Banglish--English generator
    (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:generator}, \eqref{eq:part-budget}, and
    \eqref{eq:risk-escalation}), so that every gene maps to a named response
    behaviour and the system runs without a GPU or a language model
    (Sections~\ref{sec:genome}--\ref{sec:generator} and
    Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

  \item \textbf{C2. A risk-aware scalar fitness.} A rule-based scorer for
    empathy, safety, and structure, combined into a per-example fitness with
    safety penalties and a distress-gated escalation term
    (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:empathy}--\eqref{eq:global}; Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}).

  \item \textbf{C3. Formal properties of the search problem.} Four
    propositions on neutral genes, a selection-invariant penalty, and the
    piecewise structure plateau, which explain why different search methods
    reach the same fitness region (Section~\ref{sec:landscape}).

  \item \textbf{C4. A language-model interface.} A shared prompt and scoring
    interface through which pre-trained language models are evaluated on the
    same records and with the same scorer, and a specified design for a
    genome-conditioned LLM generator (Section~\ref{sec:llm-interface}).

  \item \textbf{C5. A trace-based diagnostic method.} Trace analysis together
    with controlled diagnostics (a truncation diagnostic and a one-factor
    ablation) that expose four reward-design and generator failures concealed by
    aggregate fitness (Sections~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}
    and~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}).

  \item \textbf{C6. A reproducible evaluation protocol.} Seeded evaluation with
    common random numbers, 30 paired seeds, an equal-budget random-search
    comparison with equivalence testing, and robustness checks
    (Sections~\ref{sec:significance}, \ref{sec:interp-baseline},
    and~\ref{sec:ethics}).
\end{itemize}

The novelty of this work is therefore not a new optimisation method, nor
evidence that evolutionary search is needed to reach a given fitness level:
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline} shows that grid search and equal-budget random
search reach the same region, and that over 30 paired seeds random search is
equivalent to the GA. The novelty lies in three elements. First, an
interpretable genome, generator, and scorer in which every gene and score
component maps to a named response behaviour, for a low-resource
Bangla--Banglish--English setting that needs no GPU or language model. Second, a
trace-based diagnosis of four reward-design and generator failures
(question-only structural collapse, empathy-marker stuffing, a structure-marker
coverage gap, and a response-truncation defect) that aggregate fitness
concealed. Third, a formal and empirical account of why different search methods
behave identically here: a deterministic structure threshold that any
sufficiently exploratory search can locate
(Sections~\ref{sec:progressive-structure} and~\ref{sec:landscape}). Whether the
same holds in genome spaces without such a threshold is an open question that
the present study does not address.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the dataset, genome, generator, and fitness
function.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.


\subsection{Research Questions}

Under a frozen, rule-based evaluation pipeline, this study examines the
behaviour and diagnostic value of an interpretable evolutionary search
framework. The guiding research questions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How consistently do the reported GA-optimised
    configurations achieve higher validation fitness and component scores than
    fixed and randomly sampled reference configurations under the frozen
    scorer?

    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Which generator mechanisms, score components, and
    baseline-design choices account for the observed differences in structure,
    the safety proxy, and overall fitness?

    \item \textbf{RQ3:} How sensitive are the observed search outcomes to
    dataset size and population size under the present evaluation design?

    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Do GA, grid search, and equal-budget random search
    reach distinguishable fitness regions in the present genome space after
    re-evaluation of selected genomes?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Evolutionary Algorithms for LLM and Prompt Optimisation}

Recent work has explored the integration of Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) and
Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly for evolutionary fine-tuning and
LLM-enhanced evolutionary algorithms. Such approaches are relevant in
black-box settings, where direct access to model gradients or other internal
information is unavailable. The survey ``When Large Language Models Meet
Evolutionary Algorithms''~\cite{when_llms_meet_ea2025} draws \emph{conceptual}
parallels between text generation and evolutionary search, motivated by their
shared collective and directional characteristics. 

In contrast, we apply evolutionary optimisation to an interpretable,
template-based response-generation policy, without evolving textual prompts,
LLM parameters, or an external LLM at inference time. Each candidate policy is
represented by a mixed continuous--discrete genome containing generation-control
weights for empathic, safety-oriented, and structural response components,
together with escalation thresholds and template-configuration variables. These
evolved genes determine the composition of the generated response. They are
distinct from the per-response empathy, safety, and structure scores used for
evaluation, as well as from the fixed scalarisation coefficients used to
aggregate these scores into the fitness function.

A related approach in spirit is Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (MLES)~\cite{mles2026}, which incorporates visual feedback-driven behavioural-level analysis of
candidate programmatic control policies within the evolutionary loop. MLES uses
this evidence to identify failure patterns and guide targeted LLM-mediated
policy revisions while quantitative metrics remain the objective for selection
and convergence.
Our framework adopts a related diagnostic perspective, but applies it
post hoc rather than as an in-loop search mechanism. Specifically, the
trace-level analysis in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck} examines generated
responses to reveal failure modes that are not apparent from aggregate fitness
alone, including structural collapse and empathy overfitting. These observations
then motivate revisions to the scoring design and safety penalties.

Furthermore, GenDLN~\cite{gendl2025} applies genetic algorithm to jointly optimise
prompt pairs in a stacked-LLM architecture. It is designed to
leverage commercial API free tiers and, in the authors' experiments, enabled resource-limited teams to conduct
EA-based prompt optimisation at no API cost. This focus on deployment cost is
relevant to our resource-constrained setting.

GAAPO~\cite{gaapo2025} explicitly studies evaluation-cost trade-offs in
evolutionary prompt optimisation. It compares complete evaluation, successive-halving,
and bandit selection, noting that subset-based evaluation can
substantially reduce API calls but may prematurely eliminate promising
candidates because of variability in subset-based scores. It also varies
population size and generation count under a comparable total LLM-call budget.

In contrast to GenDLN and GAAPO, which evaluate prompt optimisation on
labelled classification, paraphrase-detection, and question-answering-style
benchmarks, our objective evaluates open-ended response quality through
task-specific proxy measures of empathy, safety, and structural support. Although risk labels inform safety evaluation,
these dimensions do not admit a single externally verifiable ground-truth
response. Consequently, shortcomings in proxy-based fitness design may be
difficult to identify from aggregate fitness alone.


\subsection{Mental-Health Conversational Agents}

Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials suggest that mental-health
chatbot interventions may yield small-to-moderate overall reductions in
mental-health symptoms, particularly depressive symptoms, although effects
vary across systems, outcomes, and populations
\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots}. These findings motivate the
investigation of chatbots as scalable, complementary sources of support in
settings where access to professional mental-health care is limited, which is
the deployment context targeted by this work. They do not establish chatbots
as replacements for clinical care.

A systematic review of empathic conversational-agent design
\cite{jmir2024_empathic} surveys architectures for detecting and responding
to user emotion, together with approaches for evaluating their technical
performance and acceptability to users. Empathy is therefore included as one
of the response-quality dimensions in our fitness function. The remaining
dimensions, conversational structure and escalation safety, were informed by
consultation with five collaborating clinical psychologists
(Section~\ref{sec:fitness-rationale}).

Two further findings shape the design directly. A preliminary comparison of
LLM-generated responses with certified-counselor responses, using manual
qualitative analysis and cosine similarity, found that GPT-4 and Mistral could
produce syntactically correct, coherent, and professionally toned responses,
but often did not capture the emotional nuance, personalization, and
dialogue-oriented engagement present in counselor responses
\cite{humanai2024}. This motivates scoring empathy separately rather than
folding it into a single response-quality measure.

Earlier mental-health chatbots such as Woebot established in a randomized trial
that a CBT-oriented automated conversational agent significantly reduced
depressive symptoms relative to an information-only control
\cite{woebot2017}. Woebot used a decision-tree architecture with suggested
responses whose structure and parameters remained fixed for each participant
during the study; related reviews likewise characterise it as using rule-based
CBT modules~\cite{ijca2025emotion}. In contrast, the present framework exposes
response-composition behaviour as an evolvable, inspectable parameter set.

We operationalize empathy through an interpretable marker-based proxy rather
than a learned affect-recognition model. This choice supports transparent
fitness evaluation but limits construct validity, particularly for contextual,
implicit, sarcastic, or culturally specific expressions
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

on interpretable optimisation frameworks that explicitly and jointly evaluate
empathy, response structure, safety, and risk-sensitive escalation for
multilingual mental-health support.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let \(X\) denote the space of user prompts and
\(R = \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}\) the three
risk/support-intensity categories used by the system. These categories
combine PHQ-4 severity with an explicit self-harm keyword override
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) and are not presented as clinically
validated suicide-risk classifications. A response-generation policy is a function
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{\theta} : X \times R \rightarrow Y,
\end{equation}
mapping a prompt \(x \in X\) with risk \(r \in R\) to a textual response
\(y \in Y\). Instead of optimising LLM parameters directly, we parameterise
\(\pi_{\theta}\) via a higher-level generator \(G\) controlled by a genome
\(g \in \mathcal{G}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generator}
  y = G(x, r; g).
\end{equation}
The design goal is to find a genome \(g^{\star}\) that maximises a scalar
fitness function \(F(g)\) combining empathy, safety, and structure, with
safety considerations entering as penalty terms within $F(g)$ itself
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) rather than as constraints that
render a genome infeasible:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
  g^{\star} = \arg\max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} F(g).
\end{equation}
We approximate~\eqref{eq:objective} using a GA operating on a finite set of
prompts.

\subsection{Dataset}

We use a dataset of \(N = 2872\) de-identified records collected from Relaxy PTE. LTD.,
a mental health technology company operating in Bangladesh. Each record
originates as a set of structured, analyst-labelled fields --
\texttt{common\_themes} (e.g., ``loneliness $\vert$ desire for romantic
relationship $\vert$ emotional distress'') and \texttt{thought\_progression}
(e.g., ``initial greeting $\vert$ expressed loneliness and prolonged duration $\vert$ desire for a girlfriend'') -- rather than as a raw, free-text user message. These structured fields are concatenated into an intermediate theme-text string, which is then passed through \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}, a function that uses keyword matching against the theme text to select and assemble multilingual (Bangla/Banglish/English) sentences into a synthetic, natural-language prompt (e.g., ``I feel really alone these days,'' ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi lately,'' or the corresponding Bangla-script sentence), adding closing phrases with some stochastic variation. 
Writing $u_i$ for the theme-text string and $\xi_i$ for these random phrase
choices, the prompt is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prompt-construction}
  x_i = B(u_i;\,\xi_i),
\end{equation}
where $B$ denotes \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}.
The resulting synthetic text is what we denote $x_i$. The template generator
(B1--B5) selects its opening line and computes the distress score from the
underlying theme-text string, while the pre-trained-LLM baselines (B6--B8,
Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}) receive $x_i$ itself; all conditions
therefore see the same records, in the form each system requires:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:record}
  (x_i, r_i, t_i),
\end{equation}
where \(x_i\) is this synthetically constructed prompt in Bangla, Banglish,
or English;
\(r_i \in R\) is the risk label (low, mid, high), assigned from PHQ-4 score
and an explicit self-harm keyword override applied to a separate clinician
risk-assessment note (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) -- not from $x_i$'s
content; and \(t_i\) is a high-level
theme description (e.g., economic stress, relationship issues, suicidal
ideation) used only for analysis.

Because every prompt in this dataset is a synthetic reconstruction from
structured summary fields rather than a verbatim user message, we do not
claim ecological validity for the specific phrasing, code-switching
patterns, or conversational style of $x_i$: the multilingual variation
reflects the phrase banks used by \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}
rather than authentic Bangla-English code-switching as it naturally occurs
in user speech. What is preserved from the original clinical population is
the distribution of themes, risk levels, and demographic composition
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}), not the literal text users originally wrote.
The distress score $h_i$ used for mid-risk escalation
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) is computed by keyword matching on the
intermediate theme-text string, not on the synthetic prompt $x_i$, so it
does not depend on the phrases selected by
\texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}. The theme fields were written by
analysts and may nonetheless correlate with the information used to assign
$r_i$, so $h_i$ is not treated as a fully independent signal; in practice it
affects escalation for only one validation prompt
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-safety}).

The corpus is partitioned once into an optimisation set and a validation set
using an 80/20 split of the $N = 2{,}872$ usable prompts, applied after a
single random shuffle at seed 7. This yields exactly $2{,}297$ optimisation
prompts and $575$ validation prompts. The partition is a simple random split
and is not stratified by risk label. The validation set is disjoint from the
optimisation set, but was also used during scorer development
(Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}); we therefore describe it as a combined
development and validation split rather than as an untouched final test set.
During GA optimisation, fitness is evaluated on a random sample of
$|\mathcal{I}| = 600$ prompts drawn from the optimisation set once per run and
held fixed across generations (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}).
Each row in the underlying dataset
corresponds to a distinct user: the raw collection of $N=3{,}279$ rows
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) has $3{,}279$ unique user identifiers with no
user contributing more than one row, and this one-row-per-user property is
preserved after filtering to the $N=2{,}872$ usable prompts used throughout
this work. The 80/20 split therefore partitions users rather than only
prompts: no user appears in both the optimisation and validation sets under
this split, and no user-level leakage is possible.

To study data efficiency we vary the size of the optimisation set only, using
nested subsets $N_s \in \{100, 300, 600, 1200, 2297\}$. The $2{,}297$
optimisation prompts are shuffled once at seed 42 and each subset is taken as
a prefix of that ordering, so every smaller subset is contained in each larger
one. The validation set is held fixed at the same $575$ prompts for every
condition, with final genomes scored on a common $400$-prompt sample of it, so
that differences between conditions reflect the amount of optimisation data
rather than a change in the evaluation population. For subsets of $600$
prompts or fewer the per-run evaluation sample is the entire subset.

\subsubsection{Risk-Label Construction}
\label{sec:risk-labels}

Each prompt's risk label $r_i \in \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}$
is derived from two signals: the prompt's PHQ-4 total score and the presence
of explicit self-harm/suicide language in an accompanying clinician-authored
risk-assessment note recorded at the point of data collection. The PHQ-4 is
a four-item screening instrument for depression and anxiety
severity~\cite{phq4}, with standard severity bands of Normal (0--2), Mild
(3--5), Moderate (6--8), and Severe (9--12) (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}). We use its score only as one input to a study-specific support-intensity label.
Two qualifications apply to our use of it. First, PHQ-4 contains no suicide
or self-harm item, so it is not a validated suicide-risk instrument on its
own; we use its severity score as one input to a risk label rather than as
a direct measure of suicide risk. Second, the original PHQ Anxiety Study enrolled patients from 15 US primary-care sites;
the 2,149 participants used for the GAD-7 development and validation also
constituted the sample analysed in the PHQ-4 study~\cite{phq4}. A Bangla-language
validation has since been published: in 357 adults sampled from one urban
and two rural Bangladeshi communities, the Bangla PHQ-4 reproduced the
two-factor structure with excellent fit, showed high internal consistency
($\omega = 0.869$) and strong construct validity, and achieved good
diagnostic accuracy (AUC $= 0.906$) against clinician
judgement~\cite{banglaphq4_2026}. That study supports the instrument's
psychometric properties in Bangla, though its sample is a general community
population rather than a self-referring digital-support population, and a cutoff score of 6 yielded sensitivity
of 82\% and specificity of 83\%, although the investigators note that other
cutoff-selection methods favoured 7~\cite{banglaphq4_2026}. This community
validation supports the psychometric use of the PHQ-4 in Bangla, but it does
not directly establish validity for a self-referring digital-support population
or validate the four-band severity stratification used here
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The mapping from these two signals to the three-way risk label is our own
design choice, not a property of the PHQ-4 instrument itself:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-label}
  r_i =
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{high} & \text{explicit risk indicator present, or PHQ-4 total} \ge 9 \\
    \mathrm{mid}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \in [6, 8] \\
    \mathrm{low}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \le 5
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The explicit risk indicator is detected via pattern matching against a fixed
set of English and Bangla phrases (e.g., ``suicide,'' ``kill myself,''
``hurt myself,'' ``end my life,'' ``unsafe,'' {\bengalifont আত্মহত্যা}) applied
to the risk-assessment note; a match escalates the label to \emph{high}
regardless of PHQ-4 score, since an explicit indicator is treated as taking
priority over screening-instrument severity. This escalation rule, and the
decision to retain explicit keyword detection as a parallel signal alongside
PHQ-4 severity, was proposed by the five clinical psychologists who later
served as evaluators in our human evaluation study
(Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}); the rationale given was that a PHQ-4 score
alone can understate acute risk in cases where a user discloses suicidal or
self-harm intent without this being reflected in elevated depression/anxiety
severity on the four PHQ-4 items.

The score-to-label mapping in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-label} and the keyword
list have not been formally validated against clinician-assigned labels on
a held-out sample, and the keyword override cannot handle negation,
hypothetical phrasing, or context-dependent meaning; we do not present the
resulting risk labels as equivalent in rigor to a clinician-assigned
ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Genome Representation}
\label{sec:genome}

An individual genome \(g\) is a vector of continuous and discrete genes,
matching the fields of the implementation's \texttt{Genome} data
structure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:genome}
  g = \bigl( w_e,\, w_s,\, w_c,\, \theta_{\mathrm{mid}},\,
             \theta_{\mathrm{high}},\, \gamma,\, d,\, m \bigr),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_e \in [0,1]\): empathy weight controlling the number and intensity
        of empathy lines.
  \item \(w_s \in [0,1]\): safety weight. It does not affect the visible response or any score
  directly; it participates only in the joint renormalisation of
  $w_e,w_s,w_c$ and so indirectly changes the effective $w_e$ and $w_c$
  (Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}). Crisis-language inclusion is governed by the
  risk label (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}).
  \item \(w_c \in [0,1]\): structure weight controlling inclusion of grounding,
        action, and questions.
  \item \(\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}, \theta_{\mathrm{high}} \in [0,1]\): two
        escalation-threshold genes, currently inactive and reserved for
        future graded escalation; see Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}
        for details.
  \item \(\gamma \in [0, 0.2]\): distress-gating parameter. Unlike
        $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, $\gamma$ is
        read directly by the generation pipeline: it
        sets the effective distress threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma)$ used to
        decide mid-risk escalation (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation},
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation}), and is therefore an active,
        functional gene rather than a reserved one. Its relationship to the
        fitness function's direct distress-gating penalty term is more
        limited than it may first appear; see the caveat in
        Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}.
  \item \(d\): discrete template selector (implemented as the field
        \texttt{p\_id}), indexing a small set of response templates.
  \item \(m \in \{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\): response-part budget (implemented as
        the integer field \texttt{memory\_window}). Despite the
        implementation name, $m$ is \emph{not} a conversational context
        window: the present generator is single-turn and does not retain
        dialogue history. Instead, $m$ is a categorical gene that gates a
        binary output-component cap after response assembly
        (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}). Only the threshold
        $m \le 256$ is behaviourally active; the three larger values are
        currently equivalent (no truncation).
\end{itemize}

To establish a meaningful performance reference, we define three baseline
conditions. The minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) instantiates the generator with
minimum empathy and structure weights and a safety weight absorbing the
remainder ($w_e = 0.10$, $w_c = 0.10$, $w_s = 0.80$; these already sum to
1, so genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged and $w_c$ genuinely
remains in the bottom, no-structure regime, Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
a short
response-part budget $m = 256$ (truncating assembly to four parts; see
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}), and maximally conservative escalation thresholds
($\theta_\mathrm{mid} = 0.70$, $\theta_\mathrm{high} = 0.95$), representing
response generation with no parameter optimisation. Because B1 also triggers a truncation defect (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}), B2 is the more informative non-GA comparator.
The fixed seed baseline B2 uses the balanced initialisation genome
$g_{\mathrm{bal}}$ without evolutionary search, providing a fixed non-GA
reference condition.
The random baseline (B3) averages five genomes sampled uniformly from the
genome space, establishing a chance-level reference.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Generator}
\label{sec:generator}

The generator \(G\) composes responses from several component banks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Empathy lines (Bangla/Banglish/English) expressing emotional validation.
  \item Grounding instructions (e.g., breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 sensory exercise).
  \item Action suggestions (e.g., contact trusted friend, seek professional
        help).
  \item Reflective questions encouraging further disclosure.
\end{itemize}

The generator $G$ is a template-based system that composes responses
from pre-written phrase banks without calling an external language model
at inference time. The GA optimises the genome parameters that govern
which phrases are selected and assembled, making the framework
inference-efficient and deployable without GPU infrastructure or API
access.

\subsubsection{Response-Part Budget and Truncation}
\label{sec:part-budget}

The generator assembles each reply as an ordered list of \emph{parts}
(strings), typically beginning with two internal tags
(\texttt{[TEMPLATE]}, \texttt{[SAFETY]}), followed by one or more empathy
lines, optional escalation content, and optional structure lines
(grounding, action, question). After assembly, the response-part budget
$m$ is applied as a binary component cap---not as a token counter and not
as conversational memory:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:part-budget}
  \mathrm{parts} \leftarrow
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{parts}[:4] & \text{if } m \le 256 \text{ and } |\mathrm{parts}| > 4, \\
    \mathrm{parts} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Internal tags are then stripped to produce the visible response. When
$m \le 256$, the two tags occupy the first two of the four retained slots,
leaving at most two visible content lines; later parts (including
escalation and structure) can therefore be dropped before scoring. Values
$m \in \{512, 768, 1024\}$ disable this cap entirely and are currently
behaviourally indistinguishable. The gene remains evolvable because B1 is
initialised at $m=256$ while seed and GA genomes typically use
$m \ge 512$, and the resulting truncation interaction is a material driver
of the B1--B4 safety gap (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). We retain the
implementation field name \texttt{memory\_window} for code compatibility,
but refer to $m$ as a response-part budget throughout this manuscript to
avoid implying multi-turn context retention.

\subsubsection{Progressive Structure Enforcement}
\label{sec:progressive-structure}

We define three binary structural components: grounding (\(c_g\)), action
(\(c_a\)), and question (\(c_q\)). Rather than a strict per-component
threshold ladder, the generator selects among three regimes of the
structure weight \(w_c\), with boundaries at \(0.25\) and \(0.55\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_c < 0.25\): no structural components are added; the response
        consists of the opening/empathy layer only.
  \item \(0.25 \le w_c < 0.55\): components are inserted stochastically,
        so the number and identity of components vary across responses
        generated from the same genome. Fewer than three are typical, but
        all three can occur, particularly toward the lower end of this
        range (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}).
  \item \(w_c \ge 0.55\): all three components are included deterministically,
        i.e.\ \(c_g = c_a = c_q = 1\).
\end{itemize}
Formally, the set of structural components included in a response is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:structure-regime}
  \mathcal{S}_i(w_c) =
  \begin{cases}
    \varnothing, & w_c < 0.25,\\
    Q_i \subseteq \{c_g, c_a, c_q\}, & 0.25 \le w_c < 0.55,\\
    \{c_g, c_a, c_q\}, & w_c \ge 0.55,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $Q_i$ is drawn stochastically for each response as described above.
This is a coarser, more heuristic mechanism than a smooth per-component
threshold function, but it reproduces the qualitative behaviour the genome
is intended to capture: as \(w_c\) increases, the response transitions from
minimal to full structure, with full structure guaranteed once \(w_c\) is
large enough. The reported genome \(w_c^{\star} = 0.768\)
(Section~\ref{sec:hyperparams}) falls in the top regime, which is why the
worked example above activates all three structural components
deterministically rather than probabilistically.

\subsubsection{Risk-Aware Escalation}
\label{sec:risk-escalation}

Crisis-style escalation content is included if the prompt's risk label is high,
\textit{or} if a continuous distress score $h(x_i)$ exceeds a learned threshold
and the risk label is mid:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-escalation}
    \text{escalate}(x_i, r_i, h_i) = \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{high} \} \vee
    \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{mid} \land h_i > \tau_h - \gamma \cdot h_i \},
\end{equation}

where $h_i \in [0,1]$ is a distress score computed from keywords in the record's theme text (Section~\ref{sec:methods})

(suicide ideation, self-harm, hopelessness, isolation, severe overwhelm),
$\tau_h = 0.65$ is the distress threshold, and $\gamma \in [0, 0.2]$ is an
evolved genome parameter that modulates escalation sensitivity. Rearranging,
escalation for a mid-risk prompt requires $h_i > \tau_h / (1+\gamma)$:
increasing $\gamma$ lowers the effective distress threshold below $\tau_h$,
allowing earlier escalation to crisis language without changing the formal
risk label.

The distress score is a capped, weighted count of distress markers in the
theme text $u_i$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:distress}
  h_i = \max\Bigl\{0,\; \min\Bigl(1,\, \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \omega_k\,
        \mathbb{I}\{\kappa_k \sqsubseteq u_i\}\Bigr) - 0.05\,\mathbb{I}\{n_i > 3\}\Bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}$ is a fixed lexicon of 32 Bangla and English markers
$\kappa_k$ (suicide ideation, self-harm, hopelessness, isolation, severe
overwhelm) with severity weights $\omega_k \in [0.60, 1.0]$,
$\kappa_k \sqsubseteq u_i$ denotes a case-insensitive substring match, and
$n_i = \sum_{k} \mathbb{I}\{\kappa_k \sqsubseteq u_i\}$ is the number of
matched markers; the 0.05 reduction discourages marker stuffing.

Escalation here \emph{augments} the response rather than terminating it: the
generator appends crisis guidance to a complete empathetic, structured reply
rather than issuing a refusal or halting the exchange. This is a deliberate
design choice. Recent work characterises hard refusal as a common safety break
in current models and argues that the manner of disengagement may itself have
psychological consequences. Drawing on psychotherapy and crisis-line evidence,
it notes that premature or imposed termination is associated with poorer
outcomes and that unplanned disconnection can interrupt de-escalation and
aftercare~\cite{journey2026}. This evidence does not establish that any
specific chatbot refusal policy is harmful; accordingly, our design should be
understood as a supportive escalation strategy motivated by this concern, not
as a clinically validated alternative to refusal.

A further fitness term penalises mid-risk responses without escalation when
$h_i>\tau_h$, but it exerts no direct pressure on $\gamma$, since escalation is
already guaranteed above that threshold (Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty}).
High-risk prompts always receive crisis escalation and low-risk prompts never
do. The evolved $\gamma^{\star}\approx0.058$ is only weakly identified and
changes escalation for a single validation prompt
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-safety}), so we give it no behavioural interpretation.

\subsection{Automatic Scoring}
\label{sec:automatic-scoring}

Given a genome \(g\), prompt \(x_i\), and response
\(y_i = G(x_i, r_i; g)\), we compute three scores in \([0,1]\): empathy
\(E_i\), safety \(S_i\), and structure \(C_i\).

\subsubsection{Empathy Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:empathy}
  E_i = \min\!\left( 1,\;
    \frac{1}{K_e} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_e} \mathbb{I}\{ m \in y_i \}
  \right) - \beta R_i^{\mathrm{rep}},
\end{equation}
where \(K_e\) is the target number of distinct markers, \(R_i^{\mathrm{rep}}\)
is a repetition ratio, and \(\beta\) is a small penalty coefficient.
In practice, empathy markers are grouped into four categories
(validation, reflection, normalisation, and warmth) with
category-specific weights of 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, and 0.15
respectively, summing to a maximum of 0.90 before length bonuses.

\subsubsection{Safety Score}

Rather than a continuous decomposition into risk-appropriate-guidance and
unsafe-content components, the implemented safety score is a categorical
lookup keyed on the risk label and a single boolean, \(\text{esc}_i\),
indicating whether the response contains at least one escalation-language
marker:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:safety}
  S_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.85 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.60 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.75 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 1
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These are the same values reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail};
there is no separate continuous \(S_i^{+}\)/\(S_i^{-}\) decomposition, and no
\(\gamma\) or \(\eta\) parameter, in the implemented scoring function.

The mid-risk safety score of approximately 0.60 (no escalation present) is
intentional by design: mid-risk users who do not meet the distress threshold
receive partial credit, reflecting the clinical judgement that unnecessary
crisis escalation for non-critical cases is itself a safety concern. We note
that this design means \emph{every} mid-risk response is capped below the
0.94 safety soft-threshold (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail}) and
so incurs the associated $-0.15$ penalty regardless of response quality,
since 0.85 (the best attainable mid-risk score) is itself below 0.94; the
soft threshold as currently set cannot distinguish an acceptable from an
excellent mid-risk response (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection{Structure Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:structure}
  C_i =
  \begin{cases}
    0.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 0 \\
    0.20 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 1 \\
    0.60 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 2 \\
    1.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 3
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is a discrete lookup on the number of structural components detected in
the response text (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), rather than a
continuous average. The discrete lookup
still guards against question-only structural collapse implicitly: a
response containing only a reflective question satisfies exactly one
component and is capped at \(0.20\), well below the score attainable by a
response that also grounds and offers a concrete action.

\subsubsection{Length Penalty and Per-Example Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:per-example}
  L_i = \max\!\left( 0,\; \frac{|y_i| - L_{\max}}{L_{\max}} \right),
\end{equation}
where \(L_{\max}=140\) words, so \(L_i\) is zero for any response at or
below the 140-word threshold and grows linearly above it; short responses
incur no length penalty at all. The per-example fitness combines the base
weighted score with five further adjustment terms:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:per-example-fitness}
    f_i(g) = {}& A\,E_i + B\,S_i + C\,C_i - D\,L_i \notag\\
      & - 0.50\,\mathbb{I}\{r_i=\mathrm{high} \wedge S_i<1.0\}
        - 0.15\,\mathbb{I}\{S_i<0.94\} \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.70-C_i)}{0.70}
        - 0.18\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.62-E_i)}{0.62} \notag\\
      & + 0.05\,E_i + 0.05\,C_i + 0.05\,\min(C_i,\,0.8) \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid} \wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\},
\end{align}

where $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are the fixed fitness-weight coefficients reported in
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring} ($A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$).
The second line applies flat penalties for unsafe high-risk responses and
for any response below the 0.94 safety threshold (both can apply to the
same example, e.g.\ a high-risk response without escalation incurs a
combined \(-0.65\)). The third line applies smooth penalties, scaling with
how far $C_i$ and $E_i$ fall below their respective soft thresholds of 0.70
and 0.62. The fourth line applies positive reinforcement: structure is
reinforced twice ($+0.05\,C_i$ and $+0.05\,\min(C_i,0.8)$) while empathy is
reinforced once ($+0.05\,E_i$) and safety receives no reinforcement beyond
its base term. This asymmetry is a deliberate design choice reflected in the
implementation rather than an oversight, and we return to its consequences
for the converged genome in Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}. The final
line is the direct distress-gating penalty discussed critically above
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}), which we now describe only as an
additional (largely inactive, per that discussion) safety-adjacent penalty
rather than as a mechanism that directly selects for $\gamma$.

\subsubsection{Fitness Weight Rationale}
\label{sec:fitness-rationale}

The fixed coefficients $A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
were determined via manual inspection on a held-out development set (100 prompts, stratified by
risk level) prior to GA initialisation, reflecting clinical priorities identified with the five
collaborating psychologists: empathy and safety are weighted equally and most heavily ($A=B=0.40$), since clinical
feedback indicated both are foundational and supportive responses lacking safety can cause harm;
structure receives a smaller weight ($C=0.15$) as a supporting rather than primary
role; and the length penalty is minimal ($D=0.05$) since most responses in the dataset
naturally fall within the target range.

\subsubsection{Global Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:global}
  F(g) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} f_i(g).
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:global} is used with two distinct evaluation sets
$\mathcal{I}$ throughout this manuscript, and we distinguish them
explicitly to avoid conflating them: training-time fitness
$F_{\mathrm{train}}(g) = \frac{1}{600}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{train}}} f_i(g)$
is computed over a random $|\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{train}}|=600$
sample drawn once per run from the optimisation set, held fixed across generations, and is what the GA uses as its selection signal; validation fitness
$F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{400}\sum_{i \in V_{400}} f_i(g)$ is
computed once, after search, over a fixed 400-prompt subsample $V_{400}$
(drawn at seed 7) of the disjoint 575-prompt validation set $V$,
and is what Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and
the significance analysis (Table~\ref{tab:significance}) report. The two
use the same per-example fitness formula but different, non-overlapping
prompt populations, and are not directly interchangeable. Note that all penalty and reinforcement terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
are applied per example, before averaging. There is no separate global
override that eliminates a genome outright based on any single unsafe
example; a genome whose responses are frequently unsafe is instead driven
toward low mean fitness by the accumulation of per-example penalties across
the evaluation sample, which has a similar aggregate effect to a hard
constraint without being implemented as one.

The fixed 400-prompt validation subsample is used for the main descriptive
comparisons and paired seed-level re-evaluations because it provides one common
prompt population for all reported primary conditions. The age-record robustness check in
Section~\ref{sec:ethics} uses the corresponding 400-prompt subsample of the
validation set with those records removed.

\emph{Evaluation noise.} Because the generator samples from its phrase
banks at generation time without a fixed random seed, repeated evaluation
of the same genome on the same prompts is not deterministic. The five-seed re-scores in Table~\ref{tab:significance}, the 30-seed analyses in
Sections~\ref{sec:significance} and~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}, and the age-record
check in Section~\ref{sec:ethics} seed the generator with the run seed, using the
same seed for every condition compared, so these evaluations are deterministic;
the remaining single evaluations were not seeded. Ten repeated
evaluations of the reported genome on the fixed 400-prompt subsample span
0.6966--0.7041 (mean 0.7002, SD 0.0027); a second, independent set of ten
repeats (seeds 1000--1009, used in the B2--B4 ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) spans 0.6955--0.7126 (mean 0.7027,
SD 0.0051). Both vary only the generation draw, since the prompt subsample is
fixed. Each standard deviation is estimated from ten repeats and the two are
consistent with a common value of about 0.004 (pooled). All fitness values
reported in this manuscript are single evaluations and therefore carry a
single-evaluation standard deviation of roughly 0.003--0.005. Differences between
two reported values smaller than roughly 0.01 should not be treated as
resolvable. We return to the consequences of this noise floor for the
GA-versus-random-search comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}.


\subsubsection{Frozen Scoring and Reporting Protocol}
\label{sec:frozen-scorer}

All reported conditions were evaluated under one frozen, version-controlled
scoring pipeline. Here, ``frozen'' means that the scorer implementation,
marker lists, fitness formula, and evaluation procedure were held fixed for
the final reported analyses.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm Configuration}
\label{sec:ga-config}

We employ a mixed continuous/discrete GA with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Population size
        \(P \in \{30, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400\}\).
  \item Generations $T = 20$.
  \item Tournament selection of size~3.
  \item Uniform crossover for every gene: each child gene is copied from
        parent~$a$ or parent~$b$ with equal probability, including continuous
        weights and the discrete genes \(d\) and \(m\).
  \item Per-gene mutation with probability $p_m = 0.30$. Continuous genes
        ($w_e$, $w_s$, $w_c$, $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$,
        $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, $\gamma$) receive additive uniform noise
        clipped to their feasible ranges. Discrete \(d\) is resampled from
        \(\{0,1,2\}\). The response-part budget \(m\) is resampled
        categorically from \(\{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\) (integer-coded, but
        not mutated as a continuous integer).
  \item Elitism: the top \(E\) individuals are copied to the next generation.
\end{itemize}
Formally, one generation maps the population $\mathcal{P}_t$ to
$\mathcal{P}_{t+1}$ as follows. Each parent is chosen by tournament,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ga-select}
  \mathrm{Sel}(\mathcal{P}_t) = \arg\max_{g \in K} F(g),
  \qquad K \subset \mathcal{P}_t \text{ drawn uniformly}, \ |K| = 3.
\end{equation}
Two parents $a$ and $b$ produce a child $c$ by uniform crossover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ga-cross}
  c_\ell =
  \begin{cases}
    a_\ell & \text{with probability } \tfrac12,\\
    b_\ell & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and each gene $\ell$ of the child is then mutated with probability $p_m$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ga-vary}
  c'_\ell =
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{clip}_{[L_\ell, U_\ell]}(c_\ell + \epsilon_\ell),
      & \ell \text{ continuous},\\
    \text{uniform draw from the domain of } \ell, & \ell \in \{d, m\},
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \epsilon_\ell \sim \mathcal{U}(-\delta_\ell, \delta_\ell),
\end{equation}
with step sizes $\delta_\ell = 0.25$ for $w_e, w_s, w_c$, $0.06$ for
$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}, \theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, and $0.05$ for $\gamma$, and
ranges $[L_\ell, U_\ell]$ as in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring} (with
$[0, 1]$ for the weights before renormalisation). After crossover and after
mutation, the weights are floored and renormalised,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ga-normalise}
  w_k \leftarrow \frac{\max(w_k, 0.10)}{\sum_{j \in \{e, s, c\}} \max(w_j, 0.10)},
  \qquad k \in \{e, s, c\},
\end{equation}
and the next population keeps the $E$ fittest genomes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ga-update}
  \mathcal{P}_{t+1} = \mathrm{Top}_E(\mathcal{P}_t) \,\cup\,
  \bigl\{\mathrm{Mut}\bigl(\mathrm{Cross}(\mathrm{Sel}(\mathcal{P}_t), \mathrm{Sel}(\mathcal{P}_t))\bigr)\bigr\}_{P - E}.
\end{equation}
Five independent runs with different random seeds are performed per setting;
we report mean and standard deviation of validation fitness, with
per-generation best-individual genomes logged and archived alongside
fitness (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).

\subsection{Seeded Initialisation}

Instead of random initialisation, we use four seed genomes:
\begin{align}
  g_{\mathrm{bal}}  &: \text{balanced empathy, safety, and structure,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{emp}}  &: \text{empathy-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{str}}  &: \text{structure-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{safe}} &: \text{safety-heavy.}
\end{align}
The remaining $P - 4$ genomes of the initial population are drawn uniformly
at random from the search space (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring}).

\label{sec:hyperparams}

and~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga} report all
fixed hyperparameters used in the experiments. Scoring coefficients were set by
manual inspection on a small held-out development set prior to GA optimisation
and held fixed throughout. The fitness function weights empathy and safety
equally at 0.40 each, with smaller contributions from structure (0.15) and a

\subsection{Properties of the Search Space and Fitness Function}
\label{sec:landscape}

This subsection states four properties of the search problem that follow
directly from the generator and fitness definitions above. They are not
convergence results; their purpose is to make explicit the structure of the
landscape that the empirical comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}
later reflect. Throughout, $\mathcal{I}$ is a fixed evaluation set and
$\tilde w_k = w_k/(w_e+w_s+w_c)$, $k\in\{e,s,c\}$, denotes a renormalised weight.

\begin{Proposition}[Neutral genes]\label{prop:neutral}
$F(g)$ is invariant to $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$.
Consequently, the effective search space is
$\Delta^{2}\times[0,0.2]\times\{0,1,2\}\times\{0,1\}$, where $\Delta^{2}$ is the
2-simplex of renormalised weights $(\tilde w_e,\tilde w_s,\tilde w_c)$, the
interval is the range of $\gamma$, $\{0,1,2\}$ indexes the template $d$, and
$\{0,1\}$ is the indicator $\mathbb{I}\{m\le256\}$, the only behaviourally
active distinction of the response-part budget (Eq.~\eqref{eq:part-budget}).
\end{Proposition}
\begin{proof}
Neither the generator $G$ (Section~\ref{sec:generator}) nor any term of
$f_i$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) reads $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ or
$\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$; the weights enter $G$ only through their
renormalised values; and $m\in\{512,768,1024\}$ produce identical outputs.
\end{proof}

\begin{Proposition}[Neutral genes do not alter the search on active genes]\label{prop:dynamics}
Let $A_t$ denote the active genes of the population at generation $t$. Under
tournament selection on $F$, uniform crossover, and per-gene mutation
(Section~\ref{sec:ga-config}), the law of $(A_t)_{t\ge0}$ is the same whether
or not $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are present in the
genome. The same holds for uniform random search.
\end{Proposition}
\begin{proof}
Selection depends on the population only through $F$, which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:neutral} depends only on active genes. Uniform crossover
draws each child gene independently of the others, and mutation perturbs each
gene independently, so the active genes of an offspring are conditionally
independent of the neutral genes given the parents' active genes.
Renormalisation involves only $w_e,w_s,w_c$.
\end{proof}
\noindent As an empirical check, five GA runs with $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and
$\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ frozen at constant values gave exactly the same
validation fitness as the full-genome runs with the same seeds
(0.6949--0.7027), although the selected genomes differ in their other genes.


\begin{Proposition}[A selection-invariant penalty]\label{prop:offset}
For every mid-risk example the soft-threshold indicator
$\mathbb{I}\{S_i<0.94\}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} equals~1 for
all genomes. Hence, over a fixed $\mathcal{I}$ containing $n_{\mathrm{mid}}$
mid-risk examples,
\[
  F(g) = F^{\circ}(g) - 0.15\,\frac{n_{\mathrm{mid}}}{|\mathcal{I}|},
\]
where $F^{\circ}$ omits that indicator for mid-risk examples, and the second
term does not depend on $g$. The maximiser of $F$, the ranking of genomes, and
all paired differences $F(g)-F(g')$ are therefore unaffected by this term.
\end{Proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}, $S_i\in\{0.60,0.85\}$ whenever $r_i=\mathrm{mid}$,
and both values are below $0.94$.
\end{proof}
\noindent Escalation quality for mid-risk prompts is still distinguished, but
through the base term: $B\,(0.85-0.60)=0.10$ per example.
Empirically, lowering the threshold to 0.80, so that it no longer penalises
escalated mid-risk responses, raises the validation fitness of the 30 GA genomes
of Section~\ref{sec:significance} by 0.007--0.014, that of B2 by 0.008--0.012, and
that of B1 by 0.002--0.005, while preserving the ranking of the GA genomes
(Spearman $\rho=0.94$); none of the comparisons reported in this paper changes.

\begin{Proposition}[Piecewise structure contribution]\label{prop:plateau}
Let $\phi(C_i)=C\,C_i-0.10\,\max(0,0.70-C_i)/0.70+0.05\,C_i+0.05\min(C_i,0.8)$
denote the structure-dependent terms of $f_i$. If $\tilde w_c\ge0.55$ and all
three inserted components are detected, then $C_i=1$ and
$\phi(C_i)=0.24$ for every example. If $\tilde w_c<0.25$ and no component is
detected, then $C_i=0$ and $\phi(C_i)=-0.10$. The structure-dependent part of
$F$ is therefore constant on each of these two regions, with a gap of $0.34$
per example between them.
\end{Proposition}
\begin{proof}
Direct substitution into Eqs.~\eqref{eq:structure}
and~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} with $C=0.15$, using the regimes of
Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}.
\end{proof}
\noindent Proposition~\ref{prop:plateau} explains the main empirical
observation of Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}: any search that samples a
point with $\tilde w_c\ge0.55$ reaches the structure plateau, and on the
plateau the remaining variation in $F$ can come only from the other
components. The detection condition is an empirical one, verified for the
reported genome in Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}; the keyword detector
can occasionally over- or under-count components
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}).

\emph{Evaluation cost.} Each fitness evaluation generates and scores
$|\mathcal{I}|$ responses, each at a cost bounded by a constant (a bounded
number of response parts and fixed marker lists). One GA run therefore costs
$\mathcal{O}(P\,T\,|\mathcal{I}|)$ generator-and-scorer calls, i.e.\
$100\times20\times600=1.2\times10^{6}$ in the main configuration. The
equal-budget random search of Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline} has the same
order of cost. Observed wall-clock times range from 64\,s to 882\,s per run
across the population sizes tested, on CPU only
(Appendix~\ref{app:population-tost}).




\subsection{Interface with Large Language Models}
\label{sec:llm-interface}

EvoRiri does not call a language model when generating, scoring, or searching:
the generator $G$ assembles responses from fixed phrase banks
(Section~\ref{sec:generator}). Language models interact with the system at one
implemented point, the comparison interface used for the baselines B6--B8, and
the architecture exposes a second, proposed point at which a language model
could take the place of $G$ (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, layer 5). We specify both
so that the integration mechanisms and the interactions between components are
explicit. Keeping the language model outside the generation and search loop is
a deliberate design choice: it keeps crisis escalation deterministic, makes
every response traceable to named genes, and allows the system to run without
GPU infrastructure.

\emph{Implemented comparison interface.} For a pre-trained model with frozen
parameters $\phi$, the response to a record is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llm-baseline}
  y_i^{\mathrm{LLM}} = \mathrm{Dec}_{\phi}\bigl(T(x_i)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ is the synthetic prompt constructed from the same record that the
template generator receives (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), $T$ wraps it in a fixed
English instruction template (``You are a compassionate mental health
assistant. Respond empathetically and helpfully to the following message.
User: $x_i$ Assistant:''), and $\mathrm{Dec}_{\phi}$ is the model's decoder.
Inputs are truncated to 512 tokens and at most 150 new tokens are generated,
using beam search with four beams for the sequence-to-sequence models (mT5-small
and BanglaT5) and greedy decoding for the causal model (BanglaGPT), whose output
is taken after the ``Assistant:'' marker. Each response is scored by the same
frozen scorer and per-example fitness as the template generator
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:empathy}--\eqref{eq:global}), on the same validation prompts. The
interaction is one-directional: the language model receives no genome
parameters and no feedback from the genetic algorithm, and its weights are not
updated. Results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}.

\emph{Proposed genome-conditioned generator (not evaluated).} The same genome
could control a language model in place of $G$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llm-proposed}
  y_i = \mathrm{Esc}\Bigl(\mathrm{Dec}_{\phi}\bigl(T_g(x_i, r_i);\,\kappa(g)\bigr),\; r_i,\, h_i,\, \gamma\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where the instruction composer $T_g$ maps the genome to explicit instructions
(for example, $w_c$ in the top regime adds requests for a grounding exercise, a
concrete action, and a reflective question, $w_e$ sets the extent of emotional
validation, and the template gene $d$ selects the persona), $\kappa(g)$ maps the
response-part budget $m$ to decoding controls such as the token limit, and
$\mathrm{Esc}$ applies the escalation rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation}
deterministically after generation, so that crisis guidance does not depend on
the language model. The scorer, the fitness function, and the genetic algorithm
would remain unchanged, so Proposition~\ref{prop:offset}, which depends only on
the scorer, would continue to hold. Two considerations explain why this design
was not evaluated here. First, each fitness evaluation would require
$|\mathcal{I}|$ generations, so one run would require
$P\,T\,|\mathcal{I}| = 1.2\times10^{6}$ generations, which is impractical without
the GPU infrastructure this work aims to avoid. Second, the zero-shot
Bangla-capable models evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines} did not
produce usable responses, so an instruction-following or fine-tuned model would
be needed first. We list this design as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Sensitivity to Dataset and Population Size}
\label{sec:dataset-size}\label{sec:population-size}

To address RQ3, we repeated the GA with five seeds at each of five nested
optimisation-set sizes ($N=100$ to $2297$) and six population sizes ($P=30$ to
$400$). Mean validation fitness varied only between 0.698 and 0.704 across
dataset sizes and between 0.699 and 0.703 across population sizes, with no
monotonic trend in either. Two one-sided tests at a pre-specified margin of
$\varepsilon=0.01$ establish equivalence to the reference condition for every
population size and for three of the four dataset sizes; $N=600$ narrowly
fails the upper test ($p=0.101$). Because this margin is about 2.5 times the
evaluation noise standard deviation and each condition rests on five seeds,
these tests bound rather than resolve any effect, and they describe stability
under the frozen scorer rather than generalisation. Smaller populations
($P=50$--$100$) are therefore sufficient here. Figures, full statistics, and
runtimes are given in Appendix~\ref{app:equivalence}
(Figures~\ref{fig:dataset-size} and~\ref{fig:population-size},
Table~\ref{tab:tost}).

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}

The convergence behaviour of the GA, from the main run under the frozen scoring pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}
($P=100$, $T=20$, $|\mathcal{I}|=600$), is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:fitness-curve}. The best (training-time) fitness begins
at 0.701 in generation~1 and fluctuates within a narrow band of approximately
0.698--0.704 for the remainder of the run, with no sustained secondary
improvement phase; this band is itself at the single-evaluation noise floor
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), so the single highest value observed
(0.704, generation~19)
is not resolvable from the initial value. Average fitness rises from 0.362
(generation~1) to approximately 0.60--0.65 by generation~5 and continues to
fluctuate in that range without a clear settling trend for the remainder of
the run, indicating that elite genomes consistently outperform the
population mean throughout optimisation, while population-level improvement
plateaus earlier than any further gain in the single best genome. Note that
these training-time fitness values, on a randomly sampled $|\mathcal{I}|=600$
subset of the optimisation set drawn once per run, differ both in scale
and in the underlying prompt sample from the validation-set scores reported
in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}.

The near-optimal generation-1
best fitness is attributable to a specific individual, not to the balanced
seed genome $g_\mathrm{bal}$: per-generation genome logs show
the generation-1 best individual is already structurally similar to the
eventual reported genome, consistent with a descendant of the
structure-heavy seed $g_\mathrm{str}$ rather than $g_\mathrm{bal}$ (full
genome values in Appendix~\ref{app:gen1}). In this run, therefore, a high-structure individual was present from the
first generation, and the remaining generations did not produce a resolvable
improvement on it.
Population fitness variance, which is not a direct measure of genotype
diversity, fluctuates between about 0.03 and 0.10 without a sustained downward
trend (Figure~\ref{fig:diversity-curve}, Appendix~\ref{app:genome-evolution}).

than B1 and B2. These are rule-based proxy scores: as the empathy marker-stuffing case shows

\subsection{MSGA-Seeded Initialisation Comparison}
\label{sec:msga}

To evaluate whether multiple-seed, domain-coverage initialisation can
substitute for manually designed seed genomes, we implemented an MSGA-inspired initialisation condition (MSGA)~\cite{kabir2017msga} as
an alternative initialisation strategy (condition B5). The original MSGA subdivides its solution space into $m$ domains, selects one
high-fitness chromosome from each domain as a seed, and uses the resulting
multiple seeds to generate an initial population. In our adaptation, the mixed genome space is
partitioned into \(m=4\) domains using empirical distance boundaries estimated
from 500 randomly sampled genomes. We retain an archive of 25 candidates per
domain and use archive-derived seeds to initialise the population.

B5 was evaluated under the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}) as B1--B4, in the same pipeline
run, with its final genome archived as \texttt{best\_genome\_B5.json}. It
achieves empathy 0.439, safety 0.900, structure 1.000, and fitness 0.695,
compared with B4's fitness of 0.703
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}). The 0.008-point difference is within evaluation noise and rests on a single
B5 run, so we do not interpret it as a difference between B4 and B5.

\subsubsection{Zero-Shot Bangla LLM Characterisation (B6--B8)}
\label{sec:llm-baselines}

For context, Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} reports three pre-trained
Bangla language models evaluated in zero-shot mode: mT5-small
\cite{mt5_2021} (B6; \texttt{google/mt5-small}), BanglaT5
\cite{banglat5_2022} (B7; \texttt{csebuetnlp/banglat5}), and BanglaGPT
\cite{banglagpt_2023} (B8; \texttt{shahidul034/BanglaGPT}). These conditions
are not GA genomes and receive no task-specific fine-tuning, in-context
examples, or EvoRiri response templates. They are evaluated on the same fixed
400-prompt seed-7 validation subsample as B1--B5. The underlying user messages
are constructed using \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()} and wrapped in
a common English instruction template requesting an empathetic and helpful
assistant response (Section~\ref{sec:llm-interface}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:llm-baseline}).

All three unadapted checkpoints produce poor conversational outputs under the
present prompt and decoding configuration. B6 generates sentinel tokens such as
\texttt{<extra\_id\_0>}, yielding zero empathy and structure scores; this is
consistent with the T5-style span-corruption pretraining framework of
mT5~\cite{mt5_2021}. B7 achieves the strongest component scores of the three
(empathy 0.064, safety 0.426, structure 0.094, and fitness $-0.366$), but
frequently produces repeated assistant prefixes, repeated user text, or
incomplete sentences. B8 generates repetitive character sequences, with
empathy 0.003, safety 0.426, zero structure, and fitness $-0.448$. These findings describe zero-shot behaviour under the
present prompts, decoding settings, and proxy-scoring pipeline; they do not
characterise the performance of fine-tuned, instruction-following, or
task-adapted Bangla language models. All B6--B8 values are computed using the same full per-example fitness pipeline
as B1--B5.

\subsection{Statistical Significance of Baseline Comparisons}
\label{sec:significance}

To assess whether the GA--baseline differences were consistently positive
across five paired seed-level re-evaluations, we applied one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945}. For each GA seed, we re-scored that
seed's archived GA genome together with the fixed B1 and B2 genomes under
the frozen scoring pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}) ($N_{\mathrm{eval}}=400$ on the
seed-7 validation split). Every number in this section and in
Table~\ref{tab:significance} is a directly observed result from these
re-scores of archived genomes, with no imputed scalar correction applied
across seeds.

All five GA seeds scored above both fixed baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:significance}). The one-sided Wilcoxon test indicates that
this direction is consistent across seeds for both B1 ($W = 15$,
$p = 0.031$) and B2 ($W = 15$, $p = 0.031$). We note that with only $n=5$
paired observations, $p=0.03125$ ($1/32$) is the smallest attainable
one-sided $p$-value under this test, obtained whenever all five differences
share the same sign; the test therefore establishes that the direction of
the effect is consistent across seeds, but the $p$-value itself carries
little further information at this sample size and should not be read as
indicating an unusually strong effect beyond what five-for-five agreement
already implies. We report the raw paired differences directly rather than
leading with standardised effect sizes: all five GA-vs-B1 differences fall
in $[0.898, 0.933]$ (median $0.912$), and all five GA-vs-B2 differences
fall in $[0.138, 0.161]$ (median $0.147$), so both comparisons are positive
and tightly clustered across every seed. Bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals (10{,}000 resamples over the five paired differences, percentile
method) are $[0.904, 0.928]$ for GA vs.\ B1 and $[0.141, 0.154]$ for GA
vs.\ B2, both well clear of zero. We also computed Cohen's $d$ (59.1 for
GA vs.\ B1, 16.3 for GA vs.\ B2) for continuity with the effect-size
reporting convention, but flag that these values are not meaningfully
interpretable using ordinary Cohen's $d$ benchmarks at this sample size:
with only five paired differences, $d$ is dominated by the very small
variance \emph{among} those five differences rather than by the
underlying effect in the way $d$ is normally intended to summarise. Given the tightly
clustered, uniformly positive raw differences, we treat the direction and
approximate magnitude of the improvement over both baselines as
well-supported by this analysis. Because five seeds are few, we extended the
analysis to 30 seeds ($13, 26, \ldots, 390$): all 30 GA genomes scored above both
baselines (GA $0.7000 \pm 0.0035$, B1 $-0.2158 \pm 0.0098$, B2
$0.5535 \pm 0.0070$; one-sided Wilcoxon $W=465$, $p=9.3\times10^{-10}$ for both
comparisons), with a median GA--B2 difference of 0.147 (range
$[0.132, 0.161]$; bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.144, 0.149]$). The first five of these
runs reproduce the genomes and scores of Table~\ref{tab:significance} exactly.

\subsection{Structure Component Usage}
\label{sec:structure-analysis}

Early optimisation revealed structural collapse, in which responses relied
heavily on questioning while omitting grounding and actionable guidance. After
revising the structure scorer and response generator, all three structural
components were consistently detected under the final scoring pipeline.
Figure~\ref{fig:structure-usage} shows how often grounding, action, and
question components are detected in the responses of B1, B2, and the reported
optimised configuration (B4).

For B4, all three structural components reach a usage frequency of 1.0,
indicating that the optimised genome ($w_c^{\star} = 0.768$) reliably
inserts grounding, action, and question content into every response,
consistent with the deterministic top regime ($w_c \ge 0.55$) described
in Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}. The balanced reference genome B2,
whose structure weight lies in the stochastic middle regime, includes all three
components in only 33\% of responses, and B1 almost never includes any.

All conditions reported in this manuscript were evaluated under one frozen
scorer, with every run's final genome archived
(Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}); the ordinal ranking B4 $>$ B2 $>$ B1 and the
overall pattern of results are stable across all five significance-test
seeds. The corrected marker lists were derived by inspecting responses on
the same validation set used for reporting rather than a separate
development set, so that split functions as a combined development and
validation set rather than an untouched final test set
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Risk-Aware Safety Behaviour}
\label{sec:risk-safety}

Figure~\ref{fig:safety-risk} shows, for each risk level, the share of responses
in which the safety scorer detects escalation content. B2 and B4 escalate for
every high-risk prompt and for no low-risk prompt; this differentiation comes
from the fixed escalation rule applied per risk label
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) and does not depend on any evolved
parameter. B1 escalates for only 13\% of high-risk prompts, because its short
response-part budget truncates the escalation line
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). For mid-risk prompts, about one response
in five from B2 and B4 is scored as containing escalation. On inspection, these
detections come mostly from one of the five soft-support phrases inserted for
mid-risk prompts, a Bangla sentence suggesting talking to a trusted person,
which contains a word on the escalation-marker list; we note this as a further,
minor instance of the proxy limitations discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Within the mid-risk category specifically, escalation behaviour
is not entirely fixed: the evolved genome parameter $\gamma$ can in principle
vary escalation with prompt content via the distress score $h_i$ in the
narrow band described in Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}. Of the 114 mid-risk prompts in the 400-prompt validation subsample, only one
falls in this band at $\gamma^{\star}\approx0.058$, and still only one at the
largest permitted value $\gamma=0.2$; 110 of the 114 have $h_i=0$. The
distress-gating gene therefore changes escalation for a single validation
prompt, consistent with $\gamma$ being only weakly identified by the present
fitness design (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation},
Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty}).

Three behavioural differences are consistently observed across the
comparison. First, B1 responses are, in the modal case, structurally
absent: no grounding instructions, no actionable guidance, and no
reflective questions, consisting of an empathy line and a reflection line
only, consistent with $w_c=0.10$ placing B1 in the bottom, no-structure
regime (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}). B1's structure score is
correspondingly near-zero but not exactly zero (0.017,
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}); we attribute the small residual to
occasional edge cases in response assembly or scoring rather than to any
deliberate structural inclusion.
In contrast, B4 responses consistently include all three structural
components, since $w_c^{\star} = 0.768$ falls in the top, deterministic
regime, with each component serving a distinct therapeutic function:
grounding exercises anchor the user in the present moment, action
suggestions provide concrete next steps, and reflective questions invite
further disclosure.

Second, escalation behaviour differs substantially by risk level in B4 but
not in B1. The generator's escalation rule
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) applies unconditionally to both
whenever the risk label is high, independent of genome weight; the
difference visible in Table~\ref{tab:baseline_vs_optimized} instead arises
from B1's minimum response-part budget ($m=256$) truncating escalation
content before it reaches the visible response, whereas B4's $m=512$
disables truncation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:part-budget}). B4 accordingly suppresses
explicit crisis language for low-risk prompts while reliably surfacing
escalation guidance for high-risk ones.

Third, B4 responses demonstrate multilingual composition,
weaving Bangla, Banglish, and English phrases within
a single response across functionally distinct components (grounding,
action, and question); as with the prompts themselves
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), this multilingual variation reflects the
generator's hand-authored phrase banks rather than a claim about
authentic code-switching patterns in real conversations. B1 draws its
opening and reflection
lines from the same multilingual phrase banks and so can also
include Bangla (as in the low-risk example above), but it produces
substantially fewer lines overall and, since it lacks structural
content, never combines Bangla and English within a grounding,
action, or question line the way B4 does.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human_eval}

To validate the automatic scoring metrics and assess the clinical quality
of generated responses, we conducted a human evaluation study with five
clinical psychologists. Two design limitations, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, qualify
this evaluation: it compares B1 rather than B2 with B4, and the raters also
contributed to the risk-label design.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Design}

We sampled 30 response pairs from the baseline vs.\ optimised comparison
dataset, stratified by risk level (6 low-risk, 9 mid-risk, and 15 high-risk items),
approximately matching the corpus risk distribution.
For each item, annotators were shown the user prompt and two responses
labelled A and B in randomised order, without knowing which system
generated each response (rater-blinded; the researchers preparing and
analysing the response pairs were not themselves blinded to system
identity, so we describe this design as single-blind from the annotators'
perspective rather than double-blind).
Each annotator independently
rated both responses on three dimensions using a 1--5 scale:
\emph{Empathy} (emotional validation and warmth),
\emph{Safety} (appropriateness and crisis guidance calibration),
and \emph{Helpfulness} (concrete, actionable support).
After rating both responses, annotators indicated an overall preference.
Inter-annotator agreement was measured using Krippendorff's
alpha~\cite{krippendorff2011computingalpha} and pairwise percentage agreement
within one scale point.

\subsubsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} summarises the results. The GA-optimised
system (B4) is rated higher than the minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) on
all three dimensions, with item-level mean improvements of $+1.68$ on
Empathy, $+1.77$ on Safety, and $+1.95$ on Helpfulness. These estimates
and their significance tests are computed at the level of the 30
response-pair items rather than the 150 individual annotator-item
ratings, since each item was rated by all five annotators and the
within-item ratings are correlated rather than independent; we detail
the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:pseudorep} below. Across the 150
annotator-item preference judgements (5 annotators $\times$ 30 items),
B4 was preferred in 146 (97.3\%), B1 in 2 (1.3\%), with 2 judgements
(1.3\%) marked equal. At the item level, all five annotators agreed
unanimously on 27 of the 30 items (all favouring B4); the remaining
three items each drew at least one dissenting judgement (one annotator
preferring B1 on each of two items, and two annotators rating a third
item as equal), rather than uniform agreement across all 30 items. Because B1 carries a
truncation defect (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}), we read this as a
preference for complete over truncated responses, not as evidence that
evolutionary search improves clinical quality; a B2--B4 clinician comparison
is listed as future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

These ratings also bear on the automatic empathy proxy reported in
Section~\ref{sec:qualcomp}. In the B1--B4 comparison, the automatic empathy
difference is $0.161$, smaller than the corresponding structure and safety
differences. On blinded clinician ratings of the same comparison, however,
empathy improved by 1.68 points, similar in magnitude to the safety (1.77) and
helpfulness (1.95) differences. We have not,
however, formally tested whether these three effects are statistically equal
to one another (e.g.\ via a system-by-dimension interaction test), so we
describe them as similar in magnitude rather than as confirmed to be
statistically indistinguishable.

The two measurements agree in direction, both favouring B4, but not in the
apparent magnitude of the empathy improvement. One plausible explanation is
limited sensitivity of the marker-based proxy: the rule-based empathy score
saturates once a handful of distinct markers are present
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:empathy}) and penalises repetition, so it has limited dynamic
range to register further differences once a response clears that low bar.
Clinicians reading the full response may still perceive and reward differences
in warmth, specificity, and emotional attunement that the marker count does
not distinguish. This is a concrete instance of the proxy-validity limitation
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: in this B1-vs-B4 comparison, the
proxy may under-credit an improvement perceived by clinician raters, whereas
the marker-stuffing case in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck} illustrates
the opposite risk of over-crediting surface-level phrasing.

\subsubsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}
\label{sec:pseudorep}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition are not 150 independent
observations, since ratings are correlated within annotator and within
item. We therefore report inference at the level of the 30 items, the
genuine unit of independent replication, by averaging the 5 raters' scores
per item and condition before computing paired differences. This yields
the same mean improvements as the raw annotator-item means in
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} with correctly calibrated uncertainty, and
remains highly significant under paired $t$-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests, and a crossed linear mixed model. Full statistics for all three
checks are in Appendix~\ref{app:humaneval}.

\subsubsection{Inter-Annotator Agreement}

We computed Krippendorff's alpha using the \texttt{krippendorff} Python
package with an interval difference function over the 60 blinded response
units (30 items $\times$ 2 responses), yielding a $5 \times 60$ reliability
matrix with no missing ratings. Alpha was $0.56$ for Empathy, $0.58$ for
Safety, and $0.61$ for Helpfulness; percentile-bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals from 2{,}000 resamples were $[0.48, 0.62]$, $[0.50, 0.64]$, and
$[0.54, 0.66]$, respectively.

Under a commonly cited interpretation, \(\alpha \geq 0.800\) is customary
for reliable conclusions and \(\alpha \geq 0.667\) is the lowest limit for
tentative conclusions~\cite{statisticshowto_krippendorffalpha}, so we treat
the ratings as directional rather than calibrated. Pairwise agreement within
one scale point was 86.0\% for Empathy, 87.2\% for Safety, and 85.2\% for
Helpfulness.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Role of Seeded Initialisation}

Seeded initialisation played two roles in this study. We did not run a
randomly initialised control, so we make no general claim about its effect
on convergence. Per-generation genome logs show that the near-best observed generation-1 fitness (0.701,
training-time) is attributable to a structure-heavy individual ($w_c
\approx 0.68$) consistent with a mutated or crossed-over descendant of
the structure-heavy seed $g_\mathrm{str}$, rather than the balanced seed
$g_\mathrm{bal}$: $g_\mathrm{bal}$'s own directly measured fitness (0.556
as B2) is well below that generation-1 value. Via this lineage the GA
reaches near-optimal fitness within a single generation and, as
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, does not discover a substantially better
configuration across the remaining 19 generations. On the negative side,
seeds may also help the GA converge more quickly to suboptimal behavioural
patterns if the scoring function contains exploitable shortcuts: in our
experiments, all seeds converged to a question-only structural-collapse
failure mode under the initial scoring design
(Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}, Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}). This
illustrates a general principle: seeded initialisation amplifies whatever
signal the fitness function provides, whether that signal is well-designed
or not, so seeded initialisation must be paired with trace-level
evaluation to avoid accelerating convergence toward the wrong objective.

\subsection{Importance of Trace Analysis}

Scalar fitness values alone suggested successful optimisation
throughout early development. Trace analysis of exported response logs,
however, revealed major behavioural failures that aggregate metrics
concealed entirely -- most significantly, question-only structural
collapse, in which responses with fitness values of 0.6--0.7 consisted
almost entirely of reflective questions, exploiting an initial structure metric that rewarded
question presence without requiring grounding or actionable guidance
(Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}).

This has broader implications for evaluating generative AI systems.
Optimisation metrics, whether hand-crafted or learned, define what a
system optimises for, not necessarily what it should do; any sufficiently
exploratory optimiser, including evolutionary search, can discover
discrete behavioural modes that satisfy the metric while violating its
underlying intent. Trace analysis is therefore a necessary, not merely
supplementary, component of the evaluation pipeline for any reward-driven
generative system. The response-part-budget truncation defect
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) and the marker-coverage gap
(Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}) are further instances of this same
pattern: both were found only by tracing individual responses against
internal generation flags, not by inspecting aggregate scores.

\subsection{Scoring Function as Bottleneck}
\label{sec:scoring-bottleneck}

Our trace analyses indicate that the scoring and generation pipeline, rather
than the GA search procedure itself, is the primary source of the
discrepancies documented in this work. The first two failure modes
are consistent with reward hacking: the optimiser exploits an unintended
shortcut in the stated objective rather than achieving the underlying
intended behaviour~\cite{amodei2016concrete}. In our context, we observed
and either corrected or documented four distinct failure modes, together with one scoring-design weakness (safety saturation) that remains partly resolved.

First, \textit{question-only structural collapse}: the initial structure
score rewarded question presence without requiring grounding or actionable
guidance, so the GA produced responses consisting almost entirely of
reflective questions (Appendix~\ref{app:collapse}). We replaced the
continuous reward with the discrete component-count lookup in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, which caps a question-only response at $0.20$.

Second, \textit{empathy marker stuffing}: the initial empathy score rewarded
marker count linearly, incentivising repeated validation phrases
(Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing-example}). We added a stuffing penalty when total
marker hits exceed~3.

Third, \textit{structure-marker coverage gap}: an incomplete keyword list
undercounted genuinely inserted structural components in some high-risk
responses (Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}); we expanded the marker
lists.

Fourth, \textit{response-part-budget truncation}: B1's minimal budget silently
drops escalation content appended late in the assembly order
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). This is a generator defect rather than a
scoring issue, but it was likewise found by tracing individual responses
rather than from aggregate metrics.

Separately from these four, \textit{safety saturation}: the initial safety score
saturated quickly, giving little selection signal to separate adequate from
stronger crisis responses. We introduced smooth soft thresholds, although the
current threshold of $0.94$ is unattainable for any mid-risk response
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) and so does not fully resolve this for
that risk category.

The interpretable genome and modular scoring design made these diagnoses
possible: each score component and generation step mapped to a specific
behavioural dimension, so failure modes could be attributed and, where
possible, corrected individually. The first three were addressed before the
scoring pipeline was frozen for all reported analyses
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}); the fourth is specific to B1's construction
rather than to the GA's scoring or search, and is carried forward as a
limitation, as is the residual safety-saturation weakness
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).


\subsection{Interpretation of Baseline Results}
\label{sec:interp-baseline}

B1's weights ($w_e=0.10$, $w_c=0.10$, $w_s=0.80$; Section~\ref{sec:methods})
are chosen so that genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged, keeping
$w_c$ in the bottom, no-structure regime rather than drifting into the
stochastic mid-regime.

B1's near-zero structure score (0.013 in the common 400-prompt
re-evaluation) shows that this minimal-weight configuration produces almost
entirely unstructured responses, despite phrase banks containing grounding,
action, and question components. Its negative fitness ($-0.194$) is driven
primarily by low safety (0.475), which we trace to the response-part-budget
truncation mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}): B1's $m=256$ setting
silently drops escalation content for high-risk prompts, rather than the
fixed risk-aware escalation rule failing.

This is a generator-design defect, not merely an unoptimised parameter
setting, because it can silently remove required crisis content. To isolate
its effect, we re-evaluated B1, B1-fixed (B1 with \texttt{memory\_window}
raised from 256 to 512), and B4 using one frozen generation and scoring path
on the identical fixed 400-prompt validation subsample.

Table~\ref{tab:truncation-diagnostic} shows that removing truncation raises

evaluation, B1's safety deficit is therefore consistent with being driven
mainly by the truncation defect, whereas B4's structural advantage is not: it
appears only in the comparison between the non-truncating minimal-structure
configuration and B4. The total fitness difference splits descriptively into
$+0.471$ from removing truncation (B1 to B1-fixed) and $+0.423$ for the
remaining B1-fixed--B4 difference. Because these values come from one
re-evaluation run, they are a diagnostic pattern rather than noise-free
effect estimates, and the B1-fixed--B4 difference is not a pure estimate of
the GA's causal contribution because the conditions differ in multiple genome
parameters.

The B2--B4 comparison is more informative for the remaining configuration
difference, because B2 does not share B1's truncation defect. The balanced
seed genome already achieves high safety (0.903) but leaves substantial room
for structural improvement (0.648 vs.\ 1.000). The reported B4 configuration
uses a high structure weight ($w_c^{\star} \approx 0.77$), which is
associated with higher structural completeness than the balanced
initialisation. We checked this with a 12-point grid search over $w_c$ alone,
holding the $w_e{:}w_s$ ratio fixed at the reported genome's value so that
raw weights already sum to 1 and renormalisation is a no-op at every grid
point. The results are consistent with the three structure regimes and
thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}: structure is
effectively zero for $w_c < 0.25$ (with small residual values possible
because of response-assembly or scoring edge cases), fluctuates
stochastically around 0.62--0.66 for $0.25 \le w_c < 0.55$, and reaches 1.000
at every grid point with $w_c \ge 0.55$. 
The mid-regime mean exceeds the 0.60 of exactly two detected components for two reasons: the generator can insert
all three components at the lower end of this range, and the keyword-based
structure detector scores three components in roughly 9--11\% of responses at
$w_c=0.40$ and $0.50$ even though exactly two were inserted, because short
markers such as ``try'', ``step'', ``what'', ``when'', and ``?'' occur in
lines serving a different component. Fitness within the deterministic regime
is not flat: it rises from 0.652 at $w_c = 0.55$ to a peak of 0.710 at
$w_c = 0.70$, then eases to 0.701 at the top of the range, so the reported
$w_c^{\star} = 0.768$ sits on the plateau but past its peak. Each grid point
carries evaluation noise of order 0.003--0.005 (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}),
so differences of a few thousandths within the plateau, and the exact
location of its peak, should not be over-interpreted. This supports the
interpretation that the structure-dominant result does not require
evolutionary search to discover: a twelve-point sweep over a single gene
reaches the same fitness region as the GA's five converged runs, consistent
with the generator's deterministic structure threshold ($w_c \ge 0.55$) doing
most of the explanatory work.

We also ran five independent equal-budget random searches (2,000 draws each
over the full genome space, $w_e, w_s, w_c, \theta_\mathrm{mid},
\theta_\mathrm{high}, \gamma, d, m$, matching the GA's implicit budget of
$100 \times 20 = 2{,}000$ evaluations), sampled independently of the grid
search. Scored on the same 400-prompt subsample used for selection, the five
best genomes reach $0.7066$--$0.7110$ (mean $0.7085$, SD $0.0018$), above
every GA seed in Table~\ref{tab:significance}. That comparison is not sound:
each random-search maximum is the largest of 2,000 noisy evaluations on the
very set it is reported against, whereas the GA's genomes were selected on
optimisation-set samples and never on this subsample. A maximum over 2,000
draws from a measurement with SD $\approx 0.003$--$0.005$
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) inflates the reported value by several
standard deviations independently of genome quality.

Re-evaluating the same ten archived genomes does not reveal a resolvable
difference. On the 575-prompt validation split, GA $0.7001 \pm 0.0025$ versus
RS $0.7023 \pm 0.0056$; on the 400-prompt subsample, GA $0.7040 \pm 0.0032$
versus RS $0.7020 \pm 0.0024$. These are fresh, unseeded re-evaluations of the same five archived GA
genomes as in Table~\ref{tab:significance}, whose seeded re-scores average
$0.6993$; the paired difference between the two draws (mean $0.0047$, SD
$0.006$ across the five genomes) is not distinguishable from the
single-evaluation noise of Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}.
Neither set is fully clean: the random searches
selected on the 400-prompt subsample, which is contained in the 575-prompt
split, so both are re-evaluations rather than held-out tests for RS. A
re-score on the full 2,872-prompt corpus (GA $0.7015 \pm 0.0019$ versus RS
$0.6996 \pm 0.0013$) is post-selection for RS but overlaps the GA's
optimisation set, so it is not neutral in the other direction. Across all
three sets the difference is $\approx 0.002$ with an unstable sign, well
inside the single-evaluation noise floor.

We therefore conclude that no difference between the two methods is
resolvable from these five archived runs; the 30-seed comparison below removes
the selection asymmetry. On the 400-prompt subsample all five random-search genomes re-score below
their archived selection-time values (by 0.0035--0.0107, mean 0.0065), as
expected if the archived values are maxima over noisy evaluations, whereas the
GA genomes show no such downward shift (mean change $+0.0047$). On the
575-prompt split the random-search shift is smaller and not uniform (mean
$-0.0062$; four of five genomes lower). This pattern is consistent with
selection on noisy evaluations rather than with superior genome quality. This
is also consistent with prior work on GA-based prompt optimisation, whose authors
report validation--test discrepancies and attribute them to selection bias
\cite{gaapo2025}.

The 2,000 draws underlying each random search have a mean fitness of only
$\approx 0.33$ (SD $\approx 0.33$), so most random genomes are far from the
plateau; what the five runs establish is that undirected search locates the
plateau consistently at this budget, not that any individual draw is likely to.

To remove this selection asymmetry, we repeated the comparison with 30 paired
seeds under a common design: for each seed, the GA and an equal-budget random
search (2,000 draws from the same genome space) both selected on the same
600-prompt sample of the optimisation set, and the selected genomes were then
scored on the 400-prompt validation subsample with the same generation seed, so
that neither method used the validation prompts for selection. The two methods
selected different genomes ($w_c$ between 0.66 and 0.81 for the GA and between
0.66 and 0.78 for random search), but all lie on the structure plateau, and on
28 of the 30 seeds they received identical validation scores. Mean validation
fitness was 0.7000 for both methods (paired difference $1.7\times10^{-5}$, 90\%
CI $[-2.9\times10^{-5}, 6.4\times10^{-5}]$), and a paired two one-sided test
establishes equivalence at $\varepsilon=0.01$ ($p<10^{-50}$). The GA reached
higher fitness on its own selection sample (0.710 versus 0.705), but this did not
carry over to validation. In the present search space the two methods are
therefore equivalent, as Proposition~\ref{prop:plateau} predicts.

To identify which genome differences account for the B2$\to$B4 gap, we moved one factor at a time between the two genomes (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}; ten repeats on the fixed 400-prompt subsample, with generation seeds shared across conditions). The inactive thresholds $\theta_\mathrm{mid}$ and $\theta_\mathrm{high}$ were held at the base genome's values, and the response-part budget $m$ is 512 in both genomes and is therefore not tested. Moving only the structure weight $w_c$ from 0.34 to 0.768 reproduces the full gap ($+0.146$), and moving B4's $w_c$ back to 0.34 removes it. Taking all three weights from the other genome gives the same result as taking $w_c$ alone, so the $w_e{:}w_s$ split does not contribute. Neither $\gamma$ nor the template index $d$ changes fitness. This is consistent with the progressive-structure regimes of Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}, in which $w_c\ge0.55$ yields all three structural components deterministically, and with the plateau in the grid search above. The improvement of B4 over B2 is therefore attributable to a single weight that a hand-set value in the plateau would also reproduce; 
both the genetic search and equal-budget random search located this region without prior knowledge of the regime boundary, as expected for any search that samples $w_c\ge0.55$.

These observations answer RQ2 descriptively: B4 maintains safety comparable
to B2 while substantially improving structural completeness, and the grid
search and the one-factor ablation identify the generator's deterministic
structure threshold, reached through $w_c$, as the mechanism responsible. We
do not attribute the gain to evolutionary search as such: a hand-set $w_c$ in
the plateau would reproduce it, and the ablation does not test interactions
between genes or the response-part budget, which is identical in B2 and B4
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The measured improvement therefore comes from the design of the generator and
the fitness function, not from the search method.

\subsection{Scalarised Fitness and the Structure-Dominant Optimum}
\label{sec:balance-discussion}

The fixed fitness coefficients (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) give
empathy and safety the largest shares ($A=B=0.40$) and structure the smallest
($C=0.15$), reflecting our intended prioritisation of safe, empathetic
responses. Yet the reported genome is structure-dominant
($w_c^{\star}=0.768$ versus $w_e^{\star}=0.131$;
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}). The evolved $w_s^{\star}$ is not part of this
tension: as noted in Section~\ref{sec:methods}, $w_s$ is a renormalisation
participant rather than an independent safety preference, since safety
behaviour is governed by the fixed escalation rule and categorical safety
lookup.

The resolution lies in distinguishing the \emph{fixed} coefficients
$A,B,C,D$, which set how base scores combine into fitness, from the
\emph{evolved} weights $w_e, w_c$, which set how much empathy and structural
content the generator inserts; these act on different score landscapes.
Safety and empathy both saturate quickly: safety reaches a high plateau
($S_i \approx 0.90$--$1.00$) once escalation is reliably present, independent
of $w_s$, and empathy saturates once a few distinct markers appear, beyond
which further weight risks the marker-stuffing penalty. Structure has the
largest unrealised headroom in the unoptimised baseline (B1 structure
$=0.017$) and responds sharply to $w_c$ at the deterministic threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}). Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
adds a reinforcement asymmetry: structure is reinforced twice (up to $+0.09$
combined), empathy once (capped at $+0.05$), and safety not at all beyond its
base term. The structure-dominant outcome therefore reflects both where
headroom is largest and where the fitness function rewards marginal content
most, neither of which is visible from the coefficients $A,B,C,D$ alone.

This suggests the GA found a corner solution, with empathy near its
saturation floor and structure maximised, rather than a smooth trade-off
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}: empathy stays modest while structure
climbs substantially). This is a limitation of combining three objectives into one scalar fitness with fixed coefficients:
the resulting genome reflects the geometry of the constraint set as much as
the analyst's stated priorities. A multi-objective evolutionary algorithm
maintaining an explicit Pareto front over $(E_i, S_i, C_i)$ would make the
trade-off space visible and let a clinician select an operating point
directly; we list this as future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).



\subsection{Ethical Risks and Deployment Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Deploying conversational agents in mental-health contexts raises ethical
concerns beyond algorithmic performance. This subsection examines deployment
risks specific to the proposed framework and the Bangladeshi context.


\textbf{Deployment risks.} Because the scores are keyword-based proxies,
optimising them can yield responses that are metrically but not clinically
safe, so any deployment would require ongoing review by qualified
professionals. Substitution risk is heightened in Bangladesh, which has an
estimated 1.17 mental-health professionals per 100{,}000 population and where
more than 90\% of people with a mental-health condition do not seek
treatment~\cite{koly2024stakeholder}; a referral to unavailable help may feel
dismissive, so escalation should acknowledge service-availability limits. The
empathy markers were built by the research team and have not been validated
with local clinicians or community workers, and emotional restraint common in
collectivist cultures may be misread by such proxies~\cite{ijca2025emotion}.
New failure modes may arise with real users, so deployment would need
continuous trace monitoring; the interpretable genome makes targeted
corrections possible without re-running the full search. Finally, the
high-risk category includes PHQ-4 scores of 9--12 without an explicit
self-harm indicator, so it may include severe presentations without
suicidality (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}).

\textbf{Ethical oversight and data governance.} No independent ethics committee
or institutional review board approval was obtained (see the Institutional
Review Board Statement). The data are de-identified records from routine
mental-health screening and support delivered through the application, not from
a study designed and approved for research, and their research use was
authorised by the data controller's internal research team rather than by an
external body. The screening questionnaires were administered by Relaxy PTE.
LTD. as part of its routine well-being assessment and as a prerequisite for
using the application; the research team did not administer them and used only
the resulting de-identified data. De-identification, electronic consent, and
synthetic prompt reconstruction reduce risk but do not substitute for
independent ethical oversight, and its absence is a limitation of this study.

  \subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
  \label{sec:limitations}
  
  The following limitations define the scope of the present findings. Diagnostic
  evidence is reported in the referenced sections; here we summarise the
  implications for interpretation and the priorities for future work.
  
  \noindent\textbf{Evaluation and causal attribution.} The validation split also
  contributed to scorer development, so it is a combined development--validation
  set, although it was not used for selection in the 30-seed GA--random-search
  comparison. B1 is a failure-mode reference rather than a clean unoptimised
  baseline (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). The B2--B4 ablation moves one
  factor at a time and does not test interactions, and B5 rests on a single run.
  
  \noindent\textbf{Measurement.} Repeated evaluations of one genome vary by about
  0.003--0.005 (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), so differences of this
  order, including B4--B5, are not resolvable, and the
  equivalence tests bound rather than resolve the dataset- and population-size
  effects. The dataset- and population-size conditions rest on five GA seeds each. The automatic scores are
  keyword-based proxies that cannot capture context, negation, sarcasm, or
  culturally specific expression, and the structure detector occasionally
  overcounts components. The 0.94 safety soft threshold cannot be reached by any mid-risk
  response; it does not affect which genome is selected
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:offset}), but it cannot distinguish adequate from
  stronger mid-risk escalation.
  
  \noindent\textbf{Human evaluation.} The clinician study compares B1 rather than
  B2 with B4, its raters contributed to the risk-label design, and agreement
  supports directional preference only (Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}).
  
  \noindent\textbf{Scope, data, and governance.} The prompts are synthetic
  reconstructions and the responses single-turn, so the findings do not extend to
  authentic or multi-turn conversations. The risk categories are not validated
  suicide-risk classifications, some genes are inactive or weakly identified, and
  one fixed genome serves all users. No independent ethics approval was obtained
  (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}), and age is self-reported.

Natural extensions of this work include:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A generator in which safety behaviour is controlled by an evolvable
  gene, for example the placement or intensity of escalation content, so that
  the safety weight $w_s$ has a direct effect on responses rather than acting
  only through renormalisation (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}).
  \item Multi-turn genome policies conditioned on conversation history, since
  risk can accumulate across a dialogue~\cite{journey2026}; this may also
  provide selection pressure on $\gamma$ (Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty}).

  \item A more robust generator that separates response-length truncation from
  internal template and safety-control tokens, or inserts escalation content
  before lower-priority content so that the response-part budget cannot discard
  it. This would prevent the class of defect illustrated by B1 by construction
  rather than merely diagnosing it.

  \item Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms with explicit Pareto fronts over
  empathy, safety, and structure, making trade-offs visible to a clinician
  rather than collapsing them into a single scalar fitness.

  \item Learned empathy and safety evaluators trained and validated on
  independently clinician-annotated data, together with human-feedback-informed
  evaluation in which independently collected clinician and user ratings
  validate or refine the fitness function, with safety-critical criteria kept
  separate rather than reduced to a single preference score.

  \item An independent clinician-in-the-loop evaluation including B2 and a
  non-truncating B1 variant alongside B4, with mental-health professionals
  rating blinded response pairs under a controlled protocol. Any live deployment
  study would require separate clinical oversight, governance, and safety
  monitoring.

  \item Multilingual expansion beyond Bangla/Banglish/English to other South
  Asian languages with similarly limited mental-health support infrastructure.
  \item The genome-conditioned language-model generator of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:llm-proposed}, using an instruction-following Bangla model and
  evaluated with the same scorer and search (Section~\ref{sec:llm-interface}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented EvoRiri, a diagnostic case study of interpretable evolutionary
search over a template-based Bangla--Banglish--English response generator that
needs no GPU or language model. The genetic algorithm is standard; the
contribution lies in what the interpretable design made visible.

Trace-level analysis exposed reward-design and generator failures that aggregate
fitness concealed, and controlled analyses traced the measured improvement to a
single structural threshold in the generator rather than to evolutionary search:
grid search and equal-budget random search reached the same region, and random
search was equivalent to the GA. Combining three objectives into one scalar
fitness produced a structure-dominant optimum rather than a balance among
empathy, safety, and structure.

These conclusions hold for synthetic single-turn prompts and rule-based proxy
scores, and do not establish clinical effectiveness or deployment readiness.
Future work should test guided search in genome spaces without such shortcuts,
use Pareto-based multi-objective methods, validate the metrics against
independent clinical judgement, and extend the framework to multi-turn dialogue
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Evolutionary Failure Mode Examples}
\label{app:failure}

This appendix provides concrete examples of the two evolutionary failure
modes documented in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: question-only structural
collapse and empathy marker stuffing. These failure modes were discovered
through trace analysis during early development, before the scoring function
was redesigned. The examples below are representative of the response patterns
produced by high-fitness genomes under the initial (flawed) scoring design.

\subsection{Question-Only Structural Collapse}
\label{app:collapse}

Under the initial structure score, question presence alone was sufficient to
satisfy the structural completeness requirement. Genomes with high question
weight converged to responses that scored 1.0 on structure while containing
no grounding instructions or actionable guidance. A representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``I feel really anxious and overwhelmed. Family niye onek
stress e achi. What should I do now?''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high structure score):}
``How long have you been feeling this way? What do you think is causing
the most stress? Have you talked to anyone about this? Is there something
specific that happened recently? What usually helps you feel better?''

\textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad \textit{Empathy score:} 0.05 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60
\end{quote}

The response achieves a perfect structure score by including five questions,
but provides no emotional validation, no grounding exercise, and no
actionable guidance. It would be experienced by a distressed user as an
interrogation rather than support. This failure mode was corrected by
replacing the continuous, count-based structure score with the discrete
component-count lookup of Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, under which any response
satisfying only one of the three components (grounding, action, or
question) is capped at $0.20$, regardless of how many times that single
component is echoed.

\subsection{Empathy Marker Stuffing}
\label{app:stuffing-example}

Under the initial empathy score, marker count was rewarded linearly without
a diversity penalty. Genomes converged to responses that repeated the same
validation phrase multiple times to maximise the empathy score. A
representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi lately. I feel really
anxious and overwhelmed.''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high empathy score):}
``It makes sense that you feel this way. It makes sense to feel
overwhelmed. It makes sense that this is hard. It makes sense that
you're struggling. What would help you right now?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.90 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 0.20
\end{quote}

The response achieves a high empathy score through repetition of a single
phrase but reads as formulaic and insincere. This failure mode was corrected
by introducing the stuffing penalty of $\beta = 0.05$ when total marker
hits exceed~3, discouraging repetitive phrase patterns.

\subsection{Post-Fix Optimised Response (Same Prompt)}
\label{app:postfix}

After scoring redesign, the same prompt from Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing-example}
produces:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Optimised response (post-fix):}
``Feeling this alone can be really painful. {\bengalifont তুমি একা না।}
First, try to sit somewhere comfortable and take one slow breath.
Try not to stay completely isolated with these thoughts.
Is there someone nearby you feel comfortable talking to?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.50 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60 (mid-risk, no escalation content; see
Appendix~\ref{app:worked-example} for the corresponding fitness
calculation)
\end{quote}

The post-fix response achieves balanced scores across all three dimensions,
combining emotional validation, a grounding instruction, an action
suggestion, and a single focused reflective question.

\subsection{Worked Example: From Prompt to Fitness}
\label{app:worked-example}

This subsection walks the same prompt and response from
Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} through every
stage of the pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, using the reported
genome $g^{\star}$ from Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}
($w_e^{\star}=0.131$, $w_s^{\star}=0.101$, $w_c^{\star}=0.768$,
$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}^{\star}=0.588$, $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}^{\star}=0.700$).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Prompt + risk label.} $x_i = $ ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi
        lately. I feel really anxious and overwhelmed.'' For illustration we
        take $r_i = \mathrm{mid}$.
  \item \textbf{Genome.} The generator is configured with $g^{\star}$ as
        above (Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}).
  \item \textbf{Generator.} Because $w_c^{\star}=0.768$ falls in the top
        structure regime ($w_c \ge 0.55$; Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
        the generator deterministically includes grounding, action, and
        question components ($c_g=c_a=c_q=1$); $w_e^{\star}$ governs how
        many empathy lines are included.
  \item \textbf{Response.} $y_i = G(x_i,r_i;g^{\star}) = $ the response shown
        in Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} above.
  \item \textbf{Automatic scoring.} Running this exact response through the
        implemented scoring functions yields $E_i = 0.50$, $S_i = 0.60$,
        $C_i = 1.00$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:empathy}--\eqref{eq:structure}). The
        response contains no escalation-language marker (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}),
        which is expected and appropriate for a mid-risk prompt whose
        computed distress score is low ($h_i \approx 0$, well below
        $\tau_h=0.65$); under the categorical safety lookup
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}), a mid-risk response without escalation
        content scores $S_i=0.60$ by construction, and $S_i=1.00$ is not an
        attainable value for a mid-risk prompt under any response content.
  \item \textbf{Fitness.} The response is below the 140-word penalty
        threshold, so $L_i=0$. Since $S_i=0.60 < 0.94$, the flat safety
        soft-threshold penalty of $-0.15$ applies (the risk label is mid,
        not high, so the $-0.50$ high-risk penalty does not apply); since
        $h_i \le \tau_h$, the distress-gating penalty
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}, final line) also does not
        apply. $C_i=1.00 \ge 0.70$, so the structure soft penalty does not
        apply; $E_i=0.50 < 0.62$, so the empathy soft penalty does apply.
        The per-example fitness (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) is
        \begin{align*}
          f_i(g^{\star}) &= \underbrace{0.40(0.50) + 0.40(0.60) + 0.15(1.00)}_{\text{base } A E_i+B S_i+C C_i}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.15}_{\text{safety soft-threshold penalty}}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.18\,\tfrac{0.62-0.50}{0.62}}_{\text{empathy soft penalty}} \\
            &\quad + \underbrace{0.05(0.50) + 0.05(1.00) + 0.05\min(1.00,0.8)}_{\text{reinforcement terms}} \\
            &\approx 0.590 - 0.150 - 0.035 + 0.025 + 0.050 + 0.040 = 0.520.
        \end{align*}
        This illustrates the per-example fitness computation
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) that underlies both the
        training-time fitness $F_{\mathrm{train}}(g) = \frac{1}{600}
        \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{train}}} f_i(g)$, averaged over the
        $|\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{train}}|=600$ prompts sampled once per run from the optimisation set(Eq.~\eqref{eq:global}), and the validation fitness
        $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{400}\sum_{i \in V_{400}} f_i(g)$,
        averaged over the fixed 400-prompt subsample of the separate validation set. The
        reported validation fitness $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star}) \approx
        0.703$ (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}) is computed on this
        validation subsample, not on the run's fixed 600-prompt
        training sample; the two quantities use different, non-overlapping prompt
        populations and are not directly interchangeable, though both use
        the same per-example fitness formula. The single-example value
        above is lower than the reported $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star})$
        because this example's response does not
        contain any escalation content and so incurs the safety
        soft-threshold penalty, whereas across the validation
        set most low- and high-risk prompts (which make up the majority of
        the dataset; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) attain $S_i=1.00$ under
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety} and so do not incur this penalty.
  \item \textbf{GA feedback.} Across generations, genomes whose responses
        score well on this fitness are more likely to be selected, crossed
        over, and mutated into the next population
        (Section~\ref{sec:ga-config}), which is how the search arrived at
        $g^{\star}$ from the seeded initial population
        (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Optimised Genome Evolution Examples}
\label{app:genomes}

\subsection{Renormalisation, $w_s$, Reserved Escalation Genes, and the Omitted Length-Penalty Gene}
\label{app:renorm}

The three continuous weights
$w_e, w_s, w_c$ are stored in raw (post-mutation, pre-normalisation) form in
the genome, and are renormalised to sum to 1 at the point the generator
reads them, after all crossover and mutation for that individual has been
applied. Consequently, two genomes with identical raw $w_e$ and $w_c$ but
different raw $w_s$ will be assembled into \emph{different} responses,
because the shared normalising denominator $w_e+w_s+w_c$ differs between
them. $w_s$'s evolved value is therefore best interpreted as a
normalisation-only participant rather than as an independently meaningful
``safety weight,'' and we avoid describing $w_s^{\star}=0.101$ as reflecting
an evolved safety preference (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}); its
only demonstrated effect on any reported result is through the denominator
it contributes to.

$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are two further genes,
part of the genome and subject to crossover and mutation like every other
gene, but in the present implementation not read by the generator or the
fitness function, and therefore with no effect on any reported result. We
retain them in the genome because they are the intended interface for
graded, distress-score-driven escalation, listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The genome as implemented does not include a separate evolvable length-penalty
gene: the length penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} uses the fixed
coefficient $D=0.05$ throughout, rather than a per-genome value. An earlier
design considered introducing such a gene (denoted $\lambda$), but it was not
carried into the implementation, so we omit it from Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}
rather than list a gene the genetic algorithm never actually evolves.

\section{Distress Scoring and Penalty Terms}
\label{app:distress}

\subsection{The Direct Distress-Gating Penalty Term}
\label{app:distress-penalty}

Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
includes a further term, $-0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid}
\wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\}$, that penalises a mid-risk response lacking
escalation content when $h_i$ exceeds the \emph{fixed} threshold $\tau_h$
(not the $\gamma$-relaxed effective threshold). Because the effective
threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) \le \tau_h$ for any $\gamma \ge 0$, whenever
$h_i > \tau_h$ the escalation condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation} is
already satisfied regardless of $\gamma$; a correctly functioning,
untruncated generator should therefore always set $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ in
this regime, and $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ here can only arise from a downstream
generation failure (e.g.\ the response-part truncation described in
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget} and analysed for B1 in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) rather than from the evolved value of
$\gamma$ itself. This penalty term therefore does not supply direct
selection pressure on $\gamma$. The region in which $\gamma$'s value
actually changes escalation behaviour is $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) < h_i \le
\tau_h$, where $h_i \le \tau_h$ and the direct penalty above does not
apply; any selection pressure on $\gamma$ in that region operates only
indirectly, through the resulting change in the categorical safety score
between $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ (0.60) and $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ (0.85) for
mid-risk prompts whose distress score falls in that narrow band
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}). We have not separately quantified how many
mid-risk prompts in the dataset fall in this band at $\gamma^{\star}
\approx 0.058$, nor run a $\gamma=0$ ablation. The B2--B4 ablation (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) shows that changing $\gamma$ between 0.058 and 0.1 does not change fitness, but we do not assert a magnitude of benefit for the gate at $\gamma^{\star}$.

\subsection{The Marker-Stuffing Penalty on Distress Scores}
\label{app:stuffing}

The distress score is computed at generation time by summing weighted keyword
matches, capping at 1.0, and applying a small penalty for marker stuffing
(>3 distinct signals). This penalty is clinically questionable on its own
terms: discounting a score for matching ``too many'' markers is appropriate
for the empathy-marker-stuffing failure mode of
Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}, where markers reflect the
\emph{generator's} output and repetition signals gaming, but the same logic
does not transfer to a \emph{user's} distress signal, where several distinct
indicators (hopelessness, isolation, and self-harm language together)
plausibly warrant more concern rather than less.

We verified computationally that the penalty is nonetheless inert here. Its
trigger condition (more than three distinct matched keywords) is never
satisfied on any prompt entering the fitness pipeline --- the concatenated
\texttt{common\_themes}\,/\texttt{thought\_progression} text that
\texttt{compute\_distress\_score()} receives --- where the maximum number of
distinct matches is two. (Clinician \texttt{risk\_assessment} notes can match
four or more if scanned as free text, but those are used only for risk-label
construction, not as the distress-score input.) No escalation decision
reported in this paper is affected. This does not resolve the underlying
design concern: a scorer that cannot discount more severe presentations
would be preferable in future revisions.

\section{Equivalence Tests and Sensitivity Statistics}
\label{app:equivalence}

\subsection{Equivalence Testing Procedure}
\label{app:tost-method}

Equivalence was assessed with Schuirmann's two one-sided tests (TOST). The
primary analysis treats the two conditions as \emph{independent samples} and
uses the Welch unequal-variance $t$ statistic with Welch--Satterthwaite
degrees of freedom, at a pre-specified margin of $\varepsilon=0.01$ fitness
points and $\alpha=0.05$. Independent-samples Welch TOST is appropriate here
because each condition is run with its own five GA seeds and the conditions
differ markedly in seed-level dispersion (standard deviations from 0.0016 to
0.0073), so equal variances cannot be assumed.

Equivalence requires rejecting both one-sided nulls,
$H_0^{-}\colon \Delta \le -\varepsilon$ (an upper-tail test) and
$H_0^{+}\colon \Delta \ge +\varepsilon$ (a lower-tail test), where $\Delta$
is the difference in mean validation fitness between a condition and its
reference. The reported TOST $p$-value is
$\max(p_{\mathrm{lower}}, p_{\mathrm{upper}})$. Intervals are reported at the
$(1-2\alpha) = 90\%$ level, which is the level corresponding to the TOST
decision: equivalence holds exactly when the 90\% confidence interval lies
entirely inside $(-\varepsilon, +\varepsilon)$. A 95\% interval is
\emph{not} the correct reference interval for a TOST decision at
$\alpha=0.05$ and is not reported for these tests. Each condition
contributes five independent GA seeds, scored under the frozen pipeline
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen-scorer}). The implementation depends on SciPy only;
all nine tests were independently reproduced with a second, established
implementation as a numerical check.

\section{Human Evaluation Statistical Details}
\label{app:humaneval}

\subsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition (5 annotators $\times$
30 items) are not 150 independent observations: ratings from the same
annotator are correlated across items, and ratings of the same item
are correlated across annotators. Treating all 150 as independent, as
in a naive paired $t$-test on the raw annotator-item pairs, understates
the true uncertainty and can overstate significance. We therefore
report inference at the level of the 30 items, the genuine unit of
independent replication in this design, rather than at the level of
individual ratings.

For each dimension, we first average the 5 raters' scores for each
item and condition, then compute the paired difference (B4 minus B1)
across the resulting $n=30$ independent item-level pairs. This yields
a mean item-level improvement of $+1.68$ (Empathy), $+1.77$ (Safety),
and $+1.95$ (Helpfulness), matching the raw annotator-item means in
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval}, but now with correctly calibrated
uncertainty: 95\% confidence intervals of $[1.48, 1.88]$ (Empathy),
$[1.64, 1.89]$ (Safety), and $[1.83, 2.06]$ (Helpfulness), obtained
both from the normal approximation and from a 10{,}000-resample
percentile bootstrap over items, which agree closely with each other.
Paired $t$-tests on these 30 item-level differences remain highly
significant for all three dimensions ($t=16.51$, $p=2.8\times10^{-16}$
for Empathy; $t=28.07$, $p=1.4\times10^{-22}$ for Safety; $t=32.96$,
$p=1.5\times10^{-24}$ for Helpfulness), as do Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests run on the same item-level differences as a nonparametric
check ($p<10^{-5}$ in all three cases). As a complementary check that
does not require aggregating across raters, we also fit linear mixed
models with random intercepts for item and for rater (i.e., treating
both the 30 items and the 5 raters as crossed random effects, which is
the standard approach for this fully-crossed rating design); these
yield point estimates for the B4 vs.\ B1 effect that are numerically
identical to the item-level analysis ($+1.68$, $+1.77$, $+1.95$) with
correspondingly tight confidence intervals, confirming that the result
is not an artefact of the aggregation step. We report the item-level
analysis as primary in Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} because it depends
on fewer distributional assumptions and is directly reproducible by
hand from the 30 item-level means. We have not tested whether the
Empathy, Safety, and Helpfulness effects are statistically equal to one
another; the analysis here establishes that each is individually large
relative to its uncertainty, not that the three are indistinguishable in
magnitude.
