\abstract{Large language models are increasingly deployed in emotionally sensitive applications
such as mental-health support, where optimization must jointly consider empathy, safety, and
conversational structure. We present a genetic-algorithm (GA) framework for optimizing the
response-generation policy of \textit{Riri}, a Bangla/Banglish/English mental-health assistant.
Rather than updating model weights, the GA searches an interpretable genome parametrizing a
risk-aware, template-based generator requiring no LLM calls at deployment. A non-differentiable
fitness function combines automatic empathy, safety, and structure scores under per-example
safety penalties. Experiments on 2872 de-identified prompts, synthetically constructed from
structured clinical theme fields, show the GA improves structural completeness from 0.65 to
1.00 relative to a fixed hand-designed baseline while maintaining an already-strong
escalation-calibration safety proxy (0.903 to 0.905) and slightly increasing empathy. A
near-zero-structure, short-budget baseline illustrates a compounding failure mode. Validation
fitness remains within a tight, non-monotonic range across dataset sizes from 100 to 2297
training prompts, though formal equivalence was not established. Trace analysis surfaces
evolutionary failure modes that aggregate scores conceal. A grid search and an equal-budget
random search each reach comparable peak fitness, indicating a broadly reachable plateau rather
than a narrow optimum, though search reliability across repeated runs was not directly compared.}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as conversational agents in
domains such as mental-health support, coaching, and
counseling~\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots}. Deploying these systems
safely requires more than grammatical correctness or task accuracy; responses
must be empathetic, structurally helpful, and aligned with risk-aware safety
principles~\cite{jmir2024_empathic,apa2024_advisory,harvard2024_safety}.

Conventional fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
are powerful but expensive and tightly coupled to model weights. In contrast,
evolutionary algorithms (EAs) can optimize high-level, interpretable policies
using arbitrary non-differentiable
objectives~\cite{llm_ea_survey2025,when_llms_meet_ea2025}. In the context of
conversational agents, this makes them attractive for optimizing template-based
generators or routing strategies while keeping the underlying LLM
fixed~\cite{mles2026,gendl2025}.

Unlike prior evolutionary prompt optimization approaches that focus primarily on
reasoning accuracy, feature activation, or prompt engineering, the proposed
framework focuses on optimizing emotionally sensitive conversational behavior
under strong safety penalty terms. The system introduces risk-aware escalation,
progressive structure enforcement, and trace-level behavioral analysis for a
multilingual mental-health assistant. To the best of our knowledge, this is
among the first studies applying interpretable genetic optimization to
empathy--safety--structure balancing in Bangla mental-health conversational
systems.

In this article we introduce an evolutionary optimization framework for
\emph{Riri}, a mental-health-oriented assistant designed for Bangla-speaking
users. The system composes responses via a structured, template-based
generator that controls when to include empathy, grounding, action
suggestions, and reflective questions, without calling an external LLM at
inference time (Section~\ref{sec:generator}). A
genetic algorithm (GA) evolves a genome of continuous and discrete parameters
that govern this generator. The optimization objective aggregates scores for
empathy, safety, and structure, with safety considerations entering via
strong per-example penalty terms for suicide and
self-harm risk. From a methodological perspective, the work studies mixed
continuous--discrete, non-differentiable genetic optimization over an
interpretable policy space, with a scalarized multi-objective fitness
function and sensitivity analyses across dataset and population size.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A risk-aware GA formulation for mental-health response generation,
        including an interpretable genome, progressively structured generator,
        and a penalty-based fitness function incorporating safety considerations.
  \item An empirical study of GA convergence, data efficiency, and
        population-size sensitivity on a corpus of 2872 de-identified,
        synthetically constructed
        mental-health prompts with risk labels.
  \item A trace-based analysis of evolutionary failure modes (structural
        collapse, empathy overfitting) and demonstration of how revised scoring
        and safety penalty terms yield behaviorally meaningful responses.
  \item An empirical comparison of the GA against grid search and
        equal-budget random search, showing that the discovered high-fitness
        configuration sits on a broadly reachable plateau for this genome
        space and fitness design; this repositions the framework's central
        contribution around interpretable diagnosis of reward-design
        failure modes, rather than around evolutionary search being
        uniquely necessary to reach this performance level.
\end{itemize}

The novelty of this work lies less in evolutionary search being uniquely
necessary to reach a given fitness level -- Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}
shows that a grid search and an equal-budget random search both reach
comparable fitness -- and more in the interpretable genome, generator, and
scoring design providing a framework for discovering and diagnosing
reward-design failure modes (structural collapse, empathy-marker
stuffing, a genome-construction bug, and a response-truncation defect)
that aggregate fitness scores alone conceal. The framework combines
risk-aware escalation, progressive structure enforcement, and
trace-based failure analysis, and demonstrates how evolutionary search,
grid search, and random search each behave when optimizing value-laden
objectives such as empathy, safety, and structured support over the same
interpretable policy space. This equivalence between search strategies is
specific to a genome space with a deterministic structure threshold that
any sufficiently exploratory search can locate (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure});
genome spaces or fitness designs without such an easily-found shortcut,
where the interaction between genes is less separable, may show a
clearer advantage for guided evolutionary search over undirected
alternatives, and testing this is a natural direction for future work.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the dataset, genome, generator, and fitness
function.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Rather than framing this study around hypotheses to be confirmed or
rejected -- a framing that fits some of the following questions better
than others, given the confounded comparisons and formal equivalence
tests discussed in Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:interp-baseline} -- we pose four research questions that this
study investigates:

\textbf{RQ1:} Does GA optimisation improve response fitness compared with
fixed, hand-designed, and randomly sampled baseline genomes?

\textbf{RQ2:} How does progressive structure enforcement affect structural
completeness and the safety proxy, and can these effects be attributed
specifically to the GA's search rather than to other differences between
conditions?

\textbf{RQ3:} How sensitive is the optimisation outcome to dataset size
and population size?

\textbf{RQ4:} Does the GA reach high-fitness genome regions more reliably
than an equal-budget grid search or random search over the same genome
space?

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Evolutionary Algorithms for LLM and Prompt Optimization}

Recent research has increasingly explored the integration of Evolutionary
Algorithms (EAs) with Large Language Models (LLMs) for optimization, reasoning,
and controllable generation tasks. A growing body of work suggests that
evolutionary search provides a promising alternative to gradient-based alignment
methods, particularly in black-box API settings where direct model fine-tuning
is impractical.

The survey ``When Large Language Models Meet Evolutionary
Algorithms''~\cite{when_llms_meet_ea2025} establishes a conceptual bridge
between token-sequence generation in LLMs and population-based search in
evolutionary systems. The authors argue that prompts, policies, and language
structures can be treated as evolvable individuals within an optimization
landscape. Their framework motivates the use of mutation, crossover, and fitness
shaping in language-space optimization. The present work follows this paradigm
by treating conversational behavior policies as evolvable genomes composed of
interpretable parameters such as empathy weight, safety weight, structure
weight, escalation thresholds, and template configurations.

Similarly, Promptbreeder~\cite{epo2025} proposes a self-referential
prompt evolution framework in which mutation operators are themselves
evolved alongside the prompts. Promptbreeder demonstrates that genetic
search can discover diverse, high-performing prompts across reasoning
tasks without manual engineering. While Promptbreeder targets task
accuracy, the proposed framework extends the evolutionary optimization
paradigm toward safety-critical conversational behavior.

GAAPO~\cite{gaapo2025} further demonstrates the effectiveness of Genetic
Algorithms for automatic prompt optimization in LLM systems. Their work explores
mutation, crossover, selection strategies, and population hyperparameter
trade-offs across benchmark reasoning tasks such as ETHOS, MMLU-Pro, and GPQA.
Similar to GAAPO, the present study investigates population-size and
dataset-size sensitivity in evolutionary optimization. However, unlike GAAPO,
the proposed framework focuses on multi-objective optimization for mental-health
dialogue systems, balancing empathy, safety, structural completeness, and
conversational naturalness.

GenDLN~\cite{gendl2025} applies evolutionary optimization to stacked LLM
reasoning pipelines using non-differentiable objectives. The work demonstrates
that GA-based optimization can effectively operate over black-box API systems
without gradient access. However, GenDLN primarily targets logical reasoning and
classification performance, whereas the present work investigates emotionally
sensitive conversational optimization under safety constraints.

Another closely related direction is Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary
Search (MLES)~\cite{mles2026}, which combines LLM reasoning with evolutionary
optimization to generate interpretable programmatic control policies. MLES
emphasizes behavioral evidence and transparent policy evolution rather than
relying solely on scalar reward signals.

A recent systematic survey on LLMs for Evolutionary
Optimization~\cite{llm_ea_survey2025} categorizes hybrid EA-LLM systems into
low-level and high-level optimization frameworks. The proposed framework
does not fit neatly into either category, since it involves no LLM calls
at all, at training or deployment time (Section~\ref{sec:generator}): it is
evolutionary optimization of a template-based conversational policy, a
distinct and more inference-efficient point in the design space than the
LLM-in-the-loop systems this taxonomy was built to describe.

Existing EA+LLM research primarily focuses on reasoning accuracy,
feature activation, program synthesis, or prompt engineering; this work
extends that literature to risk-aware, multi-objective conversational
optimization in a mental-health setting.

\subsection{Mental-Health Conversational Agents}

Mental-health chatbots and conversational agents have received significant
attention in recent years due to their potential to provide scalable
psychological support. Systematic
reviews~\cite{jmir2025_chatbots,pmc2026_chatbots} suggest that generative
conversational agents can improve emotional accessibility and user engagement,
particularly among populations with limited access to professional mental-health
care.

Recent work on empathic conversational agent design~\cite{jmir2024_empathic}
highlights the importance of empathy, conversational structure, and safety
mechanisms in determining user trust and therapeutic effectiveness. These
findings directly motivate the three-dimensional optimization objective used in
the proposed framework: empathy, safety, and structural support.

Emotionally intelligent chatbot systems have also raised important ethical and
safety concerns~\cite{ijca2025emotion}, including data privacy, bias in
emotion recognition, and the risk of fostering emotional over-dependence,
underscoring the need for careful design of safety and escalation behaviour
rather than assuming it emerges from emotional responsiveness alone.

Other mental-health chatbot systems have relied primarily on decision-tree-based,
rule-based response generation~\cite{scitepress2025chatbot}. In contrast, the
proposed framework employs evolutionary optimization to dynamically shape
conversational behavior based on fitness feedback.

The American Psychological Association (APA) advisory on generative AI for
mental health~\cite{apa2024_advisory} further stresses the necessity of explicit
safety constraints and escalation policies in AI mental-health systems.

Empirical studies on the safety of generative AI chatbots in mental-health
contexts~\cite{harvard2024_safety} also demonstrate that conversational agents
may exhibit inconsistent crisis behavior when not explicitly constrained.

A comparative study of LLM-generated and human-therapist responses to
mental-health questions~\cite{humanai2024} found that LLM responses,
while grammatically coherent, consistently lacked the conversational
structure (e.g.\ dialogic follow-up questions) and emotional depth
present in human therapist responses, motivating explicit design
attention to both dimensions rather than assuming they emerge from
model scale alone.

\subsection{Empathy Modeling and Conversational Behavior Optimization}

Empathy modeling has become an increasingly important area within conversational
AI research. Surveys on artificial empathy
classification~\cite{empathysurvey2025} discuss various methods for evaluating
empathetic language using linguistic markers, neural scoring systems, and
psychological frameworks. The present work adopts a lightweight rubric-based
evaluation approach due to its interpretability and compatibility with black-box
optimization settings.

Neural empathetic conversational systems~\cite{empatheticagents2025} have
demonstrated strong performance in emotionally aware dialogue generation, but
these methods often require large-scale supervised training and expensive
fine-tuning pipelines. In contrast, the proposed framework explores whether
evolutionary search over interpretable conversational policies can improve
empathetic behavior without retraining the underlying LLM.

A recent survey of chatbot evolution, architecture, and design
techniques~\cite{chatbotevolution2024} traces the field's progression from
early rule-based, pattern-matching systems toward architectures that
combine retrieval, generation, and adaptive dialogue management,
reflecting a broader trend toward more context-sensitive conversational
design that the present framework's genome-driven approach also follows.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning}

Existing studies on evolutionary prompt optimization primarily focus on
improving reasoning performance, feature activation, or general task accuracy.
Similarly, prior mental-health conversational systems often emphasize either
empathy or safety independently, with limited attention to structural balance
and risk-aware escalation. There remains a gap in developing interpretable
optimization frameworks that jointly balance empathy, safety, structure, and
adaptive risk-sensitive behavior in multilingual mental-health support systems.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let \(X\) denote the space of user prompts and
\(R = \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}\) the set of
suicide/self-harm risk labels. A response-generation policy is a function
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{\theta} : X \times R \rightarrow Y,
\end{equation}
mapping a prompt \(x \in X\) with risk \(r \in R\) to a textual response
\(y \in Y\). Instead of optimizing LLM parameters directly, we parameterize
\(\pi_{\theta}\) via a higher-level generator \(G\) controlled by a genome
\(g \in \mathcal{G}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generator}
  y = G(x, r; g).
\end{equation}
The design goal is to find a genome \(g^{\star}\) that maximizes a scalar
fitness function \(F(g)\) combining empathy, safety, and structure, with
safety considerations entering as penalty terms within $F(g)$ itself
(Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) rather than as constraints that
render a genome infeasible:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
  g^{\star} = \arg\max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} F(g).
\end{equation}
We approximate~\eqref{eq:objective} using a GA operating on a finite set of
prompts.

\subsection{Dataset}

We use a dataset of \(N = 2872\) de-identified records collected from a
mental-health screening and support application in Bangladesh. Each record
originates as a set of structured, analyst-labeled fields --
\texttt{common\_themes} (e.g., ``loneliness $\vert$ desire for romantic
relationship $\vert$ emotional distress'') and \texttt{thought\_progression}
(e.g., ``initial greeting $\vert$ expressed loneliness and prolonged
duration $\vert$ desire for a girlfriend'') -- rather than as a raw,
free-text user message. These structured fields are concatenated into an
intermediate theme-text string, which is then passed through
\texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}, a function that uses keyword
matching against the theme text to select and assemble multilingual
(Bangla/Banglish/English) sentences into a synthetic, natural-language
prompt (e.g., ``I feel really alone these days,'' ``Ami onek lonely feel
kortesi lately,'' or the corresponding Bangla-script sentence), adding
closing phrases with some stochastic variation. The resulting synthetic
text is what we denote $x_i$ throughout this manuscript and pass to the
generator (Eq.~\eqref{eq:generator}) and to all baselines B1--B8 alike,
including the pre-trained-LLM comparisons (B6--B8, Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}):
\begin{equation}
  (x_i, r_i, t_i),
\end{equation}
where \(x_i\) is this synthetically constructed prompt in Bangla, Banglish,
or English;
\(r_i \in R\) is the risk label (low, mid, high), assigned from PHQ-4 score
and an explicit self-harm keyword override applied to a separate clinician
risk-assessment note (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) -- not from $x_i$'s
content; and \(t_i\) is a high-level
theme description (e.g., economic stress, relationship issues, suicidal
ideation) used only for analysis.

Because every prompt in this dataset is a synthetic reconstruction from
structured summary fields rather than a verbatim user message, we do not
claim ecological validity for the specific phrasing, code-switching
patterns, or conversational style of $x_i$: the multilingual variation
reflects the phrase banks used by \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}
rather than authentic Bangla-English code-switching as it naturally occurs
in user speech. What is preserved from the original clinical population is
the distribution of themes, risk levels, and demographic composition
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}), not the literal text users originally wrote.
This has a specific consequence for the risk-aware escalation mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}): for prompts labeled high-risk,
\texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()} deliberately selects
higher-intensity language consistent with that label, so the distress
score $h(x_i)$ -- itself computed from keyword matches on $x_i$ -- is not
fully independent of the risk label $r_i$ it is sometimes used alongside.
This does not affect the high/low risk categories, whose escalation is
governed directly by $r_i$, but it does mean $h(x_i)$'s apparent ability to
identify high-distress cases within the mid-risk category should not be
read as evidence that the mechanism would generalise to prompts whose
language was not constructed with the label already in hand (further
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

For some experiments we consider subsets of size
\(N_s \in \{100, 300, 600, 1200, 2872\}\) to study data efficiency. Each subset
is split into an optimization set and a held-out validation set using an
80/20 split, stratified by risk label so that the proportion of low-, mid-,
and high-risk prompts in the validation set matches the proportion in the
full subset. For the main experiment ($N_s = 2872$), this yields
approximately 2,297 optimization prompts and 575 validation prompts, with
fitness evaluation during GA optimisation performed on a random sample of
$|\mathcal{I}| = 600$ prompts drawn from the optimization set per generation
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}). Each row in the underlying dataset
corresponds to a distinct user: the raw collection of $N=3{,}279$ rows
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) has $3{,}279$ unique user identifiers with no
user contributing more than one row, and this one-row-per-user property is
preserved after filtering to the $N=2{,}872$ usable prompts used throughout
this work. The 80/20 split therefore partitions users rather than only
prompts: no user appears in both the optimisation and validation sets under
any split reported in this work, and no user-level leakage is possible.

\subsubsection{Risk-Label Construction}
\label{sec:risk-labels}

Each prompt's risk label $r_i \in \{\mathrm{low}, \mathrm{mid}, \mathrm{high}\}$
is derived from two signals: the prompt's PHQ-4 total score and the presence
of explicit self-harm/suicide language in an accompanying clinician-authored
risk-assessment note recorded at the point of data collection. The PHQ-4 is
a validated four-item screening instrument for depression and anxiety
severity~\cite{phq4}, with standard severity bands of Normal (0--2), Mild
(3--5), Moderate (6--8), and Severe (9--12) (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}).
PHQ-4 does not itself include a suicide or self-harm item, so it is not a
validated suicide-risk instrument on its own; we use its severity score as
one input to a risk label rather than as a direct measure of suicide risk.

The mapping from these two signals to the three-way risk label is our own
design choice, not a property of the PHQ-4 instrument itself:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-label}
  r_i =
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{high} & \text{explicit risk indicator present, or PHQ-4 total} \ge 9 \\
    \mathrm{mid}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \in [6, 8] \\
    \mathrm{low}  & \text{PHQ-4 total} \le 5
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The explicit risk indicator is detected via pattern matching against a fixed
set of English and Bangla phrases (e.g., ``suicide,'' ``kill myself,''
``hurt myself,'' ``end my life,'' ``unsafe,'' {\bengalifont আত্মহত্যা}) applied
to the risk-assessment note; a match escalates the label to \emph{high}
regardless of PHQ-4 score, since an explicit indicator is treated as taking
priority over screening-instrument severity. This escalation rule, and the
decision to retain explicit keyword detection as a parallel signal alongside
PHQ-4 severity, was proposed by the five clinical psychologists who later
served as evaluators in our human evaluation study
(Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}); the rationale given was that a PHQ-4 score
alone can understate acute risk in cases where a user discloses suicidal or
self-harm intent without this being reflected in elevated depression/anxiety
severity on the four PHQ-4 items.

The score-to-label mapping in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-label} and the keyword
list have not been formally validated against clinician-assigned labels on
a held-out sample, and the keyword override cannot handle negation,
hypothetical phrasing, or context-dependent meaning; we do not present the
resulting risk labels as equivalent in rigor to a clinician-assigned
ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Genome Representation}

An individual genome \(g\) is a vector of continuous and discrete genes,
matching the fields of the implementation's \texttt{Genome} data
structure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:genome}
  g = \bigl( w_e,\, w_s,\, w_c,\, \theta_{\mathrm{mid}},\,
             \theta_{\mathrm{high}},\, \gamma,\, d,\, m \bigr),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_e \in [0,1]\): empathy weight controlling the number and intensity
        of empathy lines.
  \item \(w_s \in [0,1]\): safety weight. In the present implementation it
        selects one of several internal safety-instruction annotations that
        are stripped before the response is finalised, so it does not
        currently influence the visible response text or any automatic score
        directly. It is not, however, entirely inert: $w_e$, $w_s$, and $w_c$
        are jointly renormalised to sum to 1 immediately after mutation and
        before the genome is used for generation (i.e.\ normalisation happens
        once, post-mutation, over all three weights together; see the note
        below). Because $w_e$ and $w_c$ are only ever read in their
        \emph{normalised} form, $w_s$'s raw value changes the normalising
        denominator and therefore indirectly changes the effective
        $w_e$ and $w_c$ that reach the generator, even though $w_s$'s own
        normalised value has no further downstream effect. $w_s$ should
        therefore not be read as a direct safety control, nor is it
        fitness-neutral in the way $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and
        $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}
        returns to this distinction); crisis-language inclusion itself is
        governed directly by the risk label
        (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}).
  \item \(w_c \in [0,1]\): structure weight controlling inclusion of grounding,
        action, and questions.
  \item \(\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}, \theta_{\mathrm{high}} \in [0,1]\): two
        escalation-threshold genes, currently inactive and reserved for
        future graded escalation; see Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}
        for details.
  \item \(\gamma \in [0, 0.2]\): distress-gating parameter. Unlike
        $\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$, $\gamma$ is
        read directly by the generation pipeline: it
        sets the effective distress threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma)$ used to
        decide mid-risk escalation (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation},
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation}), and is therefore an active,
        functional gene rather than a reserved one. Its relationship to the
        fitness function's direct distress-gating penalty term is more
        limited than it may first appear; see the caveat in
        Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}.
  \item \(d\): discrete template selector (implemented as the field
        \texttt{p\_id}), indexing a small set of response templates.
  \item \(m \in \{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\): response-part budget (implemented as
        the integer field \texttt{memory\_window}). Despite the
        implementation name, $m$ is \emph{not} a conversational context
        window: the present generator is single-turn and does not retain
        dialogue history. Instead, $m$ is a categorical gene that gates a
        binary output-component cap after response assembly
        (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}). Only the threshold
        $m \le 256$ is behaviourally active; the three larger values are
        currently equivalent (no truncation).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Note on renormalisation and $w_s$.} $w_e, w_s, w_c$ are renormalised
to sum to 1 after mutation, so $w_s$ participates in this shared
denominator but has no independently meaningful effect as a ``safety
weight''; see Appendix~\ref{app:renorm} for the full derivation. We
accordingly avoid describing $w_s^{\star}=0.101$ as reflecting an evolved
safety preference (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}). The genome also
omits a separate evolvable length-penalty gene; the length penalty in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} uses the fixed coefficient $D=0.05$
throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:renorm}).

To establish a meaningful performance reference, we define three baseline
conditions. The minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) instantiates the generator with
minimum empathy and structure weights and a safety weight absorbing the
remainder ($w_e = 0.10$, $w_c = 0.10$, $w_s = 0.80$; these already sum to
1, so genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged and $w_c$ genuinely
remains in the bottom, no-structure regime, Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
a short
response-part budget $m = 256$ (truncating assembly to four parts; see
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}), and maximally conservative escalation thresholds
($\theta_\mathrm{mid} = 0.70$, $\theta_\mathrm{high} = 0.95$), representing
response generation with no parameter optimisation. B1's short response-part
budget additionally interacts with the fixed assembly order to truncate
escalation content for high-risk prompts before it reaches the visible
response, independent of its genome weights; we treat B1 primarily as an
illustrative floor condition for this reason and rely on B2 as the more
informative non-GA comparator (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). The fixed seed baseline
(B2) uses the balanced initialisation genome $g_\mathrm{bal}$ without
evolutionary search, isolating the contribution of the search process itself.
The random baseline (B3) averages five genomes sampled uniformly from the
genome space, establishing a chance-level reference.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Generator}
\label{sec:generator}

The generator \(G\) composes responses from several component banks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Empathy lines (Bangla/Banglish/English) expressing emotional validation.
  \item Grounding instructions (e.g., breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 sensory exercise).
  \item Action suggestions (e.g., contact trusted friend, seek professional
        help).
  \item Reflective questions encouraging further disclosure.
\end{itemize}

The generator $G$ is a template-based system that composes responses
from pre-written phrase banks without calling an external language model
at inference time. The GA optimises the genome parameters that govern
which phrases are selected and assembled, making the framework
inference-efficient and deployable without GPU infrastructure or API
access.

\subsubsection{Response-Part Budget and Truncation}
\label{sec:part-budget}

The generator assembles each reply as an ordered list of \emph{parts}
(strings), typically beginning with two internal tags
(\texttt{[TEMPLATE]}, \texttt{[SAFETY]}), followed by one or more empathy
lines, optional escalation content, and optional structure lines
(grounding, action, question). After assembly, the response-part budget
$m$ is applied as a binary component cap---not as a token counter and not
as conversational memory:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:part-budget}
  \mathrm{parts} \leftarrow
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{parts}[:4] & \text{if } m \le 256 \text{ and } |\mathrm{parts}| > 4, \\
    \mathrm{parts} & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Internal tags are then stripped to produce the visible response. When
$m \le 256$, the two tags occupy the first two of the four retained slots,
leaving at most two visible content lines; later parts (including
escalation and structure) can therefore be dropped before scoring. Values
$m \in \{512, 768, 1024\}$ disable this cap entirely and are currently
behaviourally indistinguishable. The gene remains evolvable because B1 is
initialised at $m=256$ while seed and GA genomes typically use
$m \ge 512$, and the resulting truncation interaction is a material driver
of the B1--B4 safety gap (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). We retain the
implementation field name \texttt{memory\_window} for code compatibility,
but refer to $m$ as a response-part budget throughout this manuscript to
avoid implying multi-turn context retention.

\subsubsection{Progressive Structure Enforcement}
\label{sec:progressive-structure}

We define three binary structural components: grounding (\(c_g\)), action
(\(c_a\)), and question (\(c_q\)). Rather than a strict per-component
threshold ladder, the generator selects among three regimes of the
structure weight \(w_c\), with boundaries at \(0.25\) and \(0.55\):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(w_c < 0.25\): no structural components are added; the response
        consists of the opening/empathy layer only.
  \item \(0.25 \le w_c < 0.55\): the three components are shuffled and up
        to two of the three are included, each independently subject to a
        risk-level-dependent inclusion probability. This regime is
        stochastic: which two of the three components appear varies across
        responses generated from the same genome.
  \item \(w_c \ge 0.55\): all three components are included deterministically,
        i.e. \(c_g = c_a = c_q = 1\).
\end{itemize}
This is a coarser, more heuristic mechanism than a smooth per-component
threshold function, but it reproduces the qualitative behaviour the genome
is intended to capture: as \(w_c\) increases, the response transitions from
minimal to full structure, with full structure guaranteed once \(w_c\) is
large enough. The reported genome \(w_c^{\star} = 0.768\)
(Section~\ref{sec:hyperparams}) falls in the top regime, which is why the
worked example above activates all three structural components
deterministically rather than probabilistically.

\subsubsection{Risk-Aware Escalation}
\label{sec:risk-escalation}

Crisis-style escalation content is included if the prompt's risk label is high,
\textit{or} if a continuous distress score $h(x_i)$ exceeds a learned threshold
and the risk label is mid:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:risk-escalation}
    \text{escalate}(x_i, r_i, h_i) = \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{high} \} \vee
    \mathbb{I}\{ r_i = \mathrm{mid} \land h_i > \tau_h - \gamma \cdot h_i \},
\end{equation}
where $h(x_i) \in [0,1]$ is a distress score computed from prompt keywords
(suicide ideation, self-harm, hopelessness, isolation, severe overwhelm),
$\tau_h = 0.65$ is the distress threshold, and $\gamma \in [0, 0.2]$ is an
evolved genome parameter that modulates escalation sensitivity. Rearranging,
escalation for a mid-risk prompt requires $h_i > \tau_h / (1+\gamma)$:
increasing $\gamma$ lowers the effective distress threshold below $\tau_h$,
allowing earlier escalation to crisis language without changing the formal
risk label.

\emph{On the direct distress-gating penalty term.} A further fitness term
penalises mid-risk responses lacking escalation content when the distress
score exceeds the fixed threshold $\tau_h$; however, this term does not
supply direct selection pressure on $\gamma$, since escalation is already
guaranteed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation} whenever that fixed threshold
is exceeded, regardless of $\gamma$'s value. Any real selection pressure
on $\gamma$ instead operates indirectly, through the categorical safety
score in the narrow band where $\gamma$ actually changes escalation
behaviour. See Appendix~\ref{app:distress-penalty} for the full argument.

Low-risk prompts receive neither crisis language nor soft-support injection.
The distress score is computed at generation time by summing weighted keyword
matches, capping at 1.0, with a small penalty for marker stuffing
(>3 distinct signals). We verified computationally that this stuffing
penalty is clinically questionable in principle but empirically inert on
this dataset -- it never fires on any prompt that actually enters the
fitness pipeline (Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing}). This mechanism
allows the GA to learn
when escalation should trigger \textit{within} a risk category, rather than
only which category a prompt falls into. The GA converged to a small evolved
$\gamma$ ($\gamma^{\star} \approx 0.059$), indicating that only a modest
relaxation of the fixed distress threshold $\tau_h$ was found beneficial;
this suggests the fixed threshold already captures most of the useful
escalation signal for this dataset, with the learned gating mechanism
providing a secondary, fine-grained adjustment rather than a large
behavioural shift.

\subsection{Automatic Scoring}
\label{sec:automatic-scoring}

Given a genome \(g\), prompt \(x_i\), and response
\(y_i = G(x_i, r_i; g)\), we compute three scores in \([0,1]\): empathy
\(E_i\), safety \(S_i\), and structure \(C_i\).

\subsubsection{Empathy Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:empathy}
  E_i = \min\!\left( 1,\;
    \frac{1}{K_e} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_e} \mathbb{I}\{ m \in y_i \}
  \right) - \beta R_i^{\mathrm{rep}},
\end{equation}
where \(K_e\) is the target number of distinct markers, \(R_i^{\mathrm{rep}}\)
is a repetition ratio, and \(\beta\) is a small penalty coefficient.
In practice, empathy markers are grouped into four categories
(validation, reflection, normalisation, and warmth) with
category-specific weights of 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, and 0.15
respectively, summing to a maximum of 0.90 before length bonuses.

\subsubsection{Safety Score}

Rather than a continuous decomposition into risk-appropriate-guidance and
unsafe-content components, the implemented safety score is a categorical
lookup keyed on the risk label and a single boolean, \(\text{esc}_i\),
indicating whether the response contains at least one escalation-language
marker:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:safety}
  S_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.00 & r_i = \mathrm{high},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.85 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 1 \\
    0.60 & r_i = \mathrm{mid},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    1.00 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 0 \\
    0.75 & r_i = \mathrm{low},\ \text{esc}_i = 1
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These are the same values reported in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail};
there is no separate continuous \(S_i^{+}\)/\(S_i^{-}\) decomposition, and no
\(\gamma\) or \(\eta\) parameter, in the implemented scoring function.

The mid-risk safety score of approximately 0.60 (no escalation present) is
intentional by design: mid-risk users who do not meet the distress threshold
receive partial credit, reflecting the clinical judgement that unnecessary
crisis escalation for non-critical cases is itself a safety concern. We note
that this design means \emph{every} mid-risk response is capped below the
0.94 safety soft-threshold (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring-detail}) and
so incurs the associated $-0.15$ penalty regardless of response quality,
since 0.85 (the best attainable mid-risk score) is itself below 0.94; the
soft threshold as currently set cannot distinguish an acceptable from an
excellent mid-risk response (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection{Structure Score}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:structure}
  C_i =
  \begin{cases}
    0.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 0 \\
    0.20 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 1 \\
    0.60 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 2 \\
    1.00 & c_g^{(i)}+c_a^{(i)}+c_q^{(i)} = 3
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is a discrete lookup on the number of structural components detected in
the response text (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}), rather than a
continuous average. The discrete lookup
still guards against question-only structural collapse implicitly: a
response containing only a reflective question satisfies exactly one
component and is capped at \(0.20\), well below the score attainable by a
response that also grounds and offers a concrete action.

\subsubsection{Length Penalty and Per-Example Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:per-example}
  L_i = \max\!\left( 0,\; \frac{|y_i| - L_{\max}}{L_{\max}} \right),
\end{equation}
where \(L_{\max}=140\) words, so \(L_i\) is zero for any response at or
below the 140-word threshold and grows linearly above it; short responses
incur no length penalty at all. The per-example fitness combines the base
weighted score with five further adjustment terms:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:per-example-fitness}
    f_i(g) = {}& A\,E_i + B\,S_i + C\,C_i - D\,L_i \notag\\
      & - 0.50\,\mathbb{I}\{r_i=\mathrm{high} \wedge S_i<1.0\}
        - 0.15\,\mathbb{I}\{S_i<0.94\} \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.70-C_i)}{0.70}
        - 0.18\,\frac{\max(0,\,0.62-E_i)}{0.62} \notag\\
      & + 0.05\,E_i + 0.05\,C_i + 0.05\,\min(C_i,\,0.8) \notag\\
      & - 0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid} \wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\},
\end{align}

where $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are the fixed fitness-weight coefficients reported in
Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring} ($A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$).
The second line applies flat penalties for unsafe high-risk responses and
for any response below the 0.94 safety threshold (both can apply to the
same example, e.g.\ a high-risk response without escalation incurs a
combined \(-0.65\)). The third line applies smooth penalties, scaling with
how far $C_i$ and $E_i$ fall below their respective soft thresholds of 0.70
and 0.62. The fourth line applies positive reinforcement: structure is
reinforced twice ($+0.05\,C_i$ and $+0.05\,\min(C_i,0.8)$) while empathy is
reinforced once ($+0.05\,E_i$) and safety receives no reinforcement beyond
its base term. This asymmetry is a deliberate design choice reflected in the
implementation rather than an oversight, and we return to its consequences
for the converged genome in Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}. The final
line is the direct distress-gating penalty discussed critically above
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}), which we now describe only as an
additional (largely inactive, per that discussion) safety-adjacent penalty
rather than as a mechanism that directly selects for $\gamma$.

\subsubsection{Fitness Weight Rationale}
\label{sec:fitness-rationale}

The fixed coefficients $A=0.40$, $B=0.40$, $C=0.15$, $D=0.05$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
were determined via manual inspection on a held-out development set (100 prompts, stratified by
risk level) prior to GA initialization, reflecting clinical priorities identified with the five
collaborating psychologists: empathy and safety are weighted equally and most heavily ($A=B=0.40$), since clinical
feedback indicated both are foundational and supportive responses lacking safety can cause harm;
structure receives a smaller weight ($C=0.15$) as a supporting rather than primary
role; and the length penalty is minimal ($D=0.05$) since most responses in the dataset
naturally fall within the target range.

\subsubsection{Global Fitness}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:global}
  F(g) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} f_i(g).
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:global} is used with two distinct evaluation sets
$\mathcal{I}$ throughout this manuscript, and we distinguish them
explicitly to avoid conflating them: training-time fitness
$F_{\mathrm{train},t}(g) = \frac{1}{600}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_t} f_i(g)$
is computed once per generation $t$ over a random $|\mathcal{I}_t|=600$
sample drawn from the optimisation set and is
what the GA uses as its selection signal; validation fitness
$F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{575}\sum_{i \in V} f_i(g)$ is computed
once, after search, over the fixed, disjoint 575-prompt held-out
validation set $V$, and is what Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and
the significance analysis (Table~\ref{tab:significance}) report. The two
use the same per-example fitness formula but different, non-overlapping
prompt populations, and are not directly interchangeable. Note that all penalty and reinforcement terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
are applied per example, before averaging. There is no separate global
override that eliminates a genome outright based on any single unsafe
example; a genome whose responses are frequently unsafe is instead driven
toward low mean fitness by the accumulation of per-example penalties across
the evaluation sample, which has a similar aggregate effect to a hard
constraint without being implemented as one.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm Configuration}
\label{sec:ga-config}

We employ a mixed continuous/discrete GA with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Population size \(P \in \{30, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400\}\).
  \item Generations $T = 20$.
  \item Tournament selection of size~3.
  \item Uniform crossover for every gene: each child gene is copied from
        parent~$a$ or parent~$b$ with equal probability (including the
        continuous weights and the discrete genes \(d\) and \(m\)).
  \item Per-gene mutation with probability $p_m = 0.30$: continuous genes
        (\(w_e, w_s, w_c, \theta_{\mathrm{mid}}, \theta_{\mathrm{high}},
        \gamma\)) receive additive uniform noise clipped to their feasible
        ranges; discrete \(d\) is resampled from \(\{0,1,2\}\); the
        response-part budget \(m\) is resampled categorically from
        \(\{256, 512, 768, 1024\}\) (integer-coded, but not mutated as a
        continuous integer).
  \item Elitism: the top \(E\) individuals are copied to the next generation.
\end{itemize}
Five independent runs with different random seeds are performed per setting;
we report mean and standard deviation of validation fitness, with
per-generation best-individual genomes logged and archived alongside
fitness (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).

\subsection{Seeded Initialization}

Instead of random initialization, we use four seed genomes:
\begin{align}
  g_{\mathrm{bal}}  &: \text{balanced empathy, safety, and structure,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{emp}}  &: \text{empathy-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{str}}  &: \text{structure-heavy,} \\
  g_{\mathrm{safe}} &: \text{safety-heavy.}
\end{align}
These seeds are perturbed with small Gaussian noise to form the initial
population.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Dataset Size Sensitivity}
\label{sec:dataset-size}

The dataset size experiment evaluated whether increasing the number of training
samples improved validation fitness, using nested training subsets
($N=100 \subset N=300 \subset N=600 \subset N=1200 \subset N=2297$, the
full optimisation set) so that smaller sizes are literal subsets of larger
ones rather than independently drawn splits, with five GA seeds run per
size under the same frozen, corrected scorer used throughout this
manuscript (Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}). As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-size}, mean validation fitness across the five
sizes ranges narrowly from 0.698 ($N=300$) to 0.704 ($N=600$), with no
monotonic relationship to dataset size: the highest mean fitness occurs at
an intermediate size ($N=600$), not at the largest ($N=2297$: 0.700) or
smallest ($N=100$: 0.701) size tested. Standard deviations across the five
seeds per size range from 0.0017 ($N=1200$) to 0.0073 ($N=600$).

We formally tested equivalence to the full-dataset condition using
TOST at a pre-specified margin of $\varepsilon=0.01$ fitness points; none
of the four comparisons established equivalence at this margin, most
plausibly reflecting limited statistical power at five seeds per condition
rather than a genuine practically important difference (full statistics in
Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-tost}). Descriptively,
however, the total spread across all tested sizes (a 0.0068-point band,
with a maximum difference from the full-dataset condition of 0.005) is an
order of magnitude smaller than the gap between any GA-optimised condition
and the non-GA baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}, e.g.\ B4 vs.\
B2: 0.147 points), and shows no trend indicating that additional data
beyond $N=100$ improves fitness in this setting. We treat this as
practically suggestive of data efficiency rather than as formally
established equivalence (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Population Size Sensitivity}
\label{sec:population-size}

Figure~\ref{fig:population-size} shows validation fitness across population
sizes ranging from $P=30$ to $P=400$, five GA seeds per size, under the
same frozen, corrected scorer. Mean fitness ranges narrowly from 0.699
($P=300$) to 0.703 ($P=400$, the largest population tested), with no
monotonic relationship to population size: $P=100$ and $P=50$ both give
essentially identical means (0.7006), while the highest mean occurs at the
largest $P$ tested rather than at an intermediate value. Standard
deviations range from 0.0016 ($P=100$) to 0.0047 ($P=50$) and do not
decrease monotonically with $P$.

We formally tested equivalence to the $P=100$ condition using the same
TOST procedure; none of the five comparisons established equivalence at
this margin, for the same power-and-margin reasons as the dataset-size
result (full statistics in Appendix~\ref{app:population-tost}). As above,
we do not claim formal statistical equivalence, but descriptively these
differences are small relative to the GA-vs-baseline gaps reported in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and show no systematic trend with
population size once $P \ge 30$. Since larger populations incur
substantially higher computational cost with no corresponding fitness
benefit apparent in these
results, the practical recommendation remains to prefer smaller populations
(e.g., $P=50$--$100$) for computational efficiency, though we note this
recommendation rests on the same descriptive rather than formally
equivalence-tested basis as the dataset-size finding above.

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}

The convergence behaviour of the GA, from the main run under the frozen,
corrected scorer described in Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}
($P=100$, $T=20$, $|\mathcal{I}|=600$), is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:fitness-curve}. The best (training-time) fitness begins
at 0.701 in generation~1 and fluctuates within a narrow band of approximately
0.698--0.704 for the remainder of the run, with no sustained secondary
improvement phase; the single highest value observed (0.704, generation~19)
is only marginally above the initial value. Average fitness rises from 0.362
(generation~1) to approximately 0.60--0.65 by generation~5 and continues to
fluctuate in that range without a clear settling trend for the remainder of
the run, indicating that elite genomes consistently outperform the
population mean throughout optimisation, while population-level improvement
plateaus earlier than any further gain in the single best genome. Note that
these training-time fitness values, on a randomly sampled $|\mathcal{I}|=600$
subset of the optimisation set for each generation, differ both in scale
and in the underlying prompt sample from the validation-set scores reported
in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}.

The near-optimal generation-1
best fitness is attributable to a specific individual, not to the balanced
seed genome $g_\mathrm{bal}$: per-generation genome logs show
the generation-1 best individual is already structurally similar to the
eventual reported genome, consistent with a descendant of the
structure-heavy seed $g_\mathrm{str}$ rather than $g_\mathrm{bal}$ (full
genome values in Appendix~\ref{app:gen1}). This supports the
broader point that seeded initialisation (four hand-designed seeds
spanning different regions of the genome space, rather than a fully random
population) accelerates convergence toward a high-structure region, via
$g_\mathrm{str}$-like individuals reaching high fitness quickly.

\subsection{Population Diversity}
\label{sec:diversity}

Figure~\ref{fig:diversity-curve} shows fitness dispersion across
generations, measured as the variance of the population's fitness values
rather than a direct measure of genome (genotype) diversity; we adopt this
more precise name after noting that fitness variance can behave
differently from genuine genome-space diversity in a landscape containing
inactive genes ($\theta_\mathrm{mid}$, $\theta_\mathrm{high}$), threshold
effects (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}), and behaviourally
equivalent categorical values (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}). Variance
begins at approximately 0.096 in generation~1, reflecting the mixed
population of seed genomes and random initialisations. It fluctuates
between approximately 0.030 and 0.096 across the run without a sustained
downward trend. This pattern is consistent with a fitness landscape in
which many distinct genome configurations achieve similar fitness values;
confirming whether this also reflects genuine sustained genome-level
diversity (e.g.\ via per-gene variance or pairwise genome distance) rather
than fitness-score clustering alone is listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{MSGA-Seeded Initialization Comparison}
\label{sec:msga}

To evaluate whether principled domain-coverage seeding can substitute for
manually designed seed genomes, we implemented the Multiple Seeds Based
Genetic Algorithm (MSGA)~\cite{kabir2017msga} as an alternative initialization
strategy (condition B5). MSGA partitions the genome search space into $m = 4$
domains based on empirical distance boundaries computed from 500 randomly
sampled genomes, maintains an archive of size 25 per domain, and seeds the
initial population from these archives to ensure broad coverage of the fitness
landscape.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} includes the B5 results alongside B1--B4.
B5 was evaluated under the same frozen, corrected scorer as
B1--B4, in the same pipeline run, with its final genome archived
(\texttt{best\_genome\_B5.json}).
The MSGA-seeded system achieves empathy 0.439, safety 0.900, structure
1.000, and overall fitness 0.695, closely matching B4's 0.703
(Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}). We read this as evidence
that the GA converges to a similar high-fitness region regardless of
manually designed domain seeds (B4) versus archive-based domain-coverage
seeds (B5), and that MSGA-style initialization is a viable alternative
to manual seed design, removing the need for domain expertise in constructing
the initial seed set. Because B5 was run as a single seed rather than
across five independent runs, we do not report a paired significance test
or bootstrap interval for the B4-vs-B5 comparison specifically; extending
B5 to a multi-seed comparison on the same footing as
Table~\ref{tab:significance} remains a natural next step and is listed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Characterising Pre-Trained Bangla LLMs in Zero-Shot Mode (B6--B8)}
\label{sec:llm-baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} also includes three pre-trained Bangla
language models evaluated in zero-shot mode on a fixed 400-prompt subsample
of the same 575-prompt validation set used for B1--B5 (drawn with the same
random seed, $\mathrm{seed}=7$): mT5-small~\cite{mt5_2021} (B6),
BanglaT5~\cite{banglat5_2022} (B7), and BanglaGPT~\cite{banglagpt_2023} (B8).
The subsample, rather than the full 575 prompts, was used to limit the
computational cost of beam-search decoding three separate models; because it
is drawn from the same validation split with the same seed rather than a
different or independently-drawn set, B6--B8 scores remain directly
comparable to B1--B5 on the same underlying prompt population. We frame
B6--B8 explicitly as a
\emph{characterisation} of off-the-shelf Bangla LLM behaviour on this task,
not as a competing system: none of the three received task-specific
fine-tuning, in-context examples, or the template structure that defines
EvoRiri, so the comparison is not a like-for-like contest between an
LLM-based and a non-LLM-based approach. These models received natural
language prompts constructed by the same \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()}
function used in B1--B5, providing a fair input format comparison given this
characterisation goal.

All three models fail substantially on this task. B6 (mT5-small) produces
only sentinel tokens (\texttt{<extra\_id\_0>}) because mT5-small is a
masked span-filling model not designed for open-ended generation; it
scores zero on empathy and structure. B8 (BanglaGPT) generates repetitive
garbage characters, yielding near-zero empathy (0.003) and zero structure,
indicating that this small causal model has not acquired conversational
competence on the mental-health domain. B7 (BanglaT5) produces the highest
scores among the three (empathy 0.064, structure 0.094, fitness 0.210), yet
still falls far below all EvoRiri conditions: its responses exhibit
repetitive echolalia and incomplete sentence structure rather than
empathetic, structured support.

The fitness gap between B4 (0.703) and the best pre-trained LLM (B7: 0.210)
is $0.703 - 0.210 = 0.493$ points. We read this as characterising the gap
between generic zero-shot Bangla generation and a purpose-built, optimised
generator, rather than as evidence that EvoRiri outperforms LLM-based
approaches in general. A fine-tuned or carefully prompted larger LLM might
close much of this gap, and that comparison is left to future work.
EvoRiri's advantage over B6--B8 as measured here stems not from model scale
but from the combination of an interpretable risk-aware generator and
genetic optimisation of its response policy, evaluated under the same
scoring pipeline used throughout this study.

\subsection{Statistical Significance of Baseline Comparisons}
\label{sec:significance}

To assess whether the performance improvement of the GA-optimised system is
statistically reliable, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank
test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} across five independent runs with different random
seeds, with each run's final genome archived (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}
lists the manifest). For each run, we
re-scored the archived GA genome for that seed, together with the fixed
B1 and B2 genomes, under the frozen corrected structure scorer
($N_{\mathrm{eval}}=400$ on the seed-7 validation split).
Every number in this section and in
Table~\ref{tab:significance} is a directly observed result from that
re-score of an archived genome, with no imputed scalar correction applied
across seeds.

The GA consistently outperformed both baselines across all five runs
(Table~\ref{tab:significance}). The one-sided Wilcoxon test confirms that the
GA-optimised system achieves higher fitness than both B1 ($W = 15$,
$p = 0.031$) and B2 ($W = 15$, $p = 0.031$). We note that with only $n=5$
paired observations, $p=0.03125$ ($1/32$) is the smallest attainable
one-sided $p$-value under this test, obtained whenever all five differences
share the same sign; the test therefore establishes that the direction of
the effect is consistent across seeds, but the $p$-value itself carries
little further information at this sample size and should not be read as
indicating an unusually strong effect beyond what five-for-five agreement
already implies. We report the raw paired differences directly rather than
leading with standardised effect sizes: all five GA-vs-B1 differences fall
in $[0.898, 0.933]$ (median $0.912$), and all five GA-vs-B2 differences
fall in $[0.138, 0.161]$ (median $0.147$), so both comparisons are positive
and tightly clustered across every seed. Bootstrap 95\% confidence
intervals (10{,}000 resamples over the five paired differences, percentile
method) are $[0.904, 0.928]$ for GA vs.\ B1 and $[0.141, 0.154]$ for GA
vs.\ B2, both well clear of zero. We also computed Cohen's $d$ (59.1 for
GA vs.\ B1, 16.3 for GA vs.\ B2) for continuity with the effect-size
reporting convention, but flag that these values are not meaningfully
interpretable using ordinary Cohen's $d$ benchmarks at this sample size:
with only five paired differences, $d$ is dominated by the very small
variance \emph{among} those five differences rather than by the
underlying effect in the way $d$ is normally intended to summarise. Given the tightly
clustered, uniformly positive raw differences, we treat the direction and
approximate magnitude of the improvement over both baselines as
well-supported by this analysis, while
acknowledging that a larger number of seeds (e.g., 20--30) would still be preferable for a
more conventional significance test and a tighter estimate of run-to-run
variability; we list this alongside the other statistical
recommendations in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

quality over the fixed and random baseline genomes, confirmed under the same frozen,
corrected scorer used throughout Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}.

Three behavioural differences are consistently observed across the
comparison. First, B1 responses are, in the modal case, structurally
absent: no grounding instructions, no actionable guidance, and no
reflective questions, consisting of an empathy line and a reflection line
only, consistent with $w_c=0.10$ placing B1 in the bottom, no-structure
regime (Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}). B1's structure score is
correspondingly near-zero but not exactly zero (0.017,
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}); we attribute the small residual to
occasional edge cases in response assembly or scoring rather than to any
deliberate structural inclusion.
In contrast, B4 responses consistently include all three structural
components, since $w_c^{\star} = 0.768$ falls in the top, deterministic
regime, with each component serving a distinct therapeutic function:
grounding exercises anchor the user in the present moment, action
suggestions provide concrete next steps, and reflective questions invite
further disclosure.

Second, escalation behaviour differs substantially by risk level
in B4 but not in B1, for a reason distinct from a simple
difference in generation rules. The generator's escalation rule
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) applies unconditionally to both
B1 and B4 whenever the risk label is high, independent of genome
weight; the difference visible in Table~\ref{tab:baseline_vs_optimized}
instead arises from B1's minimum response-part budget ($m=256$)
truncating escalation content before it reaches the visible response,
via the mechanism traced in detail in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}.
B4's reported budget ($m=512$) disables this truncation entirely
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:part-budget}), so its escalation content survives. B4
accordingly suppresses explicit crisis language for low-risk prompts
(where no escalation content is generated in the first place) while
reliably surfacing escalation guidance (``contact a mental health
professional,'' ``local emergency or helpline support'') for
high-risk prompts. This risk-differentiation is therefore a
consequence of both the generator's fixed escalation rule and B1's
truncation defect rather than of the rule alone; it does not depend on
the genome's $\theta_\mathrm{mid}$ or $\theta_\mathrm{high}$ values,
which are not read by the generator in the present implementation.
Consequently, the reported B1-to-B4 gains here and in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} combine genuine GA
optimisation with the effect of not triggering this defect, rather
than genetic search alone (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}).

Third, B4 responses demonstrate multilingual composition,
weaving Bangla, Banglish, and English phrases within
a single response across functionally distinct components (grounding,
action, and question); as with the prompts themselves
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), this multilingual variation reflects the
generator's hand-authored phrase banks rather than a claim about
authentic code-switching patterns in real conversations. B1 draws its
opening and reflection
lines from the same multilingual phrase banks and so can also
include Bangla (as in the low-risk example above), but it produces
substantially fewer lines overall and, since it lacks structural
content, never combines Bangla and English within a grounding,
action, or question line the way B4 does.

These qualitative differences directly explain the quantitative
gaps reported in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}: the more than 58-fold
structure improvement (0.017 to 1.000), the safety gap (0.472 to
0.905), and the empathy improvement (0.269 to 0.430) all correspond
to observable, interpretable differences in response content, though
as discussed above the safety and structure gaps specifically over B1
partly reflect the truncation defect rather than genetic optimisation
alone.

The empathy gain is worth flagging on its own terms: a $+0.161$
absolute change (60\% relative) is modest next to the more than 58-fold
structure gain and the near-doubling of safety, and it is the smallest
of the three automatic-metric improvements by a wide margin. Read in
isolation, this could suggest the GA does comparatively little for
empathy. Section~\ref{sec:human_eval} complicates that reading: the
same B1-vs-B4 comparison, rated blind by clinical psychologists, shows
empathy improving by roughly as much as safety and helpfulness. We
return to this apparent conflict, and what it implies about the
automatic empathy proxy, in Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human_eval}

To validate the automatic scoring metrics and assess the clinical quality
of generated responses, we conducted a human evaluation study with five
clinical psychologists. We note at the outset two design limitations that
qualify the strength of this evaluation, discussed further in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: first, the comparison evaluated is B1 (which,
as documented in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}, carries a truncation
defect rather than only unoptimised weights) versus B4, rather than the
arguably more informative B2-versus-B4 comparison between a competently
hand-configured system and the GA-optimised one; and second, the five
participating psychologists also contributed to the risk-labeling design
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) earlier in this project, so they are not
fully independent of the system under evaluation in the way an external
panel would be.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Design}

We sampled 30 response pairs from the baseline vs.\ optimised comparison
dataset, stratified by risk level (6 low-risk, 9 mid-risk, 15 high-risk),
weighted toward high-risk cases to reflect their clinical importance.
For each item, annotators were shown the user prompt and two responses
labeled A and B in randomised order, without knowing which system
generated each response (rater-blinded; the researchers preparing and
analysing the response pairs were not themselves blinded to system
identity, so we describe this design as single-blind from the annotators'
perspective rather than double-blind).
Each annotator independently
rated both responses on three dimensions using a 1--5 scale:
\emph{Empathy} (emotional validation and warmth),
\emph{Safety} (appropriateness and crisis guidance calibration),
and \emph{Helpfulness} (concrete, actionable support).
After rating both responses, annotators indicated an overall preference.
Inter-annotator agreement was measured using Krippendorff's
alpha~\cite{krippendorff2011} and pairwise percentage agreement
within one scale point.

\subsubsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} summarises the results. The GA-optimised
system (B4) substantially outperforms the minimal-structure, short-budget baseline (B1) on
all three dimensions, with item-level mean improvements of $+1.68$ on
Empathy, $+1.77$ on Safety, and $+1.95$ on Helpfulness. These estimates
and their significance tests are computed at the level of the 30
response-pair items rather than the 150 individual annotator-item
ratings, since each item was rated by all five annotators and the
within-item ratings are correlated rather than independent; we detail
the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:pseudorep} below. Across the 150
annotator-item preference judgements (5 annotators $\times$ 30 items),
B4 was preferred in 146 (97.3\%), B1 in 2 (1.3\%), with 2 judgements
(1.3\%) marked equal. At the item level, all five annotators agreed
unanimously on 27 of the 30 items (all favouring B4); the remaining
three items each drew at least one dissenting judgement (one annotator
preferring B1 on each of two items, and two annotators rating a third
item as equal), rather than uniform agreement across all 30 items. Given
that B1's disadvantage here partly reflects the truncation defect rather
than parameter choice alone (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}), and that
the comparison is not fully blinded to the study team, we read this result
as showing that clinicians strongly prefer a complete, non-truncated,
GA-optimised response over a truncated, minimally-weighted one, rather
than as isolated evidence that evolutionary search specifically improves
clinical quality beyond what a competently hand-configured system (B2)
would already achieve; a B2-vs-B4 clinician comparison, which we did not
run, would speak more directly to that latter question, and we list it as
a required follow-up in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

This resolves the tension flagged in Section~\ref{sec:qualcomp}: on the
automatic proxy, empathy showed the smallest of the three gains
(0.269 to 0.430, 60\% relative), well behind the safety and structure
improvements. On blinded clinician ratings of the same B1-vs-B4
comparison, empathy improved by $+1.68$, similar in magnitude
to the safety ($+1.77$) and helpfulness ($+1.95$) gains
and significant at $p<0.001$; we have not, however, formally tested
whether these three effects are statistically equal to one another (e.g.\
via a system-by-dimension interaction test), so we describe them as
similar in magnitude rather than as confirmed to be statistically
indistinguishable. The two measurements agree on
direction, both favour B4, but not on how large the empathy improvement is.
The most likely explanation is proxy insensitivity rather than a
genuinely small clinical effect: the rule-based empathy score saturates
once a handful of distinct markers are present (Eq.~\eqref{eq:empathy})
and penalises repetition, so it has limited dynamic range to register
further improvement once a response clears that low bar, whereas a
clinician reading the full response can still perceive and reward
differences in warmth, specificity, and emotional attunement that the
marker count no longer distinguishes. This is a concrete instance of
the proxy-validity limitation discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: here the proxy appears to
\emph{under-credit} a real improvement rather than over-credit a
spurious one, which is the opposite failure direction from the
marker-stuffing case in Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck} and
underscores that the rule-based scores are not a reliable stand-in for
clinical judgement in either direction.

\subsubsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}
\label{sec:pseudorep}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition (5 annotators $\times$
30 items) are not 150 independent observations, since ratings from the
same annotator are correlated across items and ratings of the same item
are correlated across annotators; a naive paired $t$-test on the raw
annotator-item pairs would understate uncertainty. We instead report
inference at the level of the 30 items -- the genuine unit of independent
replication -- by averaging the 5 raters' scores per item and condition
before computing paired differences. This yields the same mean item-level
improvements as the raw annotator-item means in Table~\ref{tab:human_eval}
($+1.68$ Empathy, $+1.77$ Safety, $+1.95$ Helpfulness), now with correctly
calibrated 95\% confidence intervals, all well clear of zero, and remains
highly significant under both paired $t$-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests. A complementary linear-mixed-model analysis (crossed random effects
for item and rater) yields numerically identical point estimates,
confirming the result is not an artefact of the aggregation step. Full
statistics for all three checks are in Appendix~\ref{app:humaneval}.

\subsubsection{Inter-Annotator Agreement}

We compute Krippendorff's alpha using the \texttt{krippendorff} Python
package (interval level of measurement) over the 60 blinded response units
that annotators actually rated (30 items $\times$ 2 responses per item, with
system identity withheld during annotation), arranged as a $5 \times 60$
reliability matrix (5 raters $\times$ 60 units) with no missing ratings.
This yields $\alpha = 0.56$ for Empathy, $\alpha = 0.58$ for Safety, and
$\alpha = 0.61$ for Helpfulness, indicating moderate-to-substantial
agreement by widely used interpretive conventions for this statistic. To
characterise the uncertainty in these estimates, we additionally report
95\% confidence intervals from a percentile bootstrap (2{,}000 resamples
of the 60 units, with replacement): $[0.48, 0.62]$ for Empathy,
$[0.50, 0.64]$ for Safety, and $[0.54, 0.66]$ for Helpfulness. Pairwise
percentage agreement within one scale point is correspondingly high:
86.0\% (Empathy), 87.2\% (Safety), and 85.2\% (Helpfulness).

$\alpha$ is computed jointly over the full set of 60 blinded units
the annotators actually saw (30 items $\times$ 2 responses per item, system
identity withheld), which is the methodologically appropriate calculation
for this rater-blinded design.

Consistent with this substantial-agreement result, B4 was
preferred over B1 in 146 of 150 (97.3\%) annotator-item judgements,
with full five-annotator unanimity on 27 of 30 items.

\subsubsection{Validation of Automatic Metrics}

Comparing Tables~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized} and~\ref{tab:human_eval}, the
automatic empathy and safety scores rank B4 above B1 by the same ordering
as the corresponding human ratings, on both dimensions. The two scales are
not directly comparable in magnitude, but the consistent rank ordering
supports the automatic scores as useful optimisation signals even without
absolute calibration against clinical standards, keeping in mind the
caveats above regarding B1's truncation defect and the partial lack of
independence between the human evaluation panel and the system's design
process.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Role of Seeded Initialization}

Seeded initialization plausibly plays a dual role in the proposed
framework. On the positive side, seeding with four
hand-designed genomes alongside randomly initialised individuals is a
common approach for accelerating convergence and improving reproducibility
across runs relative to fully random initialisation. Per-generation genome
logs show that the near-optimal generation-1 best fitness (0.701, training-time)
is attributable to a structure-heavy individual ($w_c \approx 0.68$)
consistent with a mutated or crossed-over descendant of the structure-heavy
seed $g_\mathrm{str}$, rather than the balanced seed $g_\mathrm{bal}$: $g_\mathrm{bal}$'s own directly
measured fitness (0.556 as B2) is well below that generation-1 value. On
the negative side, if the scoring function contains
exploitable shortcuts, seeds may help the GA
converge more quickly to suboptimal behavioral patterns. In our
experiments, all seeds converged to question-only structural collapse
under the initial scoring design, because the scoring function allowed
question-asking alone to satisfy structural completeness requirements. This
highlights an important principle: seeded initialization amplifies whatever
signal the fitness function provides, whether that signal is well-designed
or not. The combination of seeded initialization with trace-level
evaluation is therefore essential: seeds without careful scoring validation
risk accelerating convergence toward the wrong objective.

The convergence pattern shows the GA
reaching near-optimal training fitness in generation~1, via this
structure-heavy lineage, and maintaining this
through elitism while mutation continues to explore, with no sustained
second improvement phase, indicating the search does not discover a
substantially better configuration than this early high-fitness region
within 20 generations.

\subsection{Importance of Trace Analysis}

Scalar fitness values alone suggested successful optimization throughout the
early development of this framework. However, trace analysis of exported
response logs and representative conversational samples revealed major
behavioral failures that aggregate metrics concealed entirely. The most
significant was question-only structural collapse: responses with fitness
values of 0.6--0.7 were in fact responses consisting almost entirely of
questions, which scored well on the structure metric due to a flaw in the
initial scoring function that rewarded question presence without requiring
grounding or actionable guidance.

This finding has broader implications for the evaluation of generative
AI systems. Optimization metrics, whether hand-crafted or learned, define
what the system optimizes for, not necessarily what the system should do.
When metrics are imperfectly specified, evolutionary systems are particularly
effective at finding and exploiting the gap. Unlike gradient-based methods
that optimize smoothly, evolutionary search can discover discrete behavioral
modes that appear high-fitness under the metric while violating the underlying
intent. Trace analysis is therefore not merely a supplementary diagnostic but
a necessary component of the evaluation pipeline for any reward-driven
generative system. The response-part-budget truncation defect documented in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}, and the marker-coverage gap documented in
Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}, are further instances of this same
broader pattern: both were found only through tracing individual generated
responses against internal generation flags, not through inspecting
aggregate scores.

\subsection{Scoring Function as Bottleneck}
\label{sec:scoring-bottleneck}

Our experiments demonstrate that the scoring and generation pipeline, not
the GA algorithm, is the primary source of the discrepancies documented in
this work. This finding replicates a
well-known pattern in reinforcement learning and evolutionary optimization
literature: reward hacking, where an agent achieves high reward through
unintended behavioral shortcuts~\cite{amodei2016concrete}. In our context,
several distinct failure modes were observed and corrected or documented:

First, \textit{question-only structural collapse}: the initial structure score
rewarded question presence without requiring grounding or action, leading the
GA to generate responses consisting almost entirely of reflective questions.
Corrected by replacing the continuous, unbounded reward with the discrete
component-count lookup of Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, under which a
question-only response satisfies exactly one component and is capped at
$0.20$.

Second, \textit{empathy marker stuffing}: the initial empathy score rewarded
marker count linearly, incentivizing responses that repeated validation phrases.
Corrected by introducing the stuffing penalty when total marker hits exceed~3.

Third, \textit{safety saturation}: the initial safety score saturated quickly,
providing no gradient signal to distinguish between adequate and excellent
crisis responses. Corrected by introducing smooth safety soft thresholds,
though we note (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}) that the current soft
threshold of 0.94 is itself unattainable for any mid-risk response and so
does not fully resolve this issue for that risk category.

Fourth, \textit{structure-marker coverage gap}: an incomplete keyword list
undercounted genuinely inserted structural components for a subset of
high-risk responses (Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}), which we
corrected by expanding the marker lists.

Fifth, \textit{response-part-budget truncation}: B1's minimal response-part
budget silently drops escalation content appended late in the assembly
order (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}), which is a generator
implementation defect rather than a scoring-function issue per se, but
which we group here because, like the scoring issues above, it was only
detected through tracing individual responses rather than through
aggregate metrics.

The interpretability of the genome and the modular scoring design were
essential enablers of diagnosing all five issues: because each score
component and generation step corresponded to a specific behavioral
dimension, failure modes could be diagnosed, attributed, and (where
corrected) fixed individually. The first four are unified in the single
frozen scoring pipeline used for the GA runs reported in this manuscript
(Section~\ref{sec:structure-analysis}); the fifth (response-part-budget
truncation) is a defect specific to the B1 baseline's construction rather
than to the GA's own scoring or search process, and is tracked separately
as a required baseline correction (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Data Efficiency and Practical Deployment}
\label{sec:data-efficiency}

The dataset-size experiments, using nested training subsets and a
pre-specified TOST equivalence test (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-size}), show
that mean validation fitness stays within a narrow 0.0068-point band
across all tested sizes ($N=100$ to $N=2297$) with no monotonic trend.
Formal statistical equivalence was not established at the tested
margin ($\varepsilon=0.01$), most plausibly reflecting limited power at five seeds
per condition rather than a genuine effect of dataset size
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); the practical
deployment recommendation below should accordingly be read as suggestive
rather than as a formally confirmed data-efficiency guarantee. Mean runtime on our test hardware, from
this same nested-subset run, increases from 15.9 seconds at $N=100$ to 47.0
seconds at $N=300$ and 98.4 seconds at $N=600$, then rises sharply to 478.2
seconds at $N=1200$ before dropping back to 205.6 seconds for the full
dataset ($N=2297$); this non-monotonic pattern (discussed further below)
means the practical guidance below is drawn from fitness stability rather
than from a clean runtime-scaling argument. In any case, larger datasets provide no
apparent performance benefit within the range tested.

For resource-constrained settings such as community mental-health NGOs or
university counselling services in Bangladesh, this data efficiency, if
confirmed under a higher-powered replication, would be practically significant:
the framework would require only $N=100$--$300$ de-identified
support interactions to calibrate the GA, achievable within a short
deployment pilot. The template-based generator also requires no GPU
infrastructure or LLM API calls during inference, making it deployable on
standard hardware. However, these practical advantages must be weighed against
the limitations discussed below: the dataset must cover a sufficiently diverse
range of risk levels and themes, and the scoring functions must be validated
against local clinical norms before deployment.

The computational complexity of the framework is determined primarily by three
factors: population size $P$, number of generations $T$, and evaluation subset
size $|\mathcal{I}|$. Since fitness evaluation dominates runtime (each
evaluation requires generating and scoring $|\mathcal{I}|$ responses), the
practical complexity per GA run can be approximated as
$\mathcal{O}(P \cdot T \cdot |\mathcal{I}|)$. For the main experiment settings
($P = 100$, $T = 20$, $|\mathcal{I}| = 600$), this corresponds to
approximately 1,200,000 response generation and scoring operations per run.
This algorithmic count is fixed once $|\mathcal{I}|$ saturates at its cap of
600, and so should not continue to grow with dataset size once $N \geq 600$.
Our measured wall-clock runtimes are not consistent with a clean plateau at
$N \geq 600$: runtime rises sharply to 478.2 seconds at $N=1200$ before
falling to 205.6 seconds at the full dataset size.
We attribute this to system-level timing variability on
non-dedicated consumer hardware, e.g.\ background processes, thermal
throttling, and disk I/O contention across sequentially-run experiment
blocks, rather than to any property of the algorithm itself; the
$\mathcal{O}(P \cdot T \cdot |\mathcal{I}|)$ operation count, which is what
the complexity argument above actually establishes, is unaffected by this
wall-clock variability, though we acknowledge that a runtime pattern this
variable across reruns limits how much weight the specific shape of
Figure~\ref{fig:runtime} should be given; total wall-clock time, not its
shape across $N$, is the more reliable takeaway. This contrasts favourably with conventional LLM
training and fine-tuning approaches, which typically require substantial GPU
infrastructure, consistent with reported large-scale training and fine-tuning
costs for LLMs~\cite{when_llms_meet_ea2025}.

\subsection{Interpretation of Baseline Results}
\label{sec:interp-baseline}

B1's weights ($w_e=0.10$, $w_c=0.10$, $w_s=0.80$; Section~\ref{sec:methods})
are chosen so that genome renormalisation leaves them unchanged, keeping
$w_c$ genuinely in the bottom, no-structure regime rather than drifting
into the stochastic mid-regime a naive equal-floor construction would
otherwise produce.

The baseline comparison results reveal important insights about the sources
of performance gain. The near-zero structure score of B1 (0.017) confirms
that unoptimised generation produces almost entirely unstructured responses,
despite the underlying phrase banks containing grounding, action, and question
components. This demonstrates that without genome-driven weight configuration,
the generator defaults to minimal structural output. The negative fitness of
B1 ($-0.227$) is primarily driven by its low safety score (0.472), which we
trace to the response-part-budget truncation mechanism described in
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget} (B1's $m=256$ setting silently drops
escalation content for high-risk prompts) rather than to any failure of
the risk-aware escalation rule itself.

This is a genuine software-design defect in the generator, not merely an
unoptimised parameter setting, since it silently removes mandatory crisis
content regardless of the user's actual risk level. We constructed a
bug-fixed variant, B1-fixed, using the same minimal genome weights
($w_e=0.10$, $w_s=0.80$, $w_c=0.10$) but with \texttt{memory\_window}
increased from 256 to 512 to disable the truncation defect while changing
nothing else, isolating its effect from the GA's weight optimisation. This
lets us decompose the B1-to-B4 gains reported throughout this manuscript
into two components: the effect of removing truncation alone
(B1$\to$B1-fixed) and the remaining effect of GA weight optimisation once
truncation is no longer a confound (B1-fixed$\to$B4). The decomposition is
strikingly clean along dimension lines. Of the total B1-to-B4 safety gain
(0.433 points), 98.8\% (0.428 points) is recovered simply by disabling
truncation (B1: 0.472 $\to$ B1-fixed: 0.900), with GA optimisation
contributing almost nothing further (B1-fixed: 0.900 $\to$ B4: 0.905) --
safety in this framework is overwhelmingly a truncation-defect story, not
a genome-optimisation story. Structure shows the opposite pattern: across
repeated evaluation runs, B1 and B1-fixed's structure scores are
statistically indistinguishable (B1: mean $0.015$, SD $0.0008$; B1-fixed:
mean $0.015$, SD $0.0007$; difference $0.18$ combined SDs, well within
run-to-run stochastic noise), confirming that truncation was never
meaningfully suppressing
structure in the first place (since $w_c=0.10$ already places both
conditions in the deterministic bottom, no-structure regime,
Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}); essentially all of the
B1-to-B4 structure gain is attributable to the GA
discovering a high-$w_c$ genome, not to truncation. Overall fitness splits
roughly evenly between the two effects (54\% from removing truncation,
46\% from GA optimisation), reflecting the roughly equal fitness weight
placed on safety and structure in the coefficients $A,B,C$
(Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-scoring}). Like the question-only-collapse and
empathy-marker-stuffing failures (Appendix~\ref{app:failure},
Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}), this was found only by tracing
individual responses, not by inspecting aggregate scores; the reported GA
genome ($m^{\star}=512$) does not itself exhibit the defect, since any
$m > 256$ disables the truncation cap. Future generator revisions should
either filter internal tags before truncation, or reorder assembly so
escalation content precedes empathy content and cannot be discarded
regardless of $m$.

The gap between B2 and B4 is the more informative comparison for isolating
the specific contribution of evolutionary search, since B2 does not share
B1's truncation defect. The balanced seed genome already achieves high safety (0.903) but
leaves substantial room for structural improvement (0.648 vs.\ 1.000). The GA
discovers that a high structure weight ($w_c^{\star} \approx 0.77$ for the
reported genome) consistently
produces better outcomes than the balanced initialisation. We verified
this directly with a 12-point grid search over $w_c$ alone (holding the
$w_e{:}w_s$ ratio fixed at the reported genome's value, so that raw
weights already sum to 1 and genome renormalisation is a no-op at every
grid point): the results reproduce all three structure regimes exactly at
the thresholds stated in Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure} --
structure is exactly 0.000 for $w_c < 0.25$, fluctuates stochastically
around 0.64--0.67 for $0.25 \le w_c < 0.55$, and is exactly 1.000 for
every grid point at $w_c \ge 0.55$, with no exceptions across twelve
points. Fitness in the deterministic regime peaks at 0.700, close to but
still below the GA's converged 0.703 (a 0.43\% relative gap). This
confirms, rather than merely motivates, our claim that the structure-dominant
result does not require evolutionary search to discover: a simple grid
sweep over one gene finds a fitness plateau nearly matching the GA's own,
consistent with the generator's deterministic structure threshold
($w_c \ge 0.55$; Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}) doing most of
the explanatory work.

We additionally ran an equal-budget random search
(2,000 draws over the full genome space -- $w_e, w_s, w_c, \theta_\mathrm{mid},
\theta_\mathrm{high}, \gamma, d, m$ -- matching the GA's implicit budget of
$100 \times 20 = 2{,}000$ evaluations), sampled independently of the grid
search above. The single best genome found across these 2,000 draws
reaches fitness $0.7068$ ($w_e=0.103$, $w_s=0.213$, $w_c=0.683$,
$\gamma=0.176$), fractionally above the GA's single reported main-run
fitness of $0.703$. The fairer comparison is against the five
significance-test seeds rather than that one main-run number
(Table~\ref{tab:significance}: range $0.6949$--$0.7027$, mean $0.6993$,
SD $0.0028$). Against that directly re-scored distribution, the random
search's best result exceeds all five GA seeds and sits $2.72$ standard
deviations above the GA mean -- a higher peak than this GA seed set
reached, not a draw within its run-to-run band. This sharpens, rather than
softens, the grid-search finding above: for this genome space and fitness
design, the achievable fitness ceiling is a broad plateau that undirected
search can not only reach but, in this single 2{,}000-draw run, slightly
overshoot relative to the five archived GA genomes. However, that
overshoot does not establish that undirected search reaches the plateau as
\emph{reliably} as the GA does: the 2,000 draws underlying the random
search have a mean fitness of only $0.334$ (SD $0.324$), so most random
genomes are far from the plateau, and one lucky peak does not show that a
second, differently-seeded run would land there again. We therefore
support the claim that a comparable or slightly higher \emph{peak} is
reachable by undirected search given enough draws, while the GA's five
seeds remain tightly clustered just below that peak; repeated
random-search runs (and the untested hill-climbing comparison) would be
needed to settle the reliability question, and we list both as required
future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
The finding also suggests that safety and empathy are more easily satisfied
by the fixed escalation rule, safety penalty terms, and phrase bank design than by genome weight
allocation, freeing the evolutionary search to concentrate on structural
completeness.

These observations partially answer RQ2: descriptively, the GA does not
sacrifice safety for structure (B4 maintains safety parity with B2, 0.905
vs.\ 0.903, while dramatically improving structure, a more than 58-fold
gain over B1 and 0.352 points over B2). However, B2 and B4 differ in
several respects simultaneously (genome weights, response-part budget,
and $\gamma$), so this comparison does not isolate the causal
contribution of progressive structure enforcement or of the safety
penalty mechanism specifically from these other differences; ablations
that vary each mechanism independently while holding the others fixed
would be required to answer the causal-attribution part of RQ2 directly,
and we list them in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Did the GA Achieve Multi-Objective Balance?}
\label{sec:balance-discussion}

The fixed fitness-weight coefficients
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) assign empathy and safety the largest shares
($A=B=0.40$) and structure the smallest ($C=0.15$), reflecting our
intended prioritisation of safe, empathetic responses. Yet the reported
genome is structure-dominant ($w_c^{\star}=0.768$ versus
$w_e^{\star}=0.131$; Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}), and $w_s^{\star}$ is
not part of this tension at all: as clarified in the genome note
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), $w_s$ is a renormalisation participant rather
than an independently interpretable safety preference, since safety
behaviour is governed by the fixed escalation rule and categorical safety
lookup, not by $w_s$.

The resolution lies in distinguishing the \emph{fixed} coefficients
$A,B,C,D$, which set how base scores combine into fitness, from the
\emph{evolved} genome weights $w_e, w_c$, which determine how much
empathy and structural content the generator actually inserts -- these
operate on different score landscapes. Safety and empathy both saturate
quickly: safety reaches a high plateau ($S_i \approx 0.90$--$1.00$) once
escalation is reliably present, independent of $w_s$, and empathy
saturates once a few distinct markers appear, with further weight risking
the marker-stuffing penalty. Structure, by contrast, has the largest
unrealised headroom in the unoptimised baseline (B1 structure $=0.017$)
and responds sharply to $w_c$ at the deterministic threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}). This headroom asymmetry is
compounded by an explicit reinforcement asymmetry in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}: structure is reinforced twice (up to
$+0.09$ combined) while empathy is reinforced once (capped at $+0.05$) and
safety not at all beyond its base term. So the structure-dominant outcome
reflects both where headroom is largest \emph{and} where the fitness
function rewards marginal content most -- neither of which is visible from
the coefficient table $A,B,C,D$ alone.

This suggests the GA found a corner solution -- empathy near its
saturation floor, structure maximised -- rather than a smooth trade-off
across objectives (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}: empathy stays modest
while structure climbs substantially). This is a genuine limitation of
collapsing multi-objective optimisation into a single scalar fitness with
fixed coefficients: the resulting genome reflects the geometry of the
constraint set as much as the analyst's stated priorities. A
multi-objective evolutionary algorithm maintaining an explicit Pareto
front over $(E_i, S_i, C_i)$, rather than a weighted scalar sum, would
make this trade-off space visible and let a clinician select an operating
point directly; we list this as future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Ethical Risks and Deployment Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

The deployment of optimisation-driven conversational agents in mental-health
contexts raises ethical concerns that extend beyond algorithmic performance.
This subsection critically examines deployment risks specific to the
proposed framework and the Bangladeshi context.

\textbf{Proxy metric misalignment.} The empathy and safety scores used in
this work are rule-based proxies derived from keyword detection, and safety
in particular should be understood specifically as an escalation-calibration
proxy: it verifies that at least one escalation-language marker is present
for the appropriate risk label, not that the response's content is free of
harmful advice, false reassurance, contradictory instructions, invalid
resource references, coercive language, or misinformation, none of which
the current scorer detects. Optimising against these proxies risks producing
responses that are \textit{metrically safe} but not \textit{clinically safe};
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the construct-validity evidence (and
its limits) for these proxies in full. For deployment specifically, this
means the proxies should not be treated as a substitute for clinical
oversight: we recommend that any deployment include ongoing
monitoring by qualified mental health professionals who can identify cases
where high-scoring responses are clinically inappropriate.

\textbf{Over-reliance and substitution risk.} Systems like Riri are designed
as supplementary tools, not replacements for professional care. However,
in Bangladesh, where access to mental health professionals is severely
limited~\cite{koly2024stakeholder}, users experiencing acute distress may
interact with an AI assistant as their primary or only source of support.
The high structural-component usage observed in our reported system (100\%
insertion rate) means that many responses include a referral to
professional help where appropriate, but if no such help is
accessible, this referral may feel dismissive rather than supportive.
We also note that ``100\% structural-component usage'' refers specifically
to the generator's grounding/action/question components, not exclusively to
professional referrals: an action component may equally be a breathing
exercise, a suggestion to contact a trusted friend, or a grounding
technique, and low-risk prompts specifically do not receive crisis
escalation content at all (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}). Future work should design
context-aware escalation that acknowledges service availability limitations.

\textbf{Cultural and linguistic validity.} The empathy marker sets used
in this work were constructed by the research team based on common
Bangla/Banglish expressions observed in the dataset. They have not been
validated by clinical psychologists or community mental health workers
familiar with Bangladeshi emotional expression norms. Different communities
within Bangladesh (urban versus rural, different socioeconomic
backgrounds) may use different linguistic patterns to express distress or
respond to support. A marker set that works well for the PHQ-4 screening
population in the training dataset may not generalise to other populations.
The cultural specificity of empathetic language is particularly important
given that the reported genome's high structure weight ($w_c^{\star} = 0.768$)
means structural components dominate the response, potentially at the cost
of culturally-appropriate emotional tone.

\textbf{Reward hacking in deployment.} The failure modes documented in this
work (question-only collapse, empathy marker stuffing, the structure-marker
coverage gap, and the response-part-budget truncation defect) were
discovered
through offline trace analysis before deployment. In a live deployment,
new failure modes may emerge from interactions with real users whose
language patterns differ from the training data. If the system is
periodically re-optimised using interaction logs, the GA may discover new
shortcuts that exploit patterns in real user behaviour. Robust deployment
requires continuous trace monitoring, periodic human review of response
samples, and clear mechanisms for clinical staff to flag and correct
problematic response patterns. The interpretable genome design facilitates
this: because generator behaviour is controlled by a small set of named
parameters, targeted corrections can be made without rerunning the full
optimisation pipeline.

These considerations do not invalidate the proposed framework but rather
define the conditions under which it can be responsibly deployed. The
framework provides a technically informative, though not yet
fully-validated, exploration of data-efficient mental-health conversational
optimisation. Responsible deployment requires
it to be embedded within a broader governance structure that includes
clinical oversight, user safety monitoring, and culturally-informed
evaluation protocols~\cite{apa2024_advisory,harvard2024_safety}.

\textbf{Data provenance and risk-category validity.} The high-risk category
in our dataset is populated in part by PHQ-4 scores of 9--12 alone,
independent of the explicit self-harm keyword indicator
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-label}); because PHQ-4 has no suicide-risk item
(Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}), this may inflate the high-risk category
with severe depression/anxiety presentations that do not necessarily
involve suicidality, and reward crisis escalation for such cases without
direct evidence of suicide risk specifically (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Age field reliability and the 18+ platform policy.} The
application restricts registration to users aged 18 and over, and does
not knowingly collect or use data from users under 18 for research. Age
itself, however, is self-reported at registration and not independently
verified (e.g.\ via identity document), and the dataset's age field
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}, Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats}) shows 422
records (14.7\%) with a self-reported age of 13--17. We cannot determine
from the data alone whether these reflect genuine underage users who
circumvented the registration policy, self-report errors, or another
data-quality issue with this field, and we do not treat this figure as
evidence that confirmed minors are present in the dataset. At the same
time, unverified self-report cannot fully rule out their presence either,
and we do not consider the platform's stated policy alone sufficient
grounds to set the question aside. We recommend that future versions of
this dataset either implement stronger age verification at registration,
or apply an explicit data-governance rule that filters or separately
reviews records with a self-reported age below 18 before they are used
for research, and we list resolving this ambiguity as a required
correction prior to further dissemination of this dataset or findings
derived from it.

To characterise how much this ambiguity affects the reported results
specifically, we re-evaluated the already-trained genomes for B1,
B1-fixed, B2, and B4 on the 575-prompt validation set with the 88
self-reported-minor records excluded (487 prompts), without retraining
anything, and compared against the same genomes' fitness on the full
validation set (Table~\ref{tab:minors-sensitivity}). This is a
provisional analysis pending the institutional determination described
above, and should not be read as resolving the underlying governance
question -- it addresses only whether the currently reported numbers are
statistically sensitive to this subset, not whether these records should
be included in the study at all.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This section summarises the boundaries of the present study: methodological
choices with known tradeoffs, measurement limitations, and aspects of the
experimental design that constrain how the results should be interpreted.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prompts are synthetic reconstructions, not verbatim user
        messages.} As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:methods}, each $x_i$ is
        produced by \texttt{build\_realistic\_user\_prompt()} from
        structured, analyst-labeled theme fields, applied uniformly across
        B1--B8. This limits the paper's ecological-validity and
        multilingual-authenticity claims: the Bangla/Banglish
        code-switching in $x_i$ reflects programmatic phrase-bank
        selection rather than organically observed code-switching. Because
        higher-intensity language is constructed into prompts already
        labeled high-risk, the distress score $h(x_i)$ used in mid-risk
        escalation (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) is not fully
        independent of the risk-label construction process. The findings
        in this paper regarding risk detection and escalation calibration
        should therefore be understood as characterising behaviour on this
        synthetic corpus, not yet as validated on genuine, unedited user
        messages.

  \item \textbf{The ``high-risk'' label reflects PHQ-4 severity or an
        explicit self-harm keyword match, not independently validated
        suicide risk.} PHQ-4 has no suicide-risk item
        (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}), so this category may include
        severe depression/anxiety presentations without confirmed
        suicidality alongside those with it, and both receive the same
        crisis-escalation treatment. The risk categories in this paper are
        best understood as support-intensity tiers derived from a
        depression/anxiety screening instrument plus a keyword override,
        rather than as a validated suicide-risk classification.

  \item \textbf{All GA runs and baseline comparisons in this manuscript
        share one consistent scoring pipeline.} The main B1--B5 comparison
        (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}), the five-seed significance
        analysis (Table~\ref{tab:significance}), the dataset-size and
        population-size sensitivity analyses, the convergence and
        diversity figures, and the B6--B8 pre-trained-LLM comparison were
        all produced under the same scorer, with
        per-seed genomes archived (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}). B6--B8
        were originally scored under an earlier version of the structure
        marker list; we directly rescored their already-generated
        responses under the current, corrected marker list and confirmed
        all three models' empathy, safety, and structure scores are
        unchanged, since none of them insert genuine grounding, action, or
        question content for the corrected marker list to newly detect
        (sentinel tokens or repetitive, non-conversational output;
        Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}). The
        corrected structure-marker list itself was derived by inspecting
        responses on the same held-out validation set used for reporting,
        rather than a separate development set. B5 (MSGA)
        reflects a single run rather than the five independent seeds used
        for B4, and its additional 500 preliminary genome evaluations are
        not reflected in any evaluation-budget comparison.

  \item \textbf{B1-fixed isolates the truncation-defect effect from GA
        weight optimisation for the quantitative comparison only.} The
        decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline} uses a
        single-point evaluation of B1-fixed, consistent with how B3, B4,
        and B5 are also reported, and evaluates B1-fixed on the full
        $N=2{,}872$ dataset rather than the 575-prompt validation set used
        for B1--B5 (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}), so the
        decomposition compares scores computed on different prompt
        populations. The qualitative comparison
        (Section~\ref{sec:qualcomp}) and the human evaluation
        (Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}) are both built on B1 in its
        original (truncated) form rather than B1-fixed.

  \item \textbf{The human evaluation compares B1 and B4 only, and its
        raters are not fully independent of the study.} B2 -- the
        competently hand-configured, non-defective baseline that isolates
        the GA's specific contribution most directly -- is not included in
        the human evaluation. The five participating psychologists also
        contributed to the risk-labeling design
        (Section~\ref{sec:risk-labels}) earlier in the project, so they
        are not fully independent of the system under evaluation in the
        way an external panel recruited solely for evaluation would be.
        The rating design is rater-blinded (annotators did not know which
        system produced each response) rather than double-blind (the
        researchers who prepared and analysed the response pairs were not
        themselves blinded to system identity).

  \item \textbf{RQ2's causal-attribution question is not isolated by the
        comparisons reported here.} The B2-vs-B4 comparison
        conflates several genome dimensions that differ simultaneously
        between B2 and B4 (weights, response-part budget, and $\gamma$),
        so it does not attribute the observed structure/safety gains
        cleanly to progressive structure enforcement or to the safety
        penalty mechanism specifically. Ablations that vary each
        mechanism independently while holding the others fixed would be
        required to answer this part of RQ2 directly.

  \item \textbf{RQ4 (does the GA reach high-fitness regions more reliably
        than equal-budget undirected search) is answered only partially.}
        A 12-point grid search over $w_c$ alone and a
        2,000-draw equal-budget random search over the full genome
        (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) both confirm that the
        generator's deterministic structure threshold accounts for the
        structure-dominant finding without requiring evolutionary search:
        the random search's best genome (fitness $0.7068$) exceeds all five
        GA significance-test seeds and lies $2.72$~SD above their mean
        (Table~\ref{tab:significance}), so undirected search found a
        higher peak than this GA seed set in the single equal-budget run
        we conducted. We do not read the GA's tighter seed cluster as
        evidence of a higher ceiling, only of more consistent landings
        just below that random-search peak. This comparison is still based
        on a single random-search run, however, whereas the GA reliability
        claim rests on five independent runs; establishing whether
        undirected search reaches this peak as \emph{reliably} as the GA
        lands near it (rather than only sometimes, given the 2,000 draws'
        own high variance, mean $0.334$, SD $0.324$) would require
        repeated random-search runs at the same budget, which we did not
        conduct. The corresponding hill-climbing comparison also
        remains untested.

  \item \textbf{RQ3's answer is descriptive rather than a formally
        established equivalence.} The nested-subset
        experiments (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-size}) use a pre-specified TOST
        equivalence margin ($\varepsilon=0.01$), which no comparison
        reached, despite a narrow observed spread (a 0.0068-point band
        across dataset sizes, and a maximum difference of 0.0025 from the
        $P=100$ reference for population size). This most
        plausibly reflects limited statistical power at five seeds per
        condition rather than a genuine practically important effect of
        dataset or population size, but the study does not have direct
        evidence to distinguish the two explanations with confidence.

  \item \textbf{Reported ``population diversity'' is fitness dispersion, a
        proxy for genome-space diversity rather than a direct measure of
        it.} The variance plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:diversity-curve} can
        diverge from genuine genotype diversity in the presence of
        inactive genes, threshold effects, and behaviourally equivalent
        categorical values.

  \item \textbf{Construct validity of rule-based proxy metrics.} The
        empathy and safety metrics are keyword-based, which is a genuine
        validity limitation rather than only a favourable design tradeoff.
        Keyword detection cannot distinguish genuine emotional attunement
        from surface phrasing that happens to match a marker list, cannot
        detect sarcasm, negation, or context-dependent meaning, and can be
        gamed by repetition or marker-stuffing, as documented in
        Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}. The safety score specifically
        should be understood as verifying escalation-marker presence
        calibrated to risk label, not as verifying the absence of harmful
        advice, false reassurance, contradictory instructions, invalid
        resources, coercive language, or misinformation
        (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}). The human evaluation
        (Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}) provides partial, directional
        support for these proxies on the specific B1-vs-B4 comparison
        tested, but does not establish that proxy scores track clinical
        judgement in absolute terms or across the full space of genomes
        the GA can reach. Because the GA optimises directly against these
        proxies, any systematic proxy--clinician divergence risks being
        amplified rather than corrected by evolutionary search.

  \item \textbf{The mid-risk safety scoring rule has a structural
        asymmetry.} The best attainable mid-risk safety score (0.85, with
        escalation present) sits below the fitness function's soft
        threshold (0.94), so every mid-risk response incurs the associated
        penalty regardless of its quality. This is an asymmetry in the
        current reward design across risk categories, not a finding that
        mid-risk responses are themselves less safe.

  \item \textbf{The evolved distress-gating parameter's practical
        significance is not established.} The direct distress-gating
        penalty term (Section~\ref{sec:risk-escalation}) cannot
        distinguish between different values of $\gamma$ in a correctly
        functioning generator, since escalation is already guaranteed in
        the regime where the penalty applies, regardless of $\gamma$.
        $\gamma^{\star}$'s small evolved magnitude ($\approx 0.059$)
        should therefore not be read as confirmed evidence that the fixed
        distress threshold already captures most useful escalation signal
        for this dataset, since the penalty term that might have produced
        such evidence does not functionally distinguish $\gamma$ values in
        the relevant range.

  \item \textbf{The framework addresses single-turn interactions only.}
        All experiments evaluate single-turn prompt-response pairs. Real
        mental-health conversations are multi-turn, where risk levels may
        escalate across turns and emotional context accumulates; recent
        work argues that dialogue-level, rather than single-turn, safety
        evaluation is the more appropriate unit for mental-health
        chatbots, since risk-relevant cues accumulate across a
        conversation~\cite{journey2026}, and that concerning chatbot
        behaviour can escalate across turns in ways that vary by user
        vulnerability profile~\cite{vail2026}. A preliminary exploratory
        analysis of the evolved $\gamma$ parameter across 36{,}113 real
        mid-risk conversation turns found that $\gamma$'s practical effect
        on escalation decisions is a step function rather than a smooth
        relationship, with a saturation threshold at approximately
        $\gamma \approx 0.086$ (2.41\% of turns flip status at this
        boundary) that coincides precisely with where the effective
        relaxed threshold crosses the dominant distress-score cluster in
        that dataset -- the same fitness-neutral-drift phenomenon
        documented for $\theta_\mathrm{mid}$/$\theta_\mathrm{high}$ in
        Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}, here with a specific quantitative
        explanation. This 36{,}113-turn conversation dataset is a separate
        data extraction, pulled later than and independent of the
        2{,}872-prompt PHQ-4-derived corpus used for every other result in
        this manuscript; it was used solely for this one narrow $\gamma$
        analysis and was not used anywhere in the GA search, training, or
        evaluation reported elsewhere. It was collected and de-identified
        through the same consent and de-identification pipeline as the
        main corpus (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), but it is not itself
        formally described in this manuscript as a distinct secondary
        dataset, with its own sample accounting, extraction date, and
        preprocessing pipeline. We therefore present this specific
        finding as a preliminary, scoped observation about $\gamma$'s
        behaviour rather than as a validated multi-turn result, and
        recommend formally documenting this dataset's provenance
        separately before reporting it at full length in future work.
        The
        integer field \texttt{memory\_window} was originally named with
        multi-turn context retention in mind, but in the present
        single-turn system it only implements the response-part budget
        $m$ (Section~\ref{sec:part-budget}); a true conversational memory
        window is a distinct, unimplemented capability.

  \item \textbf{Limited cultural validation.} The Bangla/Banglish empathy
        marker sets were constructed by the research team and reflect
        common expressions observed in this dataset; they have not been
        separately validated by community mental health workers in
        Bangladesh, and Bangla emotional-expression norms are only
        partially captured as a result.

  \item \textbf{Limited seed count for baseline significance testing.}
        The Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons (Section~\ref{sec:significance})
        use five independent GA runs, the smallest sample for which a
        one-sided Wilcoxon test can reject at $\alpha=0.05$; at this
        sample size, $p=0.03125$ is attained whenever all five paired
        differences agree in sign, so the $p$-value itself is
        uninformative beyond confirming directional consistency. Effect
        sizes and bootstrap confidence intervals reported alongside the
        $p$-values (Table~\ref{tab:significance}) are large and well clear
        of zero on the directly observed data, but a larger number of
        independent seeds would give a more conventional significance test
        and a tighter estimate of run-to-run variability.

  \item \textbf{No personalisation.} The optimised genome is a single
        policy applied uniformly across all users. Individual differences
        in preference for directive versus reflective responses, or in
        comfort with Bangla versus English, are not modelled.
\end{itemize}

Natural extensions of this work include:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Multi-turn sequential genome
policies, where the GA optimises escalation and structure decisions
conditioned on full conversation history, motivated both by the clinical
literature on dialogue-level safety evaluation~\cite{journey2026,vail2026}
and by the preliminary $\gamma$-saturation finding above.
  \item A more
robust generator design that decouples response-length truncation from
internal template/safety tags, or reorders assembly so escalation content
cannot be discarded regardless of the response-part budget, closing the
class of defect that B1 illustrates by construction rather than only by
comparison against B1-fixed.
  \item Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms
that maintain explicit Pareto fronts over empathy, safety, and structure,
making trade-offs visible to a clinician rather than collapsing them into
a single scalar fitness.
  \item Learned empathy and safety evaluators trained
on clinician-validated data, replacing the current rule-based proxy
scores.
  \item Human preference alignment via direct annotator feedback
integrated into the fitness function.
  \item An independent
clinician-in-the-loop evaluation, including B2 and a non-truncating B1
variant alongside B4, with mental health professionals rating response
quality in a live deployment setting.
  \item Multilingual expansion
beyond Bangla/Banglish/English to other South Asian languages where
mental-health support infrastructure is similarly limited.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a risk-aware genetic optimization framework for a mental-health
conversational agent operating in Bangla, Banglish, and English. By evolving an
interpretable genome controlling a structured generator, the system achieves
substantial improvements in response structure and overall fitness, maintaining
an already-strong escalation-calibration safety proxy and modestly increasing empathy relative
to a fixed, hand-designed baseline genome. These
headline results (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}) and the accompanying
significance analysis (Table~\ref{tab:significance}) come from a single,
consistent scoring pipeline covering the main B1--B5 comparison, all five
significance-test runs, the dataset-size and population-size sensitivity
analyses, the convergence and diversity figures, and the pre-trained-LLM
comparison (B6--B8), with per-seed genomes
archived throughout; B6--B8's responses were directly rescored under the
current structure marker list and confirmed unchanged
(Section~\ref{sec:llm-baselines}).
Decomposing the largest baseline gap (B1 to B4) shows it splits between a
software-level response-truncation effect and genuine genome optimisation
in different proportions for safety and structure respectively
(Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}); a grid search and an equal-budget
random search over the genome space both reach comparable fitness without
evolutionary search, indicating the structure-dominant result reflects a
broadly reachable fitness plateau for this genome space and fitness
design rather than something evolutionary search was specifically
required to find (Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}). Dataset-size and population-size
sensitivity are best described qualitatively as a tight, non-monotonic
range rather than as formally established statistical equivalence
(Sections~\ref{sec:dataset-size} and~\ref{sec:population-size}). The
study illustrates how
evolutionary algorithms can optimize non-differentiable, value-laden objectives,
and highlights the role of scoring design, trace-based evaluation, and
careful baseline construction in reporting AI systems for mental-health
support. The framework's data efficiency, interpretability, and deployment
feasibility on standard hardware make it a promising foundation for
resource-constrained mental-health applications in multilingual settings,
within the scope and limitations detailed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Evolutionary Failure Mode Examples}
\label{app:failure}

This appendix provides concrete examples of the two evolutionary failure
modes documented in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: question-only structural
collapse and empathy marker stuffing. These failure modes were discovered
through trace analysis during early development, before the scoring function
was redesigned. The examples below are representative of the response patterns
produced by high-fitness genomes under the initial (flawed) scoring design.

\subsection{Question-Only Structural Collapse}
\label{app:collapse}

Under the initial structure score, question presence alone was sufficient to
satisfy the structural completeness requirement. Genomes with high question
weight converged to responses that scored 1.0 on structure while containing
no grounding instructions or actionable guidance. A representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``I feel really anxious and overwhelmed. Family niye onek
stress e achi. What should I do now?''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high structure score):}
``How long have you been feeling this way? What do you think is causing
the most stress? Have you talked to anyone about this? Is there something
specific that happened recently? What usually helps you feel better?''

\textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad \textit{Empathy score:} 0.05 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60
\end{quote}

The response achieves a perfect structure score by including five questions,
but provides no emotional validation, no grounding exercise, and no
actionable guidance. It would be experienced by a distressed user as an
interrogation rather than support. This failure mode was corrected by
replacing the continuous, count-based structure score with the discrete
component-count lookup of Eq.~\eqref{eq:structure}, under which any response
satisfying only one of the three components (grounding, action, or
question) is capped at $0.20$, regardless of how many times that single
component is echoed.

\subsection{Empathy Marker Stuffing}
\label{app:stuffing-example}

Under the initial empathy score, marker count was rewarded linearly without
a diversity penalty. Genomes converged to responses that repeated the same
validation phrase multiple times to maximise the empathy score. A
representative example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{User:} ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi lately. I feel really
anxious and overwhelmed.''

\textit{Failed response (pre-fix, high empathy score):}
``It makes sense that you feel this way. It makes sense to feel
overwhelmed. It makes sense that this is hard. It makes sense that
you're struggling. What would help you right now?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.90 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 0.20
\end{quote}

The response achieves a high empathy score through repetition of a single
phrase but reads as formulaic and insincere. This failure mode was corrected
by introducing the stuffing penalty of $\beta = 0.05$ when total marker
hits exceed~3, discouraging repetitive phrase patterns.

\subsection{Post-Fix Optimised Response (Same Prompt)}
\label{app:postfix}

After scoring redesign, the same prompt from Appendix~\ref{app:stuffing-example}
produces:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Optimised response (post-fix):}
``Feeling this alone can be really painful. {\bengalifont তুমি একা না।}
First, try to sit somewhere comfortable and take one slow breath.
Try not to stay completely isolated with these thoughts.
Is there someone nearby you feel comfortable talking to?''

\textit{Empathy score:} 0.50 \quad \textit{Structure score:} 1.0 \quad
\textit{Safety score:} 0.60 (mid-risk, no escalation content; see
Appendix~\ref{app:worked-example} for the corresponding fitness
calculation)
\end{quote}

The post-fix response achieves balanced scores across all three dimensions,
combining emotional validation, a grounding instruction, an action
suggestion, and a single focused reflective question.

\subsection{Worked Example: From Prompt to Fitness}
\label{app:worked-example}

This subsection walks the same prompt and response from
Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} through every
stage of the pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, using the reported
genome $g^{\star}$ from Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-ga}
($w_e^{\star}=0.131$, $w_s^{\star}=0.101$, $w_c^{\star}=0.768$,
$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}^{\star}=0.588$, $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}^{\star}=0.700$).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Prompt + risk label.} $x_i = $ ``Ami onek lonely feel kortesi
        lately. I feel really anxious and overwhelmed.'' For illustration we
        take $r_i = \mathrm{mid}$.
  \item \textbf{Genome.} The generator is configured with $g^{\star}$ as
        above (Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}).
  \item \textbf{Generator.} Because $w_c^{\star}=0.768$ falls in the top
        structure regime ($w_c \ge 0.55$; Section~\ref{sec:progressive-structure}),
        the generator deterministically includes grounding, action, and
        question components ($c_g=c_a=c_q=1$); $w_e^{\star}$ governs how
        many empathy lines are included.
  \item \textbf{Response.} $y_i = G(x_i,r_i;g^{\star}) = $ the response shown
        in Appendix~\ref{app:postfix} above.
  \item \textbf{Automatic scoring.} Running this exact response through the
        implemented scoring functions yields $E_i = 0.50$, $S_i = 0.60$,
        $C_i = 1.00$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:empathy}--\eqref{eq:structure}). The
        response contains no escalation-language marker (Section~\ref{sec:automatic-scoring}),
        which is expected and appropriate for a mid-risk prompt whose
        computed distress score is low ($h_i \approx 0$, well below
        $\tau_h=0.65$); under the categorical safety lookup
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}), a mid-risk response without escalation
        content scores $S_i=0.60$ by construction, and $S_i=1.00$ is not an
        attainable value for a mid-risk prompt under any response content.
  \item \textbf{Fitness.} The response is below the 140-word penalty
        threshold, so $L_i=0$. Since $S_i=0.60 < 0.94$, the flat safety
        soft-threshold penalty of $-0.15$ applies (the risk label is mid,
        not high, so the $-0.50$ high-risk penalty does not apply); since
        $h_i \le \tau_h$, the distress-gating penalty
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}, final line) also does not
        apply. $C_i=1.00 \ge 0.70$, so the structure soft penalty does not
        apply; $E_i=0.50 < 0.62$, so the empathy soft penalty does apply.
        The per-example fitness (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) is
        \begin{align*}
          f_i(g^{\star}) &= \underbrace{0.40(0.50) + 0.40(0.60) + 0.15(1.00)}_{\text{base } A E_i+B S_i+C C_i}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.15}_{\text{safety soft-threshold penalty}}
            \;-\; \underbrace{0.18\,\tfrac{0.62-0.50}{0.62}}_{\text{empathy soft penalty}} \\
            &\quad + \underbrace{0.05(0.50) + 0.05(1.00) + 0.05\min(1.00,0.8)}_{\text{reinforcement terms}} \\
            &\approx 0.590 - 0.150 - 0.035 + 0.025 + 0.050 + 0.040 = 0.520.
        \end{align*}
        This illustrates the per-example fitness computation
        (Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}) that underlies both the
        training-time fitness $F_{\mathrm{train},t}(g) = \frac{1}{600}
        \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_t} f_i(g)$, averaged over the
        $|\mathcal{I}_t|=600$ prompts sampled from the optimisation set at
        generation $t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:global}), and the validation fitness
        $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g) = \frac{1}{575}\sum_{i \in V} f_i(g)$, averaged
        over the separate, fixed 575-prompt held-out validation set. The
        reported validation fitness $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star}) \approx
        0.703$ (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-optimized}) is computed on this
        held-out set, not on any single generation's 600-prompt training
        sample; the two quantities use different, non-overlapping prompt
        populations and are not directly interchangeable, though both use
        the same per-example fitness formula. The single-example value
        above is lower than the reported $F_{\mathrm{val}}(g^{\star})$
        because this example's response does not
        contain any escalation content and so incurs the safety
        soft-threshold penalty, whereas across the validation
        set most low- and high-risk prompts (which make up the majority of
        the dataset; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}) attain $S_i=1.00$ under
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety} and so do not incur this penalty.
  \item \textbf{GA feedback.} Across generations, genomes whose responses
        score well on this fitness are more likely to be selected, crossed
        over, and mutated into the next population
        (Section~\ref{sec:ga-config}), which is how the search arrived at
        $g^{\star}$ from the seeded initial population
        (Appendix~\ref{app:genomes}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Optimised Genome Evolution Examples}
\label{app:genomes}

\subsection{Renormalisation, $w_s$, Reserved Escalation Genes, and the Omitted Length-Penalty Gene}
\label{app:renorm}

The three continuous weights
$w_e, w_s, w_c$ are stored in raw (post-mutation, pre-normalisation) form in
the genome, and are renormalised to sum to 1 at the point the generator
reads them, after all crossover and mutation for that individual has been
applied. Consequently, two genomes with identical raw $w_e$ and $w_c$ but
different raw $w_s$ will be assembled into \emph{different} responses,
because the shared normalising denominator $w_e+w_s+w_c$ differs between
them. $w_s$'s evolved value is therefore best interpreted as a
normalisation-only participant rather than as an independently meaningful
``safety weight,'' and we avoid describing $w_s^{\star}=0.101$ as reflecting
an evolved safety preference (Section~\ref{sec:balance-discussion}); its
only demonstrated effect on any reported result is through the denominator
it contributes to.

$\theta_{\mathrm{mid}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{high}}$ are two further genes,
part of the genome and subject to crossover and mutation like every other
gene, but in the present implementation not read by the generator or the
fitness function, and therefore with no effect on any reported result. We
retain them in the genome because they are the intended interface for
graded, distress-score-driven escalation, listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The genome as implemented does not include a separate evolvable length-penalty
gene: the length penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness} uses the fixed
coefficient $D=0.05$ throughout, rather than a per-genome value. An earlier
design considered introducing such a gene (denoted $\lambda$), but it was not
carried into the implementation, so we omit it from Eq.~\eqref{eq:genome}
rather than list a gene the genetic algorithm never actually evolves.

\subsection{Identifying the Generation-1 Best Individual}
\label{app:gen1}

Per-generation genome logs from the main GA run show the generation-1 best
individual has $w_c \approx 0.678$, $w_e \approx 0.132$, $w_s \approx
0.190$ -- clearly \emph{not} $g_\mathrm{bal}$ (whose structure weight is
$w_c \approx 0.34$), and instead already structurally similar to the
eventual reported genome ($w_c^{\star} = 0.768$). This is consistent with
the generation-1 population containing a mutated or crossed-over
descendant of the structure-heavy seed $g_\mathrm{str}$ (or, less likely,
a favourably-drawn random individual) rather than $g_\mathrm{bal}$.

\section{Distress Scoring and Penalty Terms}
\label{app:distress}

\subsection{The Direct Distress-Gating Penalty Term}
\label{app:distress-penalty}

Equation~\eqref{eq:per-example-fitness}
includes a further term, $-0.10\,\mathbb{I}\{h_i > \tau_h \wedge r_i=\mathrm{mid}
\wedge \mathrm{esc}_i=0\}$, that penalises a mid-risk response lacking
escalation content when $h_i$ exceeds the \emph{fixed} threshold $\tau_h$
(not the $\gamma$-relaxed effective threshold). Because the effective
threshold $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) \le \tau_h$ for any $\gamma \ge 0$, whenever
$h_i > \tau_h$ the escalation condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:risk-escalation} is
already satisfied regardless of $\gamma$; a correctly functioning,
untruncated generator should therefore always set $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ in
this regime, and $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ here can only arise from a downstream
generation failure (e.g.\ the response-part truncation described in
Section~\ref{sec:part-budget} and analysed for B1 in
Section~\ref{sec:interp-baseline}) rather than from the evolved value of
$\gamma$ itself. This penalty term therefore does not supply direct
selection pressure on $\gamma$. The region in which $\gamma$'s value
actually changes escalation behaviour is $\tau_h/(1+\gamma) < h_i \le
\tau_h$, where $h_i \le \tau_h$ and the direct penalty above does not
apply; any selection pressure on $\gamma$ in that region operates only
indirectly, through the resulting change in the categorical safety score
between $\mathrm{esc}_i=0$ (0.60) and $\mathrm{esc}_i=1$ (0.85) for
mid-risk prompts whose distress score falls in that narrow band
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety}). We have not separately quantified how many
mid-risk prompts in the dataset fall in this band at $\gamma^{\star}
\approx 0.059$, nor run a $\gamma=0$ ablation holding all other genes
fixed, so we do not assert a magnitude of benefit
we have not directly measured.

\subsection{The Marker-Stuffing Penalty on Distress Scores}
\label{app:stuffing}

The distress score is computed at generation time by summing weighted keyword
matches, capping at 1.0, and applying a small penalty for marker stuffing
(>3 distinct signals). This stuffing penalty is a clinically
questionable design choice on its own terms: the practice of discounting a
score for having ``too many'' matched markers is appropriate for the
empathy-marker-stuffing failure mode described in
Section~\ref{sec:scoring-bottleneck}, where markers reflect the
\emph{generator's} output and repetition is a sign of gaming, but the same
logic does not straightforwardly transfer to a \emph{user's} distress
signal: a prompt containing several distinct distress indicators (e.g.,
hopelessness, isolation, and self-harm language together) plausibly warrants
more concern, not less. We verified computationally that this penalty has no
effect on any escalation decision reported in this paper, in two independent
ways. First, an exhaustive combinatorial check over the full keyword set
confirms that the smallest possible sum
of any four matched keywords is $2.45$, already far above both the $1.0$ cap
and the distress threshold $\tau_h=0.65$; every combination of four or more
matched keywords therefore saturates to the $1.0$ cap and is reduced by the
penalty to at most $0.95$ -- still far above $\tau_h$ under any evolved
$\gamma$ in its bounded range
$[0, 0.2]$. Second, and more directly, we checked the penalty's trigger
condition (more than three distinct matched keywords) against every
fitness prompt used in this work --- the concatenated
\texttt{common\_themes}\,/\texttt{thought\_progression} text that
\texttt{compute\_distress\_score()} receives at generation and evaluation
time --- and found that it is never satisfied: the maximum number of
distinct distress-keyword matches on any such prompt is two, so the
penalty does not fire even once on the data that actually enters the
fitness pipeline. (Clinician \texttt{risk\_assessment} notes can match
four or more keywords in a handful of rows if scanned as free text, but
those notes are used only for risk-label construction, not as the
distress-score input.) The penalty is therefore inert in practice for this
dataset and keyword set on both theoretical and empirical grounds, though
this does not resolve the underlying clinical concern; a design that does
not risk discounting more severe presentations would be preferable in
future revisions.

\section{Equivalence Tests and Sensitivity Statistics}
\label{app:equivalence}

\subsection{Dataset-Size Equivalence Testing}
\label{app:dataset-tost}

Dataset-size equivalence to the full-dataset ($N=2297$) condition was
tested using two one-sided tests (TOST) at a pre-specified margin of
$\varepsilon=0.01$ fitness points. None of the four comparisons
($N=100,300,600,1200$ vs.\ $N=2297$) established equivalence at this
margin (all TOST $p \ge 0.986$), despite raw mean differences all below
0.005 and 95\% confidence intervals on the difference no wider than
$[-0.007, 0.013]$ across any comparison. TOST requires the entire
confidence interval to fall within $\pm\varepsilon$, and with only five
seeds per condition these intervals are wide relative to
$\varepsilon=0.01$ even when point estimates are close, which we consider
the most plausible explanation for the non-equivalence result (rather than
a genuine practically important effect of dataset size).

\subsection{Population-Size Equivalence Testing}
\label{app:population-tost}

Population-size equivalence to the $P=100$ condition was tested using the
same TOST procedure and margin. None of the five comparisons
($P=30,50,200,300,400$ vs.\ $P=100$) established equivalence (all TOST
$p \ge 0.994$), for the same power-and-margin reasons as the dataset-size
result. Raw mean differences here are smaller than in the dataset-size
experiment (all below 0.0026 fitness points, versus a 0.0068-point band
across dataset sizes), and every 95\% confidence interval on the difference
is no wider than $[-0.0054, 0.0065]$. Mean runtime ranges from 64 seconds at
$P=30$ to a non-monotonic peak of 882 seconds at $P=200$.

\section{Human Evaluation Statistical Details}
\label{app:humaneval}

\subsection{Correcting for Pseudoreplication}

The 150 annotator-item ratings per condition (5 annotators $\times$
30 items) are not 150 independent observations: ratings from the same
annotator are correlated across items, and ratings of the same item
are correlated across annotators. Treating all 150 as independent, as
in a naive paired $t$-test on the raw annotator-item pairs, understates
the true uncertainty and can overstate significance. We therefore
report inference at the level of the 30 items, the genuine unit of
independent replication in this design, rather than at the level of
individual ratings.

For each dimension, we first average the 5 raters' scores for each
item and condition, then compute the paired difference (B4 minus B1)
across the resulting $n=30$ independent item-level pairs. This yields
a mean item-level improvement of $+1.68$ (Empathy), $+1.77$ (Safety),
and $+1.95$ (Helpfulness), matching the raw annotator-item means in
Table~\ref{tab:human_eval}, but now with correctly calibrated
uncertainty: 95\% confidence intervals of $[1.48, 1.88]$ (Empathy),
$[1.64, 1.89]$ (Safety), and $[1.83, 2.06]$ (Helpfulness), obtained
both from the normal approximation and from a 10{,}000-resample
percentile bootstrap over items, which agree closely with each other.
Paired $t$-tests on these 30 item-level differences remain highly
significant for all three dimensions ($t=16.51$, $p=2.8\times10^{-16}$
for Empathy; $t=28.07$, $p=1.4\times10^{-22}$ for Safety; $t=32.96$,
$p=1.5\times10^{-24}$ for Helpfulness), as do Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests run on the same item-level differences as a nonparametric
check ($p<10^{-5}$ in all three cases). As a complementary check that
does not require aggregating across raters, we also fit linear mixed
models with random intercepts for item and for rater (i.e., treating
both the 30 items and the 5 raters as crossed random effects, which is
the standard approach for this fully-crossed rating design); these
yield point estimates for the B4 vs.\ B1 effect that are numerically
identical to the item-level analysis ($+1.68$, $+1.77$, $+1.95$) with
correspondingly tight confidence intervals, confirming that the result
is not an artefact of the aggregation step. We report the item-level
analysis as primary in Table~\ref{tab:human_eval} because it depends
on fewer distributional assumptions and is directly reproducible by
hand from the 30 item-level means. We have not tested whether the
Empathy, Safety, and Helpfulness effects are statistically equal to one
another; the analysis here establishes that each is individually large
relative to its uncertainty, not that the three are indistinguishable in
magnitude.
